Abstract.

The objective of this work is to develop a computer system to manage the information management of a company dedicated to the delivery of services in the cultural field. The problem identified is the handling of service requests manually, with delays in responses to them, as well as the lack of information to prepare statistical data that allows decision making. The main objective is to automate the tasks of the departments involved using an information management system as a work tool that would allow fluid and organized control in the development of operational and administrative processes. The results obtained through interviews, surveys, requirements gathering and process documentation demonstrate the need to develop and implement a tailor-made software tool to manage the workflow of each area.

Keywords:

System; management; Information; Process automation.

Introduction

In the context of a company whose main function is to offer certified quality services, it is important to have a computer system that makes it possible to integrate and automate its administrative and operational processes efficiently and with measurable results (Lemos, 2015).

The specific case presented in this document concerns Company Z, which obtained its ISO 9001:2015 certification in October 2018 and whose goal is to implement a quality management system in its processes in order to obtain recertification under this standard. Quality Management Systems cover all the activities carried out within a company, and each department must have its own systems to control its work, with the quality function responsible for the reliability of these systems and for coordination among departments (Cortés, 2017).

To achieve this goal, it was necessary to document and standardize the administrative and operational processes to be certified, as well as to share all the process documentation across the organization. The company under study did not have a digital tool that would allow it to integrate all this information, which made it difficult to manage information optimally. Another problem identified was that most of the activities within each process were carried out manually through paper-based requests, with the help of tools such as Google Drive, or, in the case of some departments, custom-built systems for certain activities, without integration or communication among them, which made the process of transferring information difficult when different areas interacted.

It is worth mentioning that one of ISO 9001:2015's recommendations is that work requests made among the company's different departments be resolved or answered within no more than 5 business days. This certification requirement created the need for a tool to speed up the handling of interdepartmental service requests, since, being a company with more than 150 members distributed across physically distant locations, the workload for completing these service requests was heavy.

Specific objectives

  • Develop a computer system aligned with the Quality Management System recommended by ISO 9001:2015 to document the processes certified under this standard by Company Z.

  • Determine the MVP (Minimum Viable Product) that meets the functionalities identified as required by the aforementioned areas to achieve the goals set for each certified process at Company Z.

  • Provide a software tool, which we will call SGI (Internal Management System), that enables documented and measurable interaction among users of the aforementioned areas, in order to meet the ISO 9001:2015 requirement of responding to service requests within no more than 5 days.

  • Provide a technological tool that makes it possible to record basic process information and data in order to evaluate performance and strengthen its improvement.

Theoretical framework

To manage is to make things happen; managing is the process of interventions that allow things to happen in an ethically sound, process-based way. Managing means achieving proposed objectives through the coordinated action of people, through the proper application of available resources. Management is measured through the use of indicators showing the percentage of progress toward the objectives and/or goals set (Blejmar, 2022).

According to Piña (2006), information management is the use of information as an asset that improves companies' competitiveness and responsiveness; he notes that it is the result of efficiently identifying, integrating, and analyzing information and directing it toward decision-making and customer-service processes.

An organization's internal management is the business activity that plans, organizes, directs, and enables control within an organization, which, together with its members (directors, managers, employees, among others), improves its productivity and competitiveness through the use of techniques and methodologies (Trujillo, 2012). Companies rely on information systems to be more efficient by increasing their productivity, or to be more effective (Beynon-Davies, 2018). An organization that adopts a proper management system generates trust both inside and outside itself, sending the message that it will be able to achieve its objectives, thereby laying the groundwork for continuous improvement, which allows for an optimal level of satisfaction among the parties interested in the process (Merino, 2020). Information systems have an impact across all of an organization's processes (figure 1.1).

ITIL Framework

This section presents relevant information regarding the most important frameworks in use today, with the aim of documenting the good practices used to achieve the SGI's development goals. ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library) is a set of recommended good practices; it is widely accepted and was designed to help organizations get the most benefit from IT by aligning IT services with organizations' business strategy (Greenwood, 2020). Today, ITIL is owned by Axelos, a joint venture between the UK Cabinet Office and Capita (Agutter, 2020).

The Guiding Principles are a series of recommendations that, as their name indicates, guide the organization under all circumstances. Goals, staff, strategies, or organizational structure may change within a company, but the guiding principles remain constant. The guiding principles are universal and can be applied to other types of methodologies such as Agile, Lean, COBIT, among others (Agutter, 2020).

Agutter (2020) also notes that the guiding principles should foster and support continuous improvement; these apply throughout the organization, at all levels, and, in addition, all staff should be familiar with them, even though they are not always applied in every situation — it is important that they be reviewed frequently on each occasion to assess whether their use is required and appropriate. Agutter (2020) states that ITIL's guiding principles are (figure 1.2):

Principle 1. Focus on value

Everything the organization does must link, directly or indirectly, to value for itself, its customers, and other interested parties.

Principle 2. Start where you are

Do not start from scratch without first considering what is already available to leverage. It is important to objectively observe and recognize what can be used and what should be improved, or replicated as the case may be, always taking into account the risks involved in each decision.

Principle 3. Progress iteratively with feedback

Resist the temptation to do everything at once. Even large initiatives must be carried out iteratively. By organizing work into smaller, more manageable sections that can be executed and completed in a timely manner, the focus of each effort will be sharper and easier to maintain.

Principle 4. Collaborate and promote visibility

When initiatives involve the right people in the right roles, efforts benefit from greater acceptance, more relevance (because better information is available for decision-making), and a higher likelihood of long-term success.

Principle 5. Think and work holistically

No service, practice, process, department, or provider stands alone. The products the organization delivers to itself, its customers, and other interested parties will be affected unless it works in an integrated way to manage its activities as a whole, rather than as separate parts. All of the organization's activities should be focused on delivering value.

Principle 6. Keep it simple and practical

Always use the minimum number of steps needed to achieve an objective. Outcome-based thinking should be used to produce practical solutions that deliver valuable results.

Principle 7. Optimize and automate

Companies must get the most value from the work carried out by their human and technical resources alike. The four-dimensions method provides a comprehensive view of the various constraints, the different forms of resources, and other characteristics that must be taken into account when envisioning, managing, or operating a company. The use of technology helps companies carry out frequent, repetitive tasks, making it possible to manage human resources more efficiently. It should be noted that automation always helps, but organizations should not rely 100% on technology without human intervention.

Research methodology used in the SGI

The first stage is approached through a qualitative methodology, with a diagnostic-type documentary study and the conducting of exploratory interviews. In this first approach, two meetings were held at Company Z's facilities with staff directly involved in the development of this project, as well as with staff from the "Web Platforms" area. In these meetings, the internal processes carried out and how they are performed were identified; documents such as process maps, Excel spreadsheets, paper forms, electronic forms, and information shared in Google Drive files were also reviewed. A digital copy of these files was also obtained for further study and analysis.

At the end of each of these meetings, an exploratory interview was conducted with each of the five participants from each meeting. These interviews were conducted with the heads of each area, since they hold a comprehensive view of both their internal processes and mappings, and also have a view of interaction with other areas. They were asked whether they would like to have a system with the proposed SGI features. In total, ten exploratory interviews were conducted; these were recorded and transcribed for later analysis.

Based on the results of the exploratory interviews, the script for conducting semi-structured interviews was improved, since a broader view was now available of the needs identified among users and of the system's specific requirements. In these semi-structured interviews, more specific questions were formulated for each area involved, based on the information obtained in the exploratory interviews and on the analysis of the information and files previously obtained. A total of ten semi-structured interviews were conducted with the same people who took part in the exploratory interviews. In these interviews, the aim was for the person interviewed to describe their internal work process, as well as their daily duties, in order to put them down on paper, along with their interaction with staff from other areas, among other things.

In the second stage, a quantitative methodology (evaluation) was used, through the administration of surveys aimed at the SGI's end users, in order to evaluate the system developed and thereby obtain feedback for making improvements and/or new versions. The system itself includes a section where a survey on the SGI's functionality and the benefits users perceive and have obtained from using it appears at random.

Methodologies used to develop the SGI

No major project can succeed without a plan. Sometimes the plan is not followed closely, but every major project must have a plan. The plan tells project members what they should do, when and how much time they should devote to it, and, most importantly, what the project's objectives are. The plan gives the project direction (Stephens, 2015).

Software development is no easy task, as evidenced by the various methodologies proposed, as explained in earlier chapters. The framework of a software development methodology consists of:

  • A software development philosophy with a particular software development approach.

  • The use of multiple tools, methods, and models to help with the software development process.

For the SGI's development, the use of some of the development methodologies noted in earlier chapters was established, and in some cases, a combination of them. Following this premise, the proposed design takes ITIL's good practices as a basis in order to seek and ensure the delivery of value to the client by offering a range of possibilities for improving the performance of their tasks and processes, with increased productivity and reduced time to carry them out. Recall that ITIL is not a software development methodology; ITIL is a framework of good practices for the effective, service-based management of IT, which defines a series of organizational structures, departmental roles, procedures, and some measurement mechanisms.

As mentioned earlier, ITIL's technical management practices include software development and management. Axelos (2022) notes that the good habit of software application development is fundamental to creating value for customers of technology-based services, and is carried out through two widely known methodologies: waterfall and agile. The methodology used in this work is a combination of both; the waterfall model was used to define and establish the project's main stages, such as: requirements, analysis, design, coding, testing, and maintenance, but handled dynamically, that is, removing this methodology's original sequential feature and adapting these stages into an agile methodology that made it possible to deliver the MVP, which in turn is used in a testing environment to receive feedback, make corrections, schedule improvements, and deliver the module again for installation in production (ITIL's Continual Improvement process).

During execution of the software development project, information was obtained from the exploratory and semi-structured interviews conducted. This information was used to define the requirements needed for development, and modules were delivered to receive feedback from Company Z. These steps are also part of the process of applying ITIL's good practices.

Part of the methodology design used included adopting the Scrum methodology in order to be more agile in developing the SGI's modules. Daily stand-up meetings, as recommended under this methodology, were held (sometimes in person, and mostly virtually) to maintain and promote staying in tune with ITIL's principles. All of this was aimed at achieving results on time and with the established functionalities.

The "user stories" technique is used, which consists of simple, short descriptions of a feature described from the point of view of the person requesting a requirement, generally answering a series of questions, such as: How, I want, So that (Patton, 2014). For the SGI, five user stories were obtained, which were used to identify the basis for detailing specific activities for each module of the SGI.

Incremental delivery

Web-based systems require an approach designed around the development and delivery of repetitive, iterative cycles (a continuous process of experimentation) that allows for testing and evaluation of the product, creating reusable system components. As shown in figure 1.3, incremental delivery is a view of software development in which some of the increments developed are released to the client and implemented for use in a real operational environment (Sommerville, 2011). The approach for delivering the SGI was entirely one of incremental delivery, given that Company Z's requirements are dynamic and new requirements constantly arose. Likewise, once users had the MVP, new ideas emerged and new functionalities were required. This approach made it possible to deliver a product that truly generated value, as a system closely aligned with the company's operational and administrative reality.

Developing the SGI required the use of web technologies such as HTML, CSS, and JavaScript on the client side; on the server side, PHP was required on a web server, and MySQL was used as the database management application.

Phase 1. Development of the Quality Management System

The first implementation phase consisted of planning and developing the Quality Management System module to document the processes certified under the ISO 9001:2015 standard. In this stage, the code was developed in plain PHP with a connection to the database created.

The Quality Management System has important elements in its structure, among which stand out: Cover sheet, Mapping, Internal documents, External documents, Nonconforming service, Risks and opportunities, and Interested parties. In the work carried out, two specific modules were programmed for managing the Quality Management System: one called "Admin Management," used to manage all the documented information for the processes mentioned, where new menu options, new processes, new cover sheets, new mappings, among others, are added; and the second module is a query-type module for all system users, where the development of each process and structure mentioned can be viewed in detail.

Phase 2. MVP requirements analysis

Phase 2 consisted of determining the SGI's MVP functionalities needed to achieve the goals set for the Finance and Auditing, Human Development and Quality, General Services, Logistics and Technology, Communication and Press, Design and Multimedia, and Legal Department areas of Company Z.

Based on the diagnostic-type interviews, the exploratory and semi-structured interviews conducted, the application of surveys, and the analysis of the documents and files received during these interviews, the basic structure making up the SGI was defined.

The menu structure includes the options Home, Profile, My Panel, Services, Administrator, Complaints and Suggestions, Support and Help, and Log Out. It was defined that all users would see this menu, and that, depending on each user's profile, they would see specific options under Services and My Panel. Under Services, users can select the name of the department and/or area to which service requests will be made.

It is worth noting that the main function of the system developed was for the SGI to be robust enough to offer a range of options in each area to be automated, and flexible enough to adapt to constant changes during these early development phases. Recall that the "Incremental Development" method is applied in sprint cycles, so new changes and functionalities for the features delivered could arise in each weekly delivery.

Among the features defined, it was established that it was important for each user to have a general list of the requests made to their department so they could follow up on them quickly. For this, an option was programmed for each area's request list, with special search filters by request type or by date. It should be noted that this request list shows the request date, the date the status was marked as completed, and the number of days elapsed between the first date and the second, which allows us to track statistics on each request's response time; the system's functionality allows the user to click the "View or edit" button to see the request in detail.

A fundamental part of this phase 2's MVP was planning and programming notifications for requests and their responses. As mentioned in this development's objectives section, it is important for areas to respond to their requests within no more than 5 business days. To make communication through the SGI more fluid, notifications were programmed into the system, both internal and external. Internally, because accessing the SGI shows a bell icon with the number of pending unread notifications, and externally, because notifications arrive via email so that users, upon checking their email, see that they have received a new service request for their area. A history of the changes each request has undergone is also saved, in order to keep a historical record of each user's involvement in each request made.

Testing

Test development aims to show that a system does what it is defined to do; it also helps find anomalies or errors in the program before using it. During this process, tests can be implemented at three levels, which are: (Sommerville, 2011).

1. Unit tests, where detailed program functions or object classes that work individually are diagnosed.

2. Component tests, where small functionalities are gathered or integrated to create composite components or functions, focusing on testing the interfaces of these functions.

3. System tests, where some or all of a system's functionalities are handled comprehensively and the entire system developed is tested.

Through the constant use, administration, updating, and querying of the Quality Management System via the SGI, it has been shown that implementing a computer system for information management can help a company obtain or maintain ISO 9001:2015 certification. In November 2021, Company Z obtained recertification under ISO 9001:2015 from the certifying body; it should be noted that every 6 or 12 months a review is carried out of the Quality Management System's continuous improvement processes, and the SGI's Quality Management System has been a fundamental tool for documenting new improvements to certified processes. In the most recent audit, Company Z received congratulations from the auditors regarding the SGI.

SGI MVP results

Test 1: Access tests

Users access the system's main login screen, enter their username and password, and after several internal validations are performed, the SGI grants access. An attempt was deliberately made to access the system with an incorrect password, and the SGI issued an error message.

Test 2: User profile tests

Users access the SGI and intentionally attempt to access menu options restricted for their user level and/or profile; in response, the SGI does not allow access to these menu options. On the other hand, they attempt to access the system options they are authorized to use, and can view those options correctly.

Test 3: Systems user test

A user from the Finance department accesses their services panel and generates a new request to the Systems department for support in configuring the institutional email on their computer. The head of the Systems department receives the request from the Finance-area user and assigns it to the email technician. The email technician receives the support request from the Finance-area user, proceeds to follow up on the request, and, once resolved, responds to the service request or ticket for the Finance user. Once the request is completed, the new status is recorded as "completed," and data such as how many days it took to handle the request, who handled it, comments, etc., are recorded.

SGI usability perception

The questionnaire used to obtain the data needed to measure the SGI's perceived usability is the System Usability Scale (SUS). This System Usability Scale was developed by John Brooke to measure the usability of computer systems he was working on. Upon realizing that the SUS was useful, he decided to release it so that colleagues and other companies could use it. This way, developed products could be compared and industry standards could be established; the result of John Brooke's contribution was ISO 9241, part 11 (Montiel, 2017).

The SUS measures the parameters shown below (Brooke, 1996):

  • Effectiveness: Whether users can carry out their tasks and meet their objectives.

  • Efficiency: How many resources were invested to achieve the objectives.

  • Satisfaction: The level of well-being experienced in reaching those objectives.

Brooke (1996) notes that the scale is interpreted as follows: at 25 points, usability is the worst imaginable; in the range from 25 to 38, usability is poor; in the range from 39 to 52, usability is acceptable; from 53 to 73, usability is good; from 74 to 84, it is very good; at 85, usability is excellent; and from 85 to 100, it is the best possible (figure 1.4).

Montiel (2017) states that the SUS is reliable because participants respond accurately to rate the different features of a software development, and is valid because it measures what it says it measures. When applying the SUS, participants are asked to rate, from 1 to 5 (1 being strongly disagree and 5 strongly agree), how they view the functions of the system they used. Once the user answers the questionnaire, an average can be generated, yielding a final score that shows the usability level of the system evaluated.

To obtain the SUS score, several calculations are performed. One point is subtracted from the score obtained on questions 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9, resulting in a maximum score of 0 to 4 for each item. For questions 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10, the selected score is subtracted from 5. Finally, the calculated totals are added together and multiplied by 2.5; the resulting number is the usability level (Brooke, 1996). The SGI was programmed so that, at random, a total of 30 users would answer the satisfaction survey once they accessed the system. The data obtained through this survey were processed in Excel, yielding the usability level. The SGI's usability percentage was 82.5, within the "very good" range as described in the preceding paragraphs. The results are shown in figure 1.5.

With the results of the tests carried out on the SGI's MVP shown in this section, as well as the results of the surveys processed through the SUS, the progress in workflow, effectiveness, improved response time, efficiency in process execution, and the user satisfaction rate obtained through the SGI's use are all made evident. These results show that implementing a computer system for information management contributes to increased productivity at a company and improves its users' work performance.

Conclusions

A web-based internal management system can motivate a company's users to feel more productive and to perceive a fluid environment of information and feedback. The SGI proposed in this work offers tools that allow them to be more productive in pursuit of the goals set, with satisfactory performance indicators.

Regarding the general objective set out in this study: "To develop and implement a web system using the PHP development framework to automate and manage the information generated through the processes of Finance and Auditing, Human Development and Quality, General Services, Logistics and Technology, Communication and Press, Design and Multimedia, and the Legal Department of Company Z," the request tests carried out among users of the departments noted yielded a satisfaction level of 98%. These tests were carried out as pilot tests, selecting one member from each department; this satisfaction level was reinforced by the results of the surveys applied to obtain the user satisfaction rate.

Regarding the specific objectives, the first was "to provide a web system aligned with the Quality Management System recommended by ISO 9001:2015 to document the processes certified by Company Z"; this objective was met, given that the SGI developed has a specific module for documenting the processes noted; in the most recent audit of Company Z by the certifying body, there was special recognition for the web system used to document its processes, and it is planned to replicate this in Company Z's sister companies. Likewise, satisfaction surveys were randomly administered to users of this module, and the satisfaction level was 100%. Regarding this objective, it can be concluded that an internal management system can serve as technological support for companies interested in obtaining and/or maintaining certification under the ISO 9001:2015 standard, allowing them to document their processes and information for the purpose of becoming certified under that standard.

The second objective was "to determine the MVP that meets the functionalities identified as required by the aforementioned areas to achieve the goals set for each certified process at Company Z." Likewise, it was shown that Company Z achieved interoperability among its areas through the use of digital service requests among the Finance and Auditing, Human Development and Quality, General Services, Logistics and Technology, Communication and Press, Design and Multimedia, and Legal Department areas of Company Z. This could be measured with performance indicators that are documented within its Quality Management System. Regarding the level of usability, an acceptable level is observed in terms of initial reception to using the SGI. It is considered that there are areas for improvement, given that the usability percentage achieved is 82.5; although this is classified as very good according to the SUS scale, it can be improved to reach the following levels. The perception that this is a system that benefits the operation of their activities is positive, even though, according to the survey responses, there was some apprehension at the start of the learning and automation process for their activities.

Regarding the third specific objective of this work, which refers to "providing a software tool that enables documented and measurable interaction among users of the aforementioned areas to respond to requests within no more than 5 business days," it was shown, first with the pilot test and then with the SGI's daily use in "production," or already in its real environment, that when a user receives a service request, they first quickly notice they have received it, since a notification appears within the SGI in the form of a small bell, along with another notification by email. Surveys showed that users find it easier to follow up on a service request through the SGI; the application of these surveys yielded a satisfaction level of 90%.

Regarding the fourth and final specific objective, "to provide a technological tool that makes it possible to record basic process information and data in order to evaluate performance and strengthen its improvement," the pilot tests carried out showed that important data for each service request are stored, including the date the request was received, the date it was resolved, the user it was assigned to, its status, response time, and number of days elapsed; with this data, it is shown that the information needed to evaluate performance by user, by area, among others, is available.

In conclusion, the outcome of developing and implementing a web-based internal management system can be considered satisfactory; its quality is acceptable and it meets the defined objectives. A new system always puts users on alert, and at first they resist change; that challenge proved successful by the end of the pilot tests and the implementation in the real production environment.
