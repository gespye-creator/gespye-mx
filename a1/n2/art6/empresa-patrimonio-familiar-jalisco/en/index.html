Analysis of the family business and family patrimony in Jalisco

Análisis de la empresa y el patrimonio familiar en Jalisco

Abstract.

This article aims to present family patrimony in Jalisco, Mexico, as an alternative for family business asset separation and protection. Methodologically, the analytical-synthetic, comparative and deductive methods were used under the documentary research technique. The importance of the study lies in separating family assets from company assets which is the most frequent problem in this type of organization. This was a qualitative work with a descriptive approach. Research contribution was to introduce an effective method family business organization in which family business property is identified from personal family property. Study limitations include the local nature of the rules governing the family patrimony in Mexico.

Keywords:

family business; family patrimony; family economy.

Introduction

The family business in Mexico holds great importance for society, as it accounts for not only 85% of the country's Gross Domestic Product, but also because 9 out of every 10 business units in Mexico are family businesses (Leyva, 2022). In the case of Jalisco, according to a census carried out among 935 companies in 2022, 41.5% of companies are classified as family businesses; of these, the majority (56%) self-identify as family businesses (Instituto de Información Estadística y Geografía de Jalisco [IIEG], 2022).

It should therefore be taken into account that, in the case of a family business, the boundaries between family patrimony and business patrimony are tenuous, since, when a business is formed on the basis of family ties, the support of the family nucleus is sought, in addition to obtaining profit given that it is a business; for this reason, the constitution of a family patrimony can help safeguard the interests of its members while also helping to draw a distinction between the two patrimonies. Thus, this article aims to present family patrimony in Jalisco, Mexico, as an alternative for the separation and protection of family business assets.

In this context, it should be noted that the family business has been studied from the standpoint of business organization, positions and compensation, as well as succession (Rizo, 2013; Soto, 2013; Durán, 2014; Armijo, 2016), while family patrimony has been addressed from a legal and family-law perspective (López, 2005; Flores, 2006; Torres, 2009; Baqueiro y Buenrostro, 2010; Galindo, 2010; Lepin, 2014; Treviño, 2017).

Along these lines, Aguilar and Briozzo (2019) highlight the need to find mechanisms that go beyond the classic view of financial management between capital structure and the socioemotional wealth of family businesses; thus, the relevance of this research lies in presenting a mixed approach between the administrative and legal standpoints on business patrimony, in order to seek the strengthening and continuity of the business.

In keeping with this reasoning, the paper is organized starting with the introductory section, followed by the methodological section, which briefly includes the methods and techniques used, as well as the research questions; the points of analysis are then developed with the aim of answering the research questions, ending with conclusions that highlight the paper's contributions.

Methodology

The object of study was family business patrimony; the research was territorially delimited by the regulations of the State of Jalisco, Mexico, and temporally, the regulations in force in 2023 were studied.

The research approach was empirical-qualitative (Villabella, 2015), since the research design was flexible in order to answer the research questions. An explanatory approach was taken based on López's (2021) framework, for which the theoretical-deductive method was used, in accordance with reasoning and deduction.

Thus, the research questions were as follows:

What is a family business in Mexico?

What are the elements of family business patrimony and of family patrimony in Jalisco?

What provisions govern family patrimony in Jalisco?

What consequences does the creation of family patrimony have for the family business?

The documentary research technique was also used, since the legal framework relevant to the object of study was consulted, as well as relevant literature, and books and articles related to the topic.

Development

The family business in Mexico

The business as such has no definition under Mexican commercial law; nonetheless, as Dávalos (2010) explains, the Ley Federal del Trabajo (Federal Labor Law) and the Código Fiscal de la Federación (Federal Fiscal Code) do refer to it. Labor legislation, for its part, explains in Article 16 that it is an economic unit capable of producing or distributing goods or services (Ley Federal del Trabajo, 1970), while tax legislation refers to specific activities that may be carried out by an individual or a legal entity (Código Fiscal de la Federación, 1981); consequently, a business, viewed from a legal standpoint, may be an individual or a legal entity — generally a commercial company — that forms an economic unit carrying out business activities.

Organizations can be identified by their own particular characteristics, among which is a structured group of people (Omaña and Briceño, 2013); thus, in the literature there are multiple definitions of family business, among which Burgoa, Herrera, and Treviño (2013) conceptualize the family business as "a form of organization surrounded by a culture whose characteristics are being transcendent and complex, and which is, in turn, different from other forms of organization both in its philosophy and its ownership, the way it does business, and its type of financing"; thus, the authors highlight organization as the differentiating element from other types of businesses.

For her part, Soto (2013) carries out a more thorough review of the literature in Mexico regarding the term, and notes that definitions follow the three-circle model, which explains the family business in terms of the family as the base, the business as the activity, and ownership as the asset; nonetheless, the author also notes divergence of opinion regarding the succession and organizational factors involved.

In turn, San Martín and Durán (2017) explain the dichotomy among the definitions authors give for the term, and conclude that the family business refers to business activities carried out by a family, including ownership, management, and control, but also the goal of passing on this continuity to future generations; this adds the element of family continuity in the ownership and management of the business entity.

In the case of Jalisco, according to the IIEG (2022), the family business is considered an economic unit in which the owners or partners, as well as the employees, are related to one another; and, additionally, decision-making is carried out strategically by the family members who make it up.

Díaz (2011) states that the family business can be legally constituted as a commercial company, notably as a sociedad anónima (stock corporation) or a sociedad de responsabilidad limitada (limited liability company); to which can be added the Sociedad por Acciones Simplificada (Simplified Stock Company) (Favier and Spagnolo, 2018) in the Mexican case; although it should be noted that it may also be an individual who employs family members to carry out business activities.

In keeping with this line of reasoning, the following elements can be identified as characteristics of the family business:

Legally, it may be an individual or a legal entity.

It has a family basis, meaning that the members of this entity must share family ties, whether as owners, partners, or employees.

It carries out commercial or industrial activities for profit.

It has family-type patrimony, management, and organization; that is, specific assets are allocated for the business's operation, while, at the same time, control and decision-making are carried out among members who share family ties.

There is an intention for its continuity and succession to future generations; this leads to the observation that the patrimonial element is, on one hand, the physical element of the family business and, on the other, a fundamental constituent for the business to exist.

Composition of family business patrimony

The Instituto de Investigaciones Jurídicas (2016) states, in a first sense, that patrimony "appears to indicate the assets a child has, inherited from their father and grandparents" (p. 2794), which entails the transmission of assets between parents and children.

Thus, Casillas (2020) explains that, in the case of patrimony and the business, family patrimony belongs to the members of a family and is built up over time as a result of the business's activity, which pertains to assets — not liabilities — generated from the business activity and owned by the family nucleus.

Likewise, family patrimony goes beyond the financial aspect or economic wealth, since it also contains intangible or non-economic patrimony related to resources generated of a human, intellectual, organizational, social, and emotional nature, with both types of patrimony complementing each other to promote the development of the business (Casillas, 2020).

It should be recalled that, from a legal standpoint, patrimony comprises two elements, one an asset side made up of goods and rights, and the other a liability side made up of charges and obligations (Instituto de Investigaciones Jurídicas, 2016); for this reason, business patrimony must also take into account the underlying charges and obligations of the business, since this is properly the liability side, which affects and reduces the asset side.

Confusion between business patrimony and family patrimony is a day-to-day problem in the life of these businesses; in this regard, Barroso and Barriuso (2014) note that, although the convergence of both types of patrimony can be advantageous, it also poses a challenge in managing the business.

To resolve this merging of patrimonies, Leach (2002), cited in Barroso and Barriuso (2014), suggests separating family life from work life, or developing strategies that help distinguish family problems from business problems, so as to achieve a balance through the professionalization of family members. Another viable solution, little studied in the literature, is separating the two patrimonial pools through the constitution of family patrimony, as discussed below.

It should be kept in mind that when the family business chooses to be constituted as a commercial company, it will have its own patrimony and legal personality, separate from that of the partners who constitute it (Mantilla, 2021); the problem of dividing family patrimony and family business patrimony arises only when it is an individual who carries out business activities.

Family patrimony in Mexico

Family patrimony is defined, from a legal standpoint, as "an asset or set of assets that the law designates as temporarily inalienable or exempt from seizure in order to secure the family's alimony creditors" (Instituto de Investigaciones Jurídicas, 2016, p. 2804); the law thus represents family patrimony as the positive side of patrimony that seeks to secure the support of its members through the protection of the assets that make it up.

Cárdenas (1998) shares this view of patrimony and adds that this regime seeks to protect the family from uncertainty. However, De la Mata Pizaña and Garzón Jiménez (2008) disagree with this position, as they consider that "it is not a patrimony… nor does it strictly belong to a family, since a family has no legal personality" (p. 355), which is explained by the fact that its asset and liability elements do not converge, nor is it incorporated as an attribute of the personality of an individual or legal entity.

The authors in question add that it is a form of property rights over the assets a family uses to meet its needs and of which its members are the holders; hence, family patrimony refers to a right, not to a pool of assets, rights, obligations, and charges.

Family patrimony in Jalisco

Regardless of the position taken on the nature of this type of patrimony, its constitution, composition, and regulation in Mexico is governed by state civil law (Cárdenas, 1998); thus, in the case of the State of Jalisco, it is regulated under Title Eleven of the Código Civil para el Estado de Jalisco (Civil Code for the State of Jalisco, CCEJ), in Articles 777 to 795.

The composition of family patrimony is set out in Article 777 of the CCEJ, which establishes the following as objects of family patrimony:

I. The house the family lives in, including household furniture and equipment;

II. A motor vehicle;

III. The equipment and tools of the micro or small industry that serves as the family's economic support;

IV. The cultivable plot held in full ownership; and

V. The small landholding under the terms of the Ley Agraria (Agrarian Law); and

VI. The lot designated for the construction of a family dwelling (CCEJ, 1995).

Thus, family patrimony seeks to protect assets directly related to family life, such as the family home and vehicle, as well as the source of family support, such as the equipment and tools necessary for the family business, provided it is a micro or small business; hence there is a direct connection with the operation and continuity of the family business.

It should be noted that state legislation does not provide for a minimum or maximum value limit for the constitution of family patrimony; rather, its constitution is limited to the assets listed in the aforementioned Article 777, noting also that it is not necessary to meet all the categories listed in order to constitute it, leaving it to the discretion of those constituting it to choose which assets should make up the family patrimony.

On the other hand, Article 778 provides that this type of patrimony may be constituted by any family member, with "family" defined by that same article as "any group of people who live in a home, are united by marriage, common-law union, or blood relation, and who, by law or voluntarily, share unity in managing the household" (CCEJ, 1995); this pertains to what is socially considered family, and is debatable insofar as it defines common-law union as a relationship between a man and a woman, which could be discussed in light of the evolution of the concept of family, as noted by De la Fuente (2012) and Benítez (2017).

Along the same lines, Pérez (2010) argues that family patrimony could be constituted by any family member, or by any person wishing to protect their family, which in the State of Jalisco is limited only to those persons who reside in the same dwelling and share family ties.

Effects and limits of family patrimony with respect to third parties

To constitute family patrimony, what is set out in Article 784 (CCEJ, 1995) applies. The effects of creating family patrimony are contained in Articles 779 and 780, which establish that family members remain the owners of the properties, although the assets, being protected under this regime, become non-transferable and exempt from seizure; that is, they cannot be used as collateral or mortgaged, cannot be sold, assigned, or transferred, and also cannot be subject to seizure.

On the other hand, only one family patrimony is permitted per family; thus, if a patrimony has already been constituted and a second one is sought while the first persists, the second constitution of patrimony will have no legal effect, as provided in Article 783 (CCEJ, 1995).

It is possible to replace assets of the family patrimony when they wear out, requiring authorization from a judge for such replacement, as set out in Article 789 (CCEJ, 1995). Likewise, it is possible to establish the succession of family patrimony, for which it is necessary to indicate the names of the successors in the event of the death of those who constituted it, pursuant to Article 787 (CCEJ, 1995).

The extinguishment of family patrimony requires the intervention of a judge, largely due to the notices that must be given to state authorities regarding it, as established in Article 792 (CCEJ, 1995). On the other hand, to carry out its extinguishment, it is necessary for the beneficiaries to request it, either because it is their wish or because a justified cause arises that prevents its continuation, pursuant to Article 791 (CCEJ, 1995).

Conclusions

Although there are many definitions of the family business, it should be kept in mind that legislation does not define the family business; rather, it is authors who, through their interpretations, have given this form of organization its characteristics and qualities; thus, this paper concludes that the family business is an economic unit in which three elements distinguishing this structure can be identified, namely, a family, a patrimony, and an organization, which answers the first research question.

In studying the patrimonial element of this business, it is noted that the literature takes into account only the asset side of patrimony, including goods and capital, without considering that the classic conception of patrimony in turn includes obligations and charges, which answers the second research question.

A recurring problem in this type of organization is the division between family assets and business assets, which arises in the Mexican case when the family business takes the legal form of an individual carrying out business activities; since, if the family decides to constitute a commercial company to carry out business activities, the company's patrimony will not form part of the family patrimony, as it will be a separate legal person from the family members who make it up.

However, when the dilemma of the family business and its patrimony arises, constituting a family patrimony viewed from a legal standpoint is a viable and feasible solution that, on one hand, allows the micro and small business to continue in the face of adversity and, on the other, ensures family members' survival through the protection of assets under this legal regime, and, lastly, separates business assets from family assets.

Thus, family patrimony in Mexico is regulated by the civil codes of the federal entities, so the rules for its constitution and the elements that make it up depend on each State. In the case of Jalisco, its regulatory framework is found in Articles 777 to 795 of the CCEJ. Analyzing its composition shows that it is a property-protection regime that takes into account only assets identified with members of a family, and also considers the basic tools and equipment needed for the family business's operation; this answers the third research question posed.

The consequences of creating this regime with respect to third parties are that its assets cannot be encumbered, transferred, or seized; these measures thus seek to preserve the assets necessary for sustaining the family nucleus, thereby answering the fourth and final research question.

Finally, the study's limitations include the local nature of the rules governing family patrimony in Mexico; likewise, future lines of research concern the family protocol, succession in the family business, and modes of family business organization regarding patrimony and its managerial administration.
