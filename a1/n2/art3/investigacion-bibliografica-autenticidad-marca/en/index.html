Abstract.

This article aims to present most relevant articles on brand authenticity published in Proquest up to the year 2022, so that it is easy to identify existing gaps in the literature. To fulfill this purpose, the articles were ordered according to the ADO model (antecedents, decisions and outcomes proposed by Paul and Benito in 2017), with the purpose of synthesizing the connection that exists between the different investigations and thus facilitating future research. It is concluded that research is needed that addresses each of the parts of the ADO model as well as the interrelation of the three stages and how the modification of one stage affects the following, to have a greater understanding of the construction of brand authenticity.

Keywords:

Brand authenticity, bibliographic, literature

Introduction

Brand authenticity is considered one of the fundamental qualities in current marketing practices, owing to consumers' constant pursuit of authenticity (Gilmore & Pine II, 2007; Potter, 2011). This has led brand managers to permeate their brands with markers of authenticity (Beverland, M. B., & Luxton, S., 2005; Beverland, M. B., Lindgreen, A., & Vink, M. W., 2008). Nonetheless, confusion still surrounds the nature and use of the term (Beverland, M. B., 2005), owing to a lack of consensus, consistency, and clarity regarding the conceptualization of what authenticity is (Eggers, F., O'Dwyer, M., Kraus, S., Vallaster, C., & Güldenberg, S., 2013; Beverland, M. B., & Farrelly, F. J.,2010).

Concept

There is agreement on the importance of authenticity; nonetheless, there is no universally accepted definition (Beverland, Michael B., and Francis J. Farrelly, 2010). Some proposed definitions are:

"Authenticity encompasses what is genuine, real and/or true" (Beverland, and Francis, 2010, p.3).

"Authenticity is not something we have or don't have. It is a practice, a conscious choice of how we want to live. Authenticity is the decision to be real and show ourselves as we truly are. The decision to be honest. The decision to let our true self be seen" (Brown, 2010, p.5).

"Brand authenticity is the extent to which consumers perceive a brand to be faithful and true to itself and to its consumers. At the same time, it helps consumers be true to themselves." (Morhart, et al., 2015, p.4).

Across these three definitions, we find that the common denominator is being real and/or true. However, the definitions do not specify which elements must be kept true. On the other hand, Brené Brown's definition opens up the possibility that, since it is a decision, there are indeed elements we can act upon in order to express authenticity. In addition, research shows that authenticity can be expressed in qualitatively very different ways, and can be both a social construct and a source of evidence (Grayson, and Martinec, 2014).

Studies that have addressed the problem

All of the articles analyzed were organized according to a model called ADO (for its initials in English) proposed by Paul and Benito (2017) in their article titled "A review of research on outward foreign direct investment from emerging countries, including China: what do we know, how do we know and where should we be heading?" This model organizes information into antecedents, decisions, and outcomes.

Figure 1 shows this order and the relationship among the base elements of authenticity (antecedents), the communication tools and brand elements that influence the perception of authenticity (decisions), and the benefits that the perception of brand authenticity brings both to brands and to consumers (outcomes).

To this model was added the consumer's perception of authenticity, which is made up of the scales used to measure brand authenticity as perceived by consumers. It is worth noting that the perception of brand authenticity can only be measured from "decisions" and "outcomes," since antecedents are elements that only the brand can define, unlike decisions and outcomes, which are the forms and benefits through which these antecedents are externalized.

Starting with the antecedents section, we find the articles that discuss the core attributes present in the conceptualization of brand authenticity, its classification, and the elements used to communicate it.

In the decisions section are the articles that discuss the communication tools and brand elements that positively influence being perceived as an authentic brand.

In the third section, outcomes, we find the articles that discuss the benefits that a brand's being perceived as authentic brings to the brand and to users.

Finally, in the perception section are the scales and dimensions that have been used to measure the perception of brand authenticity.

Axes of brand authenticity

Once the process of constructing brand authenticity (antecedents, decisions, and outcomes) is understood, the information was reorganized into 4 axes: attributes, communication, benefits, and perception.

Attributes of authenticity. In the first axis, corresponding to the attributes of brand authenticity, are all the articles that discuss brand authenticity as a concept, the elements brands use as a basis for defining their authenticity and the elements users seek, as well as the types and/or classifications of authenticity.

Base elements according to brands. Regarding the research addressing the base elements brands use to define their authenticity, four articles were found, which are detailed below:

Beverland, in his article published in 2005 titled: "Crafting Brand Authenticity: The Case of Luxury Wines," states that some of the elements are: use of place as a reference, commitment to traditional, handmade production practices, as well as excellence in production, consistency of style, and history and culture as reference points.

Napoli, Dickson, and Beverland, in their article "The brand authenticity continuum: strategic approaches for building value," list attributes such as: brand commitment to quality, brand commitment to its heritage, artisanal expertise, knowledge and time invested in developing knowledge and skills, maintenance of quality standards, passion for the craft, and symbolism associated with the brand.

Peñaloza, in her article "The Commodification of the American West: Marketers' Production of Cultural Meanings at the Trade Show," details that some elements are: cultural values and meanings, juxtaposition of commercial, educational, and business motives, historical tradition, and the use of business activity as a basis for claims of authenticity.

Finally, Newman, in his article titled: "Authenticity Is Contagious: Brand Essence and the Original Source of Production," contributes the original place of manufacture as one of the elements brands use to define their authenticity.

Base elements according to the consumer. On the consumer's side, in an article published by Leigh, Thomas W., Cara Peters, and Jeremy Shelton in 2006, it is found that consumers seek brand authenticity when purchasing an MG car in the following respects: meeting the ideal standard they hold about the brand, preserving the brand's heritage, the brand experience with the product, and the identity the user builds and confirms by using the brand.

On the other hand, research carried out by Munoz, C. L., Wood, N. T., & Solomon, M. R. in 2006 reveals that consumers find authenticity, in Irish bars, in both tangible and intangible elements. Some of the tangible elements are: stereotypical imagery, the color green, the leprechaun (a type of elf), and the shamrock (three-leafed green clover). As for the intangible elements, these include: atmosphere and its elements, staff with an Irish accent, and a casual, fun, and welcoming environment.

Classifications. Brand authenticity has been classified by various authors, among whom the classifications of Grayson and Martinec, Beverland, Lindgreen and Vink, and Morhart, Malär, Guèvremont, Girardin, and Grohmann stand out. In the literature, classifications of authenticity arise from the blending, in varying degrees, of a brand's indexical, iconic, and existential characteristics. Indexical characteristics are understood as those that make the perceiver believe that the object/brand really does have the space-time connection it conveys. Iconic characteristics are those that make the object/brand be perceived as similar to something else. For these characteristics to be interpreted correctly, the perceiver must have some prior knowledge or expectation (Grayson and Martinec, 2004). Finally, existential characteristics are those attributed to the brand that help consumers discover their true self through their consumption (Morhart et al., 2015).

In order to better understand each author's perspective, their different views are detailed below. Starting with Grayson and Martinec's classification, in their article "Consumer Perceptions of Iconicity and Indexicality and Their Influence on Assessments of Authentic Market Offerings," published in 2004, two variables were used to formulate all the classifications: indexical and iconic characteristics. Viewed as a spectrum, these variables sit at the extremes, indicating that a brand's authenticity can be purely indexical, purely iconic, or fall into six distinct degrees depending on the amount of indexical or iconic elements present in the brand's application.

Beginning by clarifying the extremes, indexical authenticity is defined as that whose characteristics are factually and spatiotemporally linked to something else. For example, two writing pens may look exactly alike, but the authentic Shakespeare pen will be the one he actually used. On the opposite end, iconic authenticity is used to describe something whose physical manifestation resembles, looks like, or alludes to something that is authentically indexical, which would speak of a reproduction or an authentic re-creation. For example, when you go to a museum and they sell you a pen like the one Shakespeare used, it is understood that it is not exactly the one he used, yet the pen would be a faithful reproduction of the one he used.

Among the six classifications in the middle, moving from the most purely indexical to the most purely iconic, according to Grayson and Martinec, we find: Real indexicality linked to the inhabitant: describes things believed to have a spatiotemporal connection with a person. Example: Moctezuma's headdress; Real indexicality linked to the inhabitant's era: describes things believed to have a spatiotemporal connection with a person's era. Example: a notebook like those used in Da Vinci's time; Hypothetical indexicality linked to the inhabitant: describes things that could potentially have been spatiotemporally linked to a person. Example: the first golden Snitch caught by Harry Potter, displayed at the Harry Potter theme park.

As for the iconic categories, we find: Iconic with history: something that looks like an image constructed on the historical facts the consumer has been exposed to. Example: the reproduction of Van Gogh's room based on how he described it; Iconic with old objects: something that looks like an image constructed by exposing the consumer to how things age. Example: a newly made chair that looks as though time has passed over it; Iconic with fiction: something that looks like an image constructed by exposing the consumer to fictional narratives. Example: the decoration of Hogwarts Castle based on the descriptions in the books.

For Beverland, Lindgreen, and Vink, according to what is presented in their article "Projecting authenticity through advertising," authenticity can only be classified in 3 ways, which are made up of two distinct types of elements and the sum of both. Pure or literal authenticity: which reflects an unwavering commitment to tradition and place, and makes use of indexical characteristics; Approximate authenticity: symbolic or abstract impressions of tradition created by advertisers, using iconic characteristics; Moral authenticity: created from an internal need and not focused on external demand, making use of both indexical and iconic characteristics (Beverland et al., 2008).

Finally, for Morhart, Malär, Guèvremont, Girardin, and Grohmann, brand authenticity is something binary: it either exists or it does not, and is therefore not classifiable. Nonetheless, they propose a classification of types of elements that construct brand authenticity, which must be interrelated with one another to result in a brand perceived as authentic; there are three: Indexical: Elements based on verifiable facts or evidence about the brand, such as origin labels, age, ingredients, or performance; Iconic: Elements based on subjective mental associations built by the brand for consumers, as opposed to the objective properties present in indexical authenticity; Existential: Elements that help consumers discover their true self or feel more in touch with their true self.

Communication of authenticity. In the second axis of the classification of all the literature reviewed is communication, a section that includes the articles discussing the communication and/or dissemination tools useful for projecting brand authenticity and the brand variables that influence the perception of brand authenticity.

Tools. Starting with the tools, the first one is found in the article "Crafting Brand Authenticity: The Case of Luxury Wines" by Michael B. Beverland, whose research analyzes the strategies 26 luxury wine brands use to project their authenticity to consumers. For these brands, being perceived as authentic is crucial, since it reinforces their status, allows them to dominate price setting, and keeps competitors at bay. To achieve this projection, the tool used was "storytelling." The brands created a story through which they got the public to recognize them as sincere and authentic by expressing their uniqueness, passion for artisanal wine production, relationship with the place, and putting their commercial motives in the background (Beverland, 2005).

In Lisa Peñaloza's article titled: "The Commodification of the American West: Marketers' Production of Cultural Meanings at the Trade Show," which empirically analyzes the processes marketing specialists use to create cultural meanings at a western livestock and rodeo trade show, it was found that regionalism is one of the main factors that help build cultural meanings, in addition to the exchange that occurs between producers and consumers in the creation of culture. Another of the most important factors was the use and adaptation of history over time. Adapting history refers to evolving the discourse and the way tradition is projected and communicated over time, so that tradition remains current. And as a third and final highlighted factor was awakening consumers' use of imagination, so that the symbolism or images projected are amplified by the consumer's mind (Peñaloza, 2000).

Another tool used to project authenticity is advertising, as described and shared by Beverland, Lindgreen, and Vink in their article titled: "Projecting authenticity through advertising." In this research, 23 semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with consumers, marketers, or B2B buyers, whether or not they consumed Trappist beer. The objective of the interviews was to explore consumers' response to different beer ads and whether they classified them as authentic or not. The findings of this research led to the identification of the three forms of authenticity described above. However, when respondents had to decide whether they considered the brand authentic, they used only one of the three forms of authenticity to assess the ads, based on the indexical or iconic characteristics they perceived. This research concludes that advertising plays a very important role in reinforcing and building the image of authenticity, since it can be used to project all 3 types of authenticity (pure, approximate, and moral), thereby achieving the widest possible spread of that perception among consumers (Beverland, Lindgreen, and Vink, 2008).

To close out this section is Dwivedi's article, who considers that the perception of brand authenticity is the result of clear communication of brand positioning, whether through advertising, social media, sponsorships, and/or corporate social responsibility actions. Nonetheless, in his article titled: "Building brand authenticity in fast-moving consumer goods via consumer perceptions of Brand marketing communications," he states that this is at least true for energy brands that have already been on the market for a while. Another of his conclusions in this research is that, for communication strategies to be fruitful, brand philosophy, brand promise, and brand personality must be effectively communicated, as well as delivering on what is promised, since these first three aspects are key elements of brand identity that, when aligned with brand image, positively affect the perception of brand authenticity (Dwivedi, 2018).

Adding to Dwivedi's research, the study carried out by Wan Abdul Ghani delves further into the four forms of marketing communication also used by Dwivedi: advertising, social media, sponsorship, and corporate social responsibility actions, but with the aim of determining which one(s) directly impact(s) brand authenticity in the category of local coffee brands. The findings reveal that social media has the highest correlation, though it should be taken into account that the age of the surveyed sample was mostly 20–29 years old. This research adds to the body of knowledge on brand authenticity; however, the author recommends extending it to other categories and age ranges to gain a broader perspective on the findings (Wan Abdul Ghani, Wan & Rashid, Norhidayah, 2020).

Brand variables that influence authenticity. Among the articles discussing the brand variables that influence the perception of brand authenticity is the research titled "Authenticity in branding – exploring antecedents and consequences of brand authenticity" carried out by Fritz, Schoenmueller, and Bruhn, which shows that brand authenticity is influenced by brand variables closely related to the brand's past (brand heritage and brand nostalgia), its virtue (through brand commercialization, brand clarity, and the brand's social commitment), the brand's representation by employees (employee passion), and the consumer's identification with the brand (brand legitimacy and self-congruence with the person one currently is).

The research takes into account the consumer's level of engagement with the brand and the impact (or lack thereof) of different brand variables for each population. It was found that the effect brand heritage has on the perception of brand authenticity is much greater for consumers with low engagement than for those with high engagement. Another difference is that signals that can be processed more easily can be more persuasive to low-engagement consumers, since, in the absence of a brand preference, they use the perception of brand authenticity as a signal of quality.

The variable exerting the strongest effect on brand authenticity is perceived cultural fit, that is, brand legitimacy between the consumer and the brand. It is therefore suggested that brand managers understand the culture, symbols, and behavior (values and norms) that permeate their target consumer, in order to integrate these characteristics into the brand's culture.

Another variable to take into account is coherence between brand behavior and its purpose. To improve the perception of authenticity, the research found that it is necessary to implement a policy aimed at preserving brand image and identity, which includes factors such as: values, norms, mission, and all of the brand's communication activities, with the aim of achieving consistent brand behavior and thereby reinforcing the perception of brand authenticity (Fritz, & Bruhn, 2017).

Benefits of authenticity. In the third axis, corresponding to the benefits of authenticity, are the articles that discuss the brand variables positively impacted by consumers' perception of authenticity, as well as the needs consumers satisfy through these brands.

Benefits for the brand. First, as can be read in the only article found on brand authenticity in Mexico, carried out by Echeverría-Ríos, Medina-Quintero, and Abrego-Almazán, which focused on the brewing sector in Northeastern Mexico, it was found that both brand authenticity-commitment-quality and authenticity-sincerity positively affect brand image and reputation, unlike authenticity-heritage, which does not influence either of the two aforementioned variables (Echeverría, et al., 2021). The research carried out by Ballantyne, Warren, and Nobbs, titled "The evolution of brand choice," reinforces the finding of Echeverría-Ríos, Medina-Quintero, and Abrego-Almazán, as it details the influence heritage and the perception of authenticity exert on brand image. Starting from the notion that brands lack functionality and meaningful differentiation from one another, the researchers identify brand image as the variable to strengthen in order to project this differentiation between products by offering distinction and individuality. Because brand image shapes consumers' perception of its attributes and affects brand choice, the authors consider it very important for the marketing plan to consider developing brands that encompass elements of heritage and authenticity, since these two variables positively impact brand image, leading consumers to perceive them as expert, trustworthy brands in their category (Ballantyne, et al., 2006).

Moving to another variable, the research carried out by Fritz, Schoenmueller, and Bruhn in 2017, already mentioned above, found that brand authenticity positively affects the relationship between the consumer and brand quality in service and retail brands, which in turn positively influences consumer behavior toward the brand. It also fosters a strong emotional bond between the consumer and the brand, which translates into brand loyalty, increased purchase intent, and willingness to pay a premium price, as well as tolerance for bad experiences with the brand and forgiveness of mistakes (Fritz, et al., 2017). In the luxury brand sector, Adiati and Nuraini, in their article titled: "Exploring the Consequences of Brand Authenticity," explored the consequences brand authenticity brings in this sector, in addition to analyzing the effect that the relationship between brand quality and trust has on purchase intent and its relationship with authenticity. The findings reveal that, unlike in the service and retail sector, brand quality does not influence purchase intent for luxury brands, unlike brand trust, which is in turn influenced by the perception of brand authenticity and does translate directly into an increase in purchase intent (Adiati Pratomo, et al., 2020).

On the other hand, Eggers, O'Dwyer, Kraus, Vallaster, and Güldenberg, in their study titled: "The impact of brand authenticity on brand trust and SME growth: A CEO perspective," confirm that brand authenticity helps drive SME growth in markets, because both consumers and other stakeholders seek sincerity and meaning in the brands they choose, which translates into brand trust, and this trust translates into increased purchase intent. Being clear and transparent about where the company comes from and who it is today — that is, communicating this part of its authenticity — results in brand trust. Because of the transparency the internet and social media have brought to everyday life, it is necessary for companies, across all their facets, not only to be perceived as authentic through their communication. The challenge lies not in communicating brand authenticity, but in fostering organizational cultures that strengthen and encourage it (Eggers, et al., 2013).

The most important finding of the research titled: "Brands Taking a Stand: Authentic Brand Activism or Woke Washing?" carried out by: Vredenburg, Kapitan, Spry, and Kemper, is that when brands align their activist message, purpose, values, and corporate practices, they create the greatest potential for social change and the greatest gains in brand value. On the other hand, if these four variables show discrepancy, brand value and its potential to drive social change will be harmed (Vredenburg, et al., 2020).

Adding further benefits for brands perceived as authentic is the study carried out by Lu, Gursoy, and Lu, titled "Authenticity perceptions, brand equity and brand choice intention: the case of ethnic restaurants," which focuses on the ethnic restaurant sector and confirms that the perception of brand authenticity positively affects the four dimensions of brand equity: brand awareness, brand associations, perceived quality, and brand loyalty. The four dimensions also hold a certain relationship to one another, such as brand awareness and brand image, dimensions whose higher value predicts a greater likelihood of brand loyalty. On the other hand, perceived quality alone directly and positively influences brand loyalty, as well as purchase preference. Additionally, this study confirms that brand equity positively impacts consumers' brand choice. It can therefore be inferred that a brand being perceived as authentic increases its chances of being chosen by the consumer (Lu, et al., 2015).

Oh, Prado, Korelo, and Frizzo, in their research titled "The effect of brand authenticity on consumer–brand relationships," carried out through 347 consumer surveys in the United States and Brazil, found that brand authenticity influences self-reinforcement, which connects the self with the brand and decreases the distance between the consumer and the brand. This results in increased purchase intent, as well as the desire to visit the store (whether physical or virtual) and recommend the brand to others (Oh, Prado, Korelo, and Frizzo, 2019).

Finally, in the study titled: "Local versus global food consumption: the role of brand authenticity" carried out by Riefler on local and global beverage brands, it was found that global brands benefit more from being perceived as authentic compared with local brands. This means that, as a strategy, local brands should not only bet on their authenticity but also on moving beyond the local, since with each passing day the effect that "local" has on the consumer keeps decreasing (Riefler, 2020).

Benefits for users. As for the benefits the perception of authenticity brings to consumers, there are those found by Beverland in his study "The quest for authenticity in consumption: consumers' purposive choice of authentic cues to shape experienced outcomes." These are three: control, connection, and virtue. When consumers seek control, they usually rely on firsthand experience and independent judgment, in addition to seeking to effectively solve a practical problem. Consumers who seek connection are driven by their judgments of authenticity; in addition, they follow community norms and seek to be closer to others. And finally, consumers who seek virtue through authentic brands look for brands that address universal needs and consistently apply a set of moral values (Beverland and Farelly, 2010).

In the study carried out by Oh, Prado, Korelo, and Frizzo, mentioned earlier under benefits for the brand, benefits for consumers are also presented, since the perception of brand authenticity positively influences the three assets that reinforce the self: self-attraction, self-enrichment, and self-enablement. When self-attraction is satisfied, the consumer seeks to reinforce themselves by receiving hedonic and pleasurable benefits. By enriching the self, they seek to reinforce themselves through symbolic benefits that represent the ideal self of the past, present, and future. And finally, by enabling the self, they seek to receive functional benefits (Oh, Prado, Korelo, and Frizzo, 2019).

Perception of authenticity. Finally, the articles within the fourth and final axis, perception, are those that propose and test a scale for measuring the perception of brand authenticity.

Measurement scales. Different articles measuring the perception of brand authenticity across completely different categories from one another were analyzed, none of which was from the design sector. Based on these articles, the most commonly used dimensions were illustrated, which are: honesty (Akbar, 2016; Becker, et al., 2019; Morhart, et al., 2015; Napoli, et al., 2014; Tran, 2018), originality (Akbar, 2016; Bruhn et al., 2012; Tran, 2018), heritage (Becker et al., 2019; Beverland, 2006; Napoli et al, 2014), quality (Akbar, 2016; Beverland, 2006; Napoli et al., 2014), consistency (Beverland, 2006; Bruhn, et al., 2012; Morhart et al., 2015), and integrity (Morhart et al., 2015; Tran, 2018; Bruhn et al., 2012).

When analyzing the dimensions according to their closeness to the definitions of authenticity, honesty was ranked first, since the definitions of authenticity present attributes such as "genuine, real, and/or true" (Beverland, & Farrelly, 2010); and honesty is measured through the perception of the presentation of a credible message (Becker et al., 2019), level of credibility (Morhart, 2015), of realism (defined by the author as sincerity) (Tran, 2018), of sincerity (Napoli et al., 2014), and of honesty (Akbar, 2016).

Another dimension with strong closeness to authenticity is originality, since it is defined as "brand genuineness and uniqueness" (Bruhn et al., 2012), and what is genuine is considered authentic.

Finally, the third dimension with the greatest closeness is heritage, though its proximity is not conceptual but rather by brand application, as expressed by Beverland in his article titled: "The 'real thing': Branding authenticity in the luxury wine trade." He states that brands hold, in their individual histories, the potential to build their brand programs. Building a bridge between the brand and its history gives the brand meaning, and in the eyes of consumers, having a solid heritage signals reliability. Heritage relates to authenticity because part of "showing one's true self" lies in honoring one's history, which can be expressed through indexical characteristics (Morhart et al., 2015).

Conclusions

After organizing all the information, analyzing the gaps or areas that have not been explored by these studies, and with the aim of deepening the understanding of the construction of brand authenticity, the following main findings were identified:

  • Authenticity and brand authenticity, as a concept, present confusion in their application because there is no consensus on their definition.

  • The concept of brand authenticity is an unexplored area in Mexico.

  • Research is needed that adds to each of the parts of the ADO model, as well as to the interrelation of the three stages and how the modification of one stage affects those that follow or precede it.
