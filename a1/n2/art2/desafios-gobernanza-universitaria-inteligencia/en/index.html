Challenges for university governance in the age of artificial intelligence

Desafíos para la gobernanza universitaria en la era de la inteligencia artificial

GESPYE GESTIÓN PÚBLICA Y EMPRESARIAL (e-ISSN: 3061-8630)

Year 1, No. 2 (Jul-Dec 2024) • Pages 16-28 • DOI https://doi.org/10.32870/gespye.vi2.2

Received: 29/06/2024 • Approved: 13/09/2024 • Published: 30/11/2024

Abstract.

The convergence between university governance and artificial intelligence (AI) presents an interesting field of study for theoretical and practical analysis that contributes to improving efficiency, equity, and the quality of decision-making and the exercise of power. This document is an exploratory analysis of the main challenges faced by governments of higher education institutions (HEIs) with the introduction of AI into university life. Its aim is to study the governance approach as a form of government and the implications that artificial intelligence has for university management. To understand both concepts, the different meanings and definitions of university governance and AI are first described. Subsequently, the influence and potential that this modern technology has for educational development are analyzed, and finally, five challenges that universities will face are listed: political, regulatory and ethical challenges, infrastructure and capabilities challenges, human and economic resources challenges, and distrust. Finally, as a conclusion, there is a reflection on the possibilities and risks of rethinking governance processes and management practices from a solely technological perspective.

Keywords:

University governance, higher education, artificial intelligence, educational management, public policies.

Introduction

Higher education, particularly in Latin America and in Mexico, has established itself as a strategic pillar for the development of society. The presence of public universities has historically been the main driver of social mobility, and in some cases of economic development. The fulfillment of universities' substantive functions — teaching, research, and outreach — does not take place in a vacuum, but rather within a state, national, and international context strongly influenced by social, technological, economic, ecological, and other trends. These trends determine, to a greater or lesser degree, institutional policies, and modify, replace, and/or improve management processes and teaching and research practices.

This paper will analyze two of these trends, their convergence, the framework that emerges from their interplay, and, above all, the challenges they pose for the management of higher education and the governance of universities. First, a descriptive analysis will be carried out of governance as a governing approach in universities, beginning with a review of its different meanings and analytical criteria; subsequently, the second major trend will be studied, one that, in more recent years, has landed in public universities as an element that can be innovative and disruptive, all depending on how it is implemented and the institutional policies built for its regulation and use. Once the importance of these two aspects has been described and justified, a critical analysis is presented of the new challenges and configurations, as possibilities, that artificial intelligence represents for university governance; the analysis will focus on the advantages, disadvantages, and challenges posed by this tool, which has arrived to change meanings and perceptions both in the academic sphere — in teaching and research — and for the management and, above all, the governance of universities.

Given the above, there is a series of initial questions that will serve as the guiding thread of this document: To what extent, and through which actions or processes, does artificial intelligence influence university governments? What are the ethical issues to consider in the use of AI for decision-making and the selection of leadership? What is needed to develop and implement algorithmic systems at the university? Does the use of AI influence, or will it influence, performance? All these questions can lead us down various and different paths; AI already has a real impact on the work of higher education institutions today, but so far the focus of discussion has centered mainly on the practical and ethical issues of its use in teaching and research. It is therefore interesting to inquire into and explore, from a governance approach — and not only from this approach — the possible ways in which algorithmic governance might be put to the test within university management spaces.

Before beginning the analysis guided by the questions mentioned above, it is necessary to establish a conceptual framework for both elements (governance and artificial intelligence) that will also make it possible to understand the processes and practices in which university governments will be most influenced, modified, or even replaced.

University governance: paradigm, concept, or definition

Governance has repercussions for the administration and government of universities and impacts the structures, forms of government, and performance of higher education systems. The concept and its premises are positioned within university management; as Brunner (2011) notes, governance starts from the hypothesis that successful university organizations are those that manage to modify their governance — the functioning of their government and forms of management — in order to adapt it to the changing demands of the environment in which they operate and to the structural transformations in which they take part and of which they form part.

The indicators each HEI will choose to measure governance, as well as to some extent the type of research and the results it will obtain, depend on the governance approach each HEI adopts for purposes of analysis, measurement, and/or evaluation.

Universities are organizations characterized by carrying out decision-making actions, the distribution of authority, and processes and objectives that take into account the complexity of the internal and external actors and social, economic, and environmental factors involved (Ganga, 2014). To this end, and on an international scale, Brunner (2011) defines university governance as "the way in which institutions are organized and operated internally — from the standpoint of their government and management — and their relationships with external actors, with a view to ensuring the objectives of higher education" (Brunner, 2011, p. 137).

Eurydice (2008), for its part, distinguishes governance from management, or from corporate governance, in that the former focuses on the rules and mechanisms through which actors (councils and governing boards) influence decisions, how they are held accountable, and to whom. This distinction emphasizes governance as the framework within which the institution pursues its goals, objectives, and policies in a coherent and coordinated way, in order to answer the questions: who is in charge, and what are the sources of legitimacy for decisions made by the various actors? This conceptualization takes the institutional framework and power relations as the core of the analysis.

Along the same lines, Hirsh and Weber (2001) refer to governance as the formal and informal exercise of authority under laws, policies, and rules that articulate the rights and responsibilities of various actors, including the rules by which they interact. It is worth noting that Eurydice's (2008) document identifies the parts of institutional governance, mainly in European universities: a Rector, president, or Vice-Chancellor; the academic body; what it calls a decision-making body responsible for strategic planning and for determining the institution's direction; and finally, a group of educational, operational, and financial overseers or monitors. These are important elements to identify for creating indices and indicators aimed at measuring governance, since each of these parts has characteristics that can be measured from both a qualitative and quantitative approach.

According to Vidal and Vieira (2014), university governance is the exercise of collective control over the achievement of institutional and shared goals, and involves answering questions about who decides, how, when, and on what matters; this exercise is also linked to the analysis of institutional capacity for change in the correct way and at the right time, in accordance with the institution's needs in response to a given context. Other authors also link governance to decision-making structure and process, as is the case with Gayle et al. (2003).

In a document produced in 2009 by the Center for the Study of Higher Education Policy, titled "Higher Education Governance in Europe. Reforms Implemented" ("Gobernanza de la Educación Superior. Reformas realizadas en Europa"), it is noted that this is a highly contested concept referring to the exercise of collective control toward shared objectives. It relates to arrangements in which public or private actors seek to solve social problems or create social opportunities. It involves the articulation of interests and the achievement of objectives. It raises questions about who decides, when, and on what. It distinguishes between internal governance (institutional arrangements: lines of decision-making authority, financing, etc.) and external governance (macro-level institutional arrangements: laws, decrees, external evaluations, etc.) of HEIs. The same text notes that numerous studies have already been conducted on governance based on this definition, including those by Eurydice (2008), OECD (2002), and Brunner (2011).

In institutional governance, the analytical core focuses on the management and coordination processes of university governing action (Acosta, 2014), that is, on the organizational structures and structural configurations (Mintzberg, 1985) that link the action of university government with other institutional actors. This link, or articulation, as Brunner (2011) notes, is expressed in institutional agendas and policies.

In the document "University Governance: Theoretical and Empirical Approaches" ("Gobernanza universitaria: aproximaciones teóricas y empíricas"), coordinated by Ganga (2014), a conceptual framework for University Governance is developed; it arrives at the conviction that this concept is shaped by five elements — stakeholder groups, participation, context, issues, and elements of government — which interact in a structured way. The author concludes that, for a better understanding, one must start by defining governance as a set of university governing activities that involve decision-making processes taking into account the multiple stakeholder groups embedded within the respective institutions. He adds, further, that university governance will be concerned with how universities are organized and structured, and with how they are administered from the standpoint of their government and management, also incorporating the institutional design they have for managing links and relationships with other agents and actors in their environment.

What does university governance entail? To answer this question, authors Ganga et al. (2014) distinguish between two types of analysis: that of Corporate Governance and that of Strategic Management. These two levels of analysis are also distinguished in the field of politics and policy, as Casanova and Rodríguez (2014) note; the first, corporate governance, falls under politics, while university strategic management would fall under the category of policy. We draw here on the distinction between politics and policy to differentiate between one type of government and another, characterizing and contextualizing corporate governance as "arenas of power" and strategic management as guidelines and specific actions.

By way of conclusion for this section, governance is considered a polysemous term, largely understood based on the context and approach being applied to it. For the purposes of this article, we adopt the concept of governance as the institutional arrangements for encouraging cooperation around shared objectives that take place within structures and processes providing an administrative, regulatory, and management framework, within which power is distributed, policies and regulations are established, and decisions are made that shape university life. This includes: governing bodies and their processes, each university's organizational structures, the delineation and assignment of authority for decision-making within those organizational structures, and the processes by which policies are defined and implemented and decisions are made (Kaplin and Lee, 2007; Acosta, 2017).

These institutional arrangements take place within contexts of federal politics and policy that, in turn, influence the type of arrangement and the results that will be obtained. It is not the objective of this article to delve deeply into this context; nonetheless, it is recognized that the very recent incorporation of AI into the higher education agenda has not yet given governments time, even in a general way, to design policies for its use and regulation; there are some important efforts, such as that carried out by the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB), as a contribution to the collaborative process for Chile's National Artificial Intelligence Policy (Martínez and Vega, 2020). At the sectoral level, in Mexico, there is no clear strategy, guideline, or policy for the education sector that establishes the foundations, recommendations, and implications of AI in higher education.

Concepts and definitions of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence is one of these technological trends which, although it has come a long way since 1956, the year in which the term was first coined, has only in recent years gained relevance mainly for the public sector and in the educational field. Currently (as with the term governance) there is no universally accepted definition of AI. UNESCO's view of this technology centers on the imitation of human intelligence: "machines capable of imitating certain functionalities of human intelligence, including features like perception, learning, reasoning, problem-solving, language interaction, and even creative production" (UNESCO, 2021, para. 3).

From the private sector, technology companies have taken different approaches on the matter. Professor John McCarthy of Stanford University, considered one of the founders of AI, was the first to coin the term, defining it as "the science and engineering of making intelligent machines, especially intelligent computer programs." On the other hand, the company GOOGLE defines it as "the science of making things smart"; other, more technical definitions, such as that of AI Watch, draw on data science: "AI systems are software designed by humans that, given a complex goal, act in the physical or digital dimension by perceiving their environment through data acquisition, interpreting the collected structured or unstructured data, reasoning on the basis of the knowledge derived from this data or processing the information derived from it, and deciding the best action(s) to take to achieve the given goal" (Delipetrev, Tsinaraki, and Kostic, 2020, in Martínez and Vega, 2020).

Researcher Yoshua Bengio, a pioneer in the study of "deep learning," describes this branch of AI as a type of learning distinguished from "machine learning" in that it is a way of making machines intelligent by allowing computers to learn from different examples of the world and, based on this learning, gradually improve their responsiveness; this type of learning is special because, in the researcher's words, it is based on neural networks: it consists of trying to teach computers multiple levels of abstraction and representation, which makes them increasingly accurate. This type of learning is classified as predictive AI, a type of machine-learning algorithm that analyzes data and forecasts future events or outcomes, while generative AI is characterized by producing new content (UNESCO, 2023).

Among its recommendations, UNESCO invites decision-makers and experts to reach a consensus on the definition of artificial intelligence, a definition that will nonetheless need to change over time in line with technological advances (UNESCO, 2023, p. 21), and it stresses the importance of not losing sight of the ethical dimensions that must be integrated into it. In this sense, AI is considered as "systems that have the capacity to process data and information in a way that resembles intelligent behavior, including aspects of reasoning, learning, perception, prediction, planning, or control" (UNESCO, 2023, p. 13).

In one sense, AI is a tool accessible to the general public, mainly through the popular ChatGPT application, which opened the door to current discussions around its use in the classroom, in teaching and research, considering all the ethical and data- and information-use aspects that can be called into question; this tool can be used as an assistant, translator, or style corrector, a resource for the academic community whose future repercussions we still do not know.

Artificial Intelligence, the rollout of the 5G network, cybersecurity, augmented reality, and robotics are some of the trends shaping the present and that will define the future. Nonetheless, it is worth questioning how much of these technologies is actually accessible to public universities; from the standpoint of government, management, and administration, the field of action is truly vast — one can list a series of processes and activities that could be improved, modified, or even eliminated through the use of new technologies; the perhaps less bright side of this proposal is the human, infrastructure, and resource costs it entails.

Implementing AI in many processes would resolve a good part of the issues related to cumbersome bureaucracy, security problems in facilities, bidding processes, and purchasing, among others; but developing an institutional AI policy also requires a strong ethical and legal component, bearing in mind that betting on emerging technologies can be innovative and effective, but can also create new challenges and problems in different areas.

University governance in the AI era

Two of the 21st-century trends have been developed conceptually and by definition: the first, governance, characterized by its focus on government and management, which unfolds within the framework of New Public Management and which is taking shape in public administrations as an effective and efficient form of government that integrates transparency, accountability, and horizontal or bottom-up decision-making that includes a greater number of stakeholders. The second trend, which shares with the first the fact that it is widely accepted and has a generic definition in the collective imagination but lacks a universally agreed-upon definition, is the use of Artificial Intelligence.

In this section we will draw on the concept of university governance (Kaplin and Lee, 2007; Acosta, 2017) to analyze it from the standpoint of AI, the changes it represents, and its potential uses for improving the efficiency and quality of management and administration. Governance is understood as an approach or style of government characterized by the various institutional arrangements made within a higher education institution to achieve its objectives; these arrangements take place within university governing bodies (governing boards or general councils) in specific organizational structures that follow a hierarchical order of authority for decision-making and the determination of institutional policies.

Selecting leadership and governing bodies: algorithmic governance

New technologies based on generative and prescriptive artificial intelligence offer a new perspective and an interesting number of possibilities for their application in university government; one of these is in the selection of leadership. Imagine a hypothetical future in which algorithms analyze enormous amounts of data on candidates and those interested in competing for the position and, based on their performance and other "adjustable" criteria, produce a set of profile options, or even the person who fits the ideal profile for the position.

From electing the rector, members of the governing councils or boards, to senior officials, positions would be assigned based on transparent and justifiable criteria. One might think that this would truly fulfill the premises of meritocracy; nonetheless, it would raise a series of new problems, because — what would happen if none of the candidates meet the desired profile and those who do are not interested in the position? This would be the least of the problems if we were to consider only the advantages of AI, but the ethical issues surrounding the use of personal data and the government (or control) of the algorithms are key factors for establishing a good level of governability and trustworthiness in the selection process and its results.

Once officials have been elected and taken office, the exercise of power begins — the government of the university, its management, and its administration; for this, information technologies (ICT) are already a great ally that universities are already familiar with: the use of computer systems for school records and the efficiency of administrative procedures, which still has many areas of opportunity in which AI or some other technological tool could be leveraged.

The exercise of governing from a governance approach requires acceptable levels of governability, since governability can exist without governance, but not governance without governability (Acosta, 2021), and here artificial intelligence has much to contribute, given a sufficient amount of reliable data, to identifying the specific problems of each educational institution and designing proposals (based on success stories) tailored to the particular characteristics of each university (size, geolocation, autonomy, type of student and academic population, performance data, etc.).

For AI tools to effectively support decision-making, there must be enough reliable data, a computing infrastructure to manage it, and sufficient human and financial resources — a combination that is not always available to most HEIs, which already have budget shortfalls or deficiencies, and which would be impossible for those in financial crisis.

The great promise of this technology, according to Innerarity (2021), is that it makes it possible to know people's real will. Algorithmic governance shapes a kind of social sensorium that makes it possible to personalize not only learning and curricular content, but also university matters, and the ideal solutions to institutional problems, all thanks to data engineering that allows for a better and greater understanding of individual interactions and the analysis of causal relationships. Consider a system capable of processing all the information from institutional development plans, statistics, indicator results, and other documents, in order to automatically allocate a budget in line with each department's capabilities and objectives, generate strategies to solve educational and administrative problems, and even anticipate future problematic situations.

The incorporation of generative and predictive technologies into HEIs' governing processes poses significant challenges for public universities. From a governance standpoint, AI represents a series of opportunities for improvement that must be approached with attention and caution; its growing presence in university research, teaching, and management requires careful study and an adaptation of decision-making processes and governing structures.

Challenges (and opportunities) for HEI governance

There are five main challenges identified for university governance: political, regulatory, resource-related, infrastructure-related, and social and trust-related. Each of these represents an analytical approach and is made up of a series of factors or elements to consider in the processes of adaptation for the use and implementation of AI.

1. Politics: arenas of power and control of the algorithms

In politics, the integration of AI presents a promising horizon full of opportunities; it has the potential to revolutionize the way campaigns are run, representatives are elected, and decisions are made. The use of algorithms and machine learning makes it possible to identify and predict complex patterns, providing arguments for choosing one option over another.

The main challenge for its integration into HEIs' governing and decision-making processes lies in establishing solid ethical protocols for its use. Institutional policies and regulations must ensure the protection of data privacy, as well as ethical and transparent methods or methodologies for obtaining and processing information. At the core of this challenge lie the legitimacy and consensus achieved to: a) establish the AI regulatory policy, and b) build legitimacy and trust in institutional policymakers and, above all, in those who control and manage the algorithms. Adequate oversight through ethics committees and transparency mechanisms will be essential to prevent abuse of the technology and to protect the rights of the university community.

2. Institutional regulatory framework

The use of algorithms and intelligent systems in governing processes does not occur in a vacuum; their use is present in classrooms and used as an auxiliary tool in research and teaching, but when it comes to governing from a governance standpoint, it is vitally important to incorporate the corresponding regulation on the topic into the institution's legal framework. That is, the regulation of AI will need to be studied and analyzed within state higher-education laws and within universities' own internal statutes and regulations. This is because it is a technology that evolves very rapidly and that is used for different processes every day; universities can draw on their critical mass to generate guidelines that will be most useful and beneficial for their own community, or even serve as a reference for laws and regulations discussed at the federal level, given the recent bill for a Ley de Regulación Ética de la Inteligencia Artificial y la Robótica (Law on the Ethical Regulation of Artificial Intelligence and Robotics) (Cámara de Diputados, 2023).

The main challenge under this criterion lies in achieving the consensus and institutional agreements needed to legally regulate a constantly evolving system, and in ensuring that these same regulations do not, in the future, become straitjackets that inhibit or hinder taking advantage of the potential of algorithms and mathematical models in decision-making regarding the university's substantive functions.

3. Human and economic resources

One of the main constraints on the use of emerging technologies in university administrative processes is the significant financial investment required to study and implement AI. It is possible to work on conceiving and creating prototypes; nonetheless, developing the tool and generating an applicable system requires, in addition to time, financial resources that public universities can hardly afford. Funding AI research is already costly in itself; funding the implementation and development of systems to put its use into practice can be even more so.

One option is to seek funding from industry by applying to calls such as the one Google opened in 2023, the Digital Futures Project, which offers financial support to universities and nonprofit organizations to develop research ideas that use AI and other technologies to solve problems. This funding covers only research development; implementation, training, and operation costs are added on top of that.

The second element to consider for AI research and implementation is human resources. Developing systems that use this tool requires not only researchers, but also highly trained programmers and developers across all the practical and technical aspects its development and operation require. As universities are places of teaching, they are moving to create degree programs, courses, and graduate programs providing training in mathematics, programming, robotics, data science, and everything related to the study of mathematical models for the design and programming of artificial intelligence systems. It will be these professionals who will work collaboratively in multidisciplinary teams to apply their knowledge in the field of higher education management.

4. Technological infrastructure: execution and operation

To implement AI projects, universities will have to invest in physical and virtual infrastructure; this will require an initial assessment of what the institution already has and what will be needed for its development and operation. The main challenge lies in flexibility and adaptability; this need for flexibility makes the cloud, particularly the hybrid cloud, the foundation of AI, especially given the need to store large amounts of data. There are elements to consider when analyzing the infrastructure capacity universities have: a) computing performance: high-speed, high-performance machines and processors for carrying out calculations; b) storage capacity: the ability to have storage space that can scale its volume as data increases; c) cybersecurity — AI involves handling confidential and sensitive data, and having tools that help protect against and prevent theft or loss of information is a key factor for trust in the algorithms and their results.

5. Reluctance to change and distrust

On their less bright side, universities are characterized as bureaucratic institutions that evolve slowly. This is reflected in outdated curricular content, traditional teaching methods, and how cumbersome administrative and school-records procedures can be. Being considered loosely coupled systems (Weick, 1976) — a term referring to the degree to which subunits are connected and interrelated — HEIs possess, at the same time, the potential for disruptive innovation and the weight of inertia, which translates into a reluctance and resistance to change. It is this latter factor that represents one of the greatest challenges for the use of artificial intelligence in highly monitored and audited processes, such as rector elections and budget allocation.

Additionally, the excitement over the promise of innovation and efficiency is also cause for concern regarding trust in its use, since it is unclear who governs or controls the algorithms, and growing concern arises due to notorious cases of biased, discriminatory, manipulative, illegal, or human-rights-violating use of AI (Vilariño, 2023). The University of Queensland, Australia, in collaboration with the consulting firm KPMG, published the study Trust in Artificial Intelligence in 2023 (Gillespie, Curtis, Pool, and Akbari, 2023), which examines in depth public trust and attitudes toward the use of AI, and expectations regarding the management and governance of the technology in 17 countries. This study shows that most people accept the use of AI to automate routine tasks, but not for human resource management. Among the countries most willing to trust AI are India, China, and Brazil, while the countries with the least trust are Finland, Japan, Estonia, the United Kingdom, and Canada.

It is interesting to note that the countries with the lowest willingness to trust the use of AI are, in turn, those that are benchmarks in higher education, whose universities rank among the top positions in international rankings. It is worth noting that, according to the study mentioned, citizens' trust in the development, use, and governance of AI leans toward universities and research institutions (76-82%), while government and industry generate less trust. (Gillespie, Curtis, Pool, and Akbari, 2023, p. 6)

In the Mexican context, the main challenge will be to socialize and disseminate the potential uses of artificial intelligence from an ethical and transparent standpoint in order to build the trust needed in its use, and, particularly in universities, it will be necessary to build literacy around the logic and functioning of this tool, in order to demystify its use, avoid falling into exaggeration, and address the outright negativity that stems from fear of being displaced or replaced by machines.

Conclusions

University governance faces a profound transformation with the introduction of artificial intelligence; it has not only modified teaching methods and pedagogical practices, but today represents a tool with enormous potential for improvement, effectiveness, and the achievement of objectives. Studying and understanding these challenges requires a collaborative, proactive approach involving all stakeholders, both within and outside the university community.

Each of the five points presented as challenges (Politics: arenas of power and control of the algorithms; Institutional regulatory framework; Human and economic resources; Technological infrastructure; and Reluctance to change and distrust) also represents a window of opportunity for strengthening government and governance. Universities are facing a positive scenario if they take advantage of technological trends and foster research and application through ethical institutional policies that promote transparency and the ongoing review of practices and structures. Despite the challenges, the integration of AI opens significant opportunities for automating administrative tasks, managing procedures, and, above all, for decision-making. Governance can turn these challenges into opportunities to drive academic excellence and improve performance in the functions of research, teaching, and outreach. The key to success lies in institutions' management capacity to capitalize on the human resources available to them, obtain and allocate financial resources for building theoretical and applied research teams, within a framework of institutional policies and regulations that govern the ethical and transparent use of emerging technologies.
