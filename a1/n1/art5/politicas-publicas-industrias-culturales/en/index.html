The influence of public policies on cultural and creative industries:
an exploration of bibliometric maps

La influencia de las políticas públicas en las industrias culturales y creativas: exploración de mapas bibliométricos

Abstract.

The cultural and creative industries (CCI) as a subject of study have gained visibility in recent years, which has increased the interest of researchers in this sector. This paper seeks to identify how CCIs have been studied and where the lines of research on CCIs are heading in relation to public policies. A bibliometric analysis was carried out using the Scopus database as a source of information where the following keywords “cultural and creative industries”, “cultural policy”, “public policy”, “policy” and “government” were used for the search; the results were processed in the VOSviewer software. It was identified that the lines of research related to CCIs and public policy are still in development, since 11 different clusters were found with very few items each.

Keywords:

Cultural and creatives industries; Government; Cultural policy; Public policy.

Introduction

Since the beginning of the 21st century, the concept of cultural and creative industries (CCI) has begun to be heard more worldwide, as it has been presented as part of an economic reactivation strategy in post-industrial countries. UNESCO has been one of the main promoters of this term, as it has been responsible for developing CCIs through the promotion of cultural heritage (Ma, 2022).

Given the above, UNESCO proposes defining cultural and creative industries as those "sectors of activity whose primary purpose is the creation, production or reproduction, promotion, distribution, and commercialization of goods, services, and activities with cultural, artistic, or heritage content" (UNESCO, 2010).

Culture is reflected through the artistic expressions of a community; these can be tangible or intangible, where these expressions take place in what is now called cultural and creative industries, represented in music, colors, and various forms that are accessible to many people and define a community's identity (UNESCO, 2010).

CCIs have become a very important sector for governments, as it has been pointed out that these industries contribute to the economic development of a country or region, adding that they have helped develop the digital economy, as well as promoting innovation, inclusion, and sustainability (Jia et al., 2024).

Cultural and creative industries have gained relevance in recent years as they have begun to be viewed as an economic sector that contributes to national GDPs, contributing 3.1% to global GDP and generating 6.2% of all employment. This has led these industries to be increasingly integrated into government public policies, although it is noted that public investment in culture has decreased in the last decade and policy priorities have focused only on certain CCI sectors such as cinema, visual arts, music, and performing arts, paying less attention to media arts and design (UNESCO, 2022).

Public policies and cultural and creative industries

Public policies are the way in which the state addresses public interest problems. In the past, their development was only the responsibility of state and federal levels of government, but recently non-governmental actors such as civil associations, business owners, professionals, academics, among others, have been included (Ziccardi, 2008). In the same sense, a public policy reflects how a country participates in geopolitical contexts and how it addresses recommendations from international organizations (Bouquillion & Ithurbide, 2023).

Thanks to these policies, creative and cultural industries have achieved growth in recent years, as they have supported this sector through subsidies, regulations, and regional and national standardization (Liu, 2021).

Among public policies addressing the cultural sector are so-called cultural policies, understood as the activities carried out by a government in favor of the arts, which may include both for-profit and non-profit initiatives. In these, the government establishes action plans where strategies to follow are determined; some of these actions may include the production, dissemination, commercialization, and consumption of cultural products (Betzler et al., 2020).

The study of cultural policies has grown along with interest in this sector, where it has been shown that governments previously focused on CCIs purely for reasons of meeting cultural needs as a public good, offering citizens a cultural and artistic supply. However, this has been changing, and now CCIs are seen as an element of economic development, tourism, and employment. Therefore, studies point out that the objectives of cultural policies will depend on the preferences of the state or nation (Betzler et al., 2020).

Due to the importance that cultural and creative industries have gained worldwide, countries have paid more attention to them and have begun to promote public policies that favor their development, to the point that some countries have positioned them as a pillar of industrial development. To this, we add that emerging countries have increased their consumption of these industries, increasing purchasing power globally. Likewise, CCIs help not only consumers but also the development of emerging industries, enhancing economic competitiveness (Liu & Chiu, 2017).

Therefore, it is noted that the most prosperous economies in the 21st century will be those that utilize creativity. Thus, it is relevant for government public policies to support culture, but for this to happen, they must first clarify certain points regarding CCIs, as there is still a diversity of opinions without reaching a consensus. Consequently, in the first instance, both policymakers and academics must clarify what the generalized definition for culture and creativity will be, as well as understand that CCI creativity differs from other types of creativity (Galloway & Dunlop, 2007).

On the other hand, McGuigan (as cited in Lysgård, 2016) points out that cultural policies can be explained from three ideological discourses:

  • State or Government: in this cultural policy approach, culture is focused on being something for society, a right.

  • Commercial market: in this second approach, cultural policy is based on relating culture to commercialization, grounded in a more economic aspect resulting in the commercialization of goods and services.

  • Democratic civil society: in this third approach, cultural policy seeks a middle ground between the state and the market.

Given the momentum cultural and creative industries are experiencing in national governments, this moment should be used to create environments where they can develop. For this, a legal, political, and regulatory framework that supports them is necessary (UNESCO, 2022).

Some countries with advanced and post-industrial economies have seen cultural and creative industries as a sector capable of providing a strategic advantage. Consequently, they have worked to generate policies that assist in the development of this sector. Therefore, a policy must promote CCIs and create an instrument that aids in their dissemination, as well as ensuring their sustainability (UNESCO, 2010).

Methodology

The database used for this work was Scopus. The following search strategy was established: Article title, abstract and keywords (“cultural and creatives industries” AND “cultural policy” OR “public policy” OR “policy” OR "Government"). This search equation was selected in order to search for the aforementioned keywords in the article title, abstract, and keywords. This yielded 333 documents, including all types of documents without applying any specific filter, so that all results were returned without discrimination.

Data processing was conducted using VOSviewer software, where information downloaded from Scopus in CSV format was uploaded. The construction of bibliometric maps was carried out based on the co-occurrence of author keywords to create networks of keywords found in the articles and determine research trends regarding cultural and creative industries and policy.

For this study, author keyword co-occurrence was chosen as the type of analysis, taking author keywords as the unit of analysis, which yielded 972 keywords. It was determined that keywords should have a minimum of 3 occurrences, resulting in 45 keywords meeting this condition.

Results

Through the bibliometric analysis carried out, map results were obtained showing the keywords used by authors with the highest co-occurrence. In these, we can observe the most studied keywords in relation to the topic proposed in this work, as well as an overlay map showing how keyword research has shifted over the years. In Figure 1, all keywords with the highest repetition in the analyzed documents are shown, grouped into 11 clusters explained below:

  • Cluster 1: Cluster 1 shown on the bibliometric map is one of the two largest, consisting of 6 items where the author keywords with the strongest link strength are: creative industry, cultural and creative products, cultural industry, employment, policy, South Africa.

  • Cluster 2: In the second largest cluster, 6 items were grouped: COVID-19 pandemic, creativity, cultural industries, culture, entrepreneurship, and soft power.

  • Cluster 3: In this cluster of most frequently used keywords by researchers, variables such as: COVID-19, cultural and creative industries (ccis), cultural policy, and Italy can be observed.

  • Cluster 4: The fourth cluster shown in the keyword network map consists of four keywords: cultural and creative industry, cultural consumption, regional development, and sustainable development.

  • Cluster 5: In the fifth cluster, keywords such as: cultural and creative industries, cultural creative industries, Fritz Machlup, and input-output table can be seen.

  • Cluster 6: The sixth cluster shown in Figure 1 consists of the following keywords: creative industries, diversity, innovation, and intellectual property.

  • Cluster 7: In Cluster 7, the following keywords can be seen: creative city, economic development, Taiwan, and urban governance.

  • Cluster 8: The eighth cluster consists of the following author keywords: China, creative cities, urban development, and policy.

  • Cluster 9: In Cluster 9, the number of keywords decreases, consisting of only 3 keywords: creative economy, higher education, and sustainability.

  • Cluster 10: The tenth cluster has 3 items: cultural policies, globalization, and public policies.

  • Cluster 11: In the last and smallest cluster, only two keywords are included: creative class and Hangzhou.

In Figure 2, it can be observed how over the years researchers have studied different keywords. In the early years of study, the overlay map shows authors focusing their research on cultural and creative industries and the economic development they could represent, specifically relating it to Taiwan. Then, from approximately 2016 to 2019, research shifted toward public policy, cultural policy, urban development, and cities began to be referred to as creative cities, with China and Italy appearing as relevant countries in the articles. Finally, from 2020 to date, studies have been linked to cultural and creative industries and COVID-19, innovation, sustainable development, entrepreneurship, education level, and South Africa appeared in research.

Table 1 shows the 15 keywords with the highest number of co-occurrences generated by VOSviewer. To obtain this result, different criteria were defined: the first was counting keywords used by article authors, and selecting those with a minimum repetition threshold of ≥3, where out of 972 keywords, 45 matched more than three times. From the above, it can be noted that the keywords with highest co-occurrence and link strength were primarily those related to the article search terms in Scopus (creative and cultural industries). Therefore, terms directly related to CCI were excluded from this table, highlighting only non-CCI terms, where main keywords include cultural policy, innovation, sustainable development, China, Fritz Machlup, policy, creative cities, intellectual property, economic development, among others.

Based on the above, it can be summarized that trends in the most used keywords in research related to cultural and creative industries and policy are those addressing cultural policies and other types of policies that foster development across various domains (economic, sustainable, regional, urban, among others).

Conclusions

Cultural and creative industries have gained significant relevance in recent times, with post-industrial countries bringing them into global focus. They have proven capable of driving development in many areas, with economic growth being a primary interest for governments.

Consequently, governments have set out to create ideal environments for CCIs to grow and continue developing, establishing cultural policies and legal frameworks that make them visible and recognized. However, this topic is still in its infancy; while CCIs have been recognized as a sector capable of generating revenue and development for a country, the next step is providing all necessary elements for them to become competitive and remain a pillar for nations, which is where the development of public policies surrounding them comes into play.

The clusters described in the results section provided an overview of how study topics related to cultural and creative industries and public policies are grouped. This revealed that several lines of research are still under study. Based on VOSviewer findings from Scopus articles, this field appears to still be in formation, given the presence of numerous clusters with very few items each.

To summarize, the 11 clusters described above were regrouped into four major clusters according to their number of items as follows:

Research line of Clusters 1 and 2: these clusters contain the most consolidated lines of research, each with 6 items. These relate cultural and creative industries to policy, employment, entrepreneurship, and how a country can influence another through culture (also known as soft power).

Research line of Clusters 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8: this research line groups the largest number of clusters, each with 4 items. These link cultural and creative industries to national development from various perspectives supported by policies, as well as connecting this sector to innovation, intellectual property, and introducing the concept of creative cities, while regions such as China and Taiwan gain prominence.

Research line of Clusters 9 and 10: this line of research is less studied than the previous ones. Here, the most studied keywords lean toward a creative economy rather than cultural and creative industries specifically, focusing more on public policy, higher education, and addressing sustainability.

Research line of Cluster 11: this final line of research is just emerging, as it contains only two keywords, one being very specific: studying the creative class in relation to a region in China called Hangzhou.

With all of the above, it can be concluded that research lines on cultural and creative industries in relation to public policies are still forming. However, it can also be observed that this sector is increasingly recognized for its multiple benefits to national development. Furthermore, the concept of CCI may evolve into simply "creative industries", potentially leading to a consensus regarding its definition. It should be highlighted that Asian and European regions appear most frequently in research with high keyword co-occurrence.
