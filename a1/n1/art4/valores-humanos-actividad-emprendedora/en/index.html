Human values and entrepreneurial activity:
Theoretical and conceptual reflections

Valores humanos y actividad emprendedora: Reflexiones teórico-conceptuales

Abstract.

This paper aims to offer a theoretical-conceptual analysis about the role that the personality of the subjects, explained from the perspective of human values, functions as a predictor of entrepreneurial activity in different regions of the world. Through a literature review, the main concepts about human values are grouped, as well as the theories that have given way to the explanation of behavior through them, and an analysis is made with the literature related to entrepreneurial activity. The literature review yields about twenty conceptual references and three theories from which the reflective analysis is carried out. It is concluded by recognizing the role played by human values, not only in the entrepreneurial intention or activity of the subjects, but also in the choice of the type of entrepreneurship to be carried out, from the perspective of the individualistic and/or collectivist orientation of their personal values.

Keywords:

Human values; Personal values; Entrepreneurship; Individualism; Collectivism.

Introduction

Firm creation is a phenomenon that draws attention from various profiles (academics and researchers) as an area of analysis, as it encompasses explanatory factors of the process in economic, political, and social spheres. The interest in analyzing business creation stems from the favorable effects it brings, ranging from increased productivity and job creation to the development of the sector or industry in which entrepreneurship takes place, even fostering innovation and development (Carree & Thurik, 2005), although it must be noted that it does not behave in the same manner across all regions. Hence, enterprises have become a core component of national development strategies, driven by the knowledge economy, communication technologies, and the opening of new markets (Stough et al., 2014).

This field of study (entrepreneurship) has continually gained importance among researchers over the past decade. However, after more than 30 years of research, literature still faces difficulties in defining fundamental terms such as entrepreneur and entrepreneurship (Gartner, 1988; Bygrave & Hofer, 1991; Rosa & Bowes, 1993). Entrepreneurship cannot be considered merely as an isolated action, but rather as an entire process involving distinct stages; and this process is not purely economic in nature, but feeds on the social context that shapes and configures business outcomes (Jack & Anderson, 2001). Thus, the process is carried out by individuals, and studying the individuals engaging in these activities is as crucial as studying the process itself. It would be nearly impossible to comprehend the entrepreneurial process without examining the individuals executing it.

In academic literature, various phenomena related to personal characteristics defining the entrepreneur have been studied; traits such as skills, talent, and knowledge that influence the potential success or failure of new ventures (Brandstätter, 2011; Sirec & Mocnik, 2012; Ahmadkhani et al., 2013; Xie, 2014).

Values hold a prominent place in scientific and public discourse across various levels (Meglino & Ravlin, 1998). They rank "among the very few social psychological concepts that have been successfully employed across all disciplines in the social sciences" (Rokeach & Ball-Rokeach, 1989, p. 775). However, limited research has examined the role that personal values play in the broader conception of entrepreneurship.

Liñán (2008) states that when individuals make complex decisions requiring the development of plans, values play a relevant role. The higher the priority assigned to a value, the greater the likelihood that individuals form action plans leading to its expression in behavior.

Individuals exhibit marked behavioral differences regarding their decisions to create and sustain entrepreneurial activity, and prior research has emphasized the importance of analyzing not only the social context of the entrepreneur but also individual aspects; the nature of the entrepreneur remains diffuse, and the lack of unequivocal research findings on this topic is widely acknowledged (Bouchikhi, 1993; Chell, 1985; Naffziger, 1995; Shaver & Scott, 1991).

Literature Review

We find that the concept of entrepreneurship has been approached from diverse perspectives; some authors conceive entrepreneurship as a role and a set of behaviors (Gartner, 1988) or as a set of knowledge-related competencies (Bird, 1995), while other studies mainly analyze the cognitive aspects of complex models of entrepreneurial decision-making (Bird, 1988; Krueger & Carsrud, 1993). According to this view, individual cognitive aspects play a key role in the different phases of the entrepreneurial process. Whether the goal of entrepreneurship is to obtain financial rewards or any other form of value, the process remains basically the same: alertness leads to recognition and subsequently to the exploitation of opportunities, followed by decisions regarding growth (Bygrave & Hofer, 1991; Venkataraman, 1997).

The role of values in decision-making has been a source of considerable interest in psychology (Bardi, Lee, Hofmann-Towfigh, & Soutar, 2009; Rohan, 2000; Schwartz & Boehnke, 2004) and cross-cultural studies (e.g., Hofstede, 1980; Schwartz, 1999), but insufficient research exists on how personal values, as opposed to cultural values, influence the decision-making of entrepreneurs or potential entrepreneurs (Holland & Shepherd, 2013). In this sense, cultural-level theories are more appropriate for research questions examining the effect of culture on group and societal structures (Bardi et al., 2008; Holland & Shepherd, 2013). However, the values an individual possesses influence how situations are defined, alternatives are considered, and ultimately, how a course of action is chosen (Holland & Shepherd, 2013).

Furthermore, although current studies suggest that values affect operational practices, work is needed to better understand the linkages between values and levels of entrepreneurial activity (Morris & Schindehutte, 2005).

The primary purpose of this paper is to offer a significant literature review on research conducted regarding personal values in the domain of entrepreneurship. This document primarily follows the work of psychologist Shalom Schwartz, one of the most prominent researchers regarding personal values. A substantial volume of academic publications was compiled to provide an approximation of the state of the art on this subject.

Origin and Conceptualization of Values

Values today represent one of the main fields of study across diverse disciplines such as sociology, anthropology, philosophy, and many other sciences. The discussion regarding what is good or bad, just or unjust, legitimate or illegitimate, expresses clear beliefs about human existence. The fact that values differ from one culture to another and from century to century raises many questions (Boudon, 2003).

Values are representations of the answers that individuals and social groups must provide in response to three universal requirements: biological needs of subjects, coordination of social actions, and the proper functioning and survival of groups. In this way, people and groups transform their needs, expressing them in terms of specific values to adapt to a concrete sociocultural reality (García, Lezcano, & Muñoz, 2017).

Furthermore, the origin of human values derives from the Latin word meaning estimable; since early history, human values were defined with the term absolutism due to how human beings were treated. An important stage in the creation of this concept also appears in the thought of the insensitives, who defined human values as something lacking higher hierarchy.

Moreover, history demonstrates that, on one hand, values are mutable and, on the other hand, this discussion surrounding the source of values is very ancient. From Ancient Greece to Kant, there have been numerous attempts to derive morality from non-absolutist or supernatural sources. Kantian morality, based on "obedience to rules," is not fully identifiable with absolutism; rather, behind Kant's vision lies the desire for the universality of human values (Vidal, 2013).

At the end of the 19th century, the issue regarding the material ethics of values emerged and developed throughout the first third of the 20th century. Delving into this topic involves not only studying a new philosophical current, but raising a question that leads to a new reflection, even when this topic was approached from idealism and empiricism, understanding that it presents itself as a fundamental question in practical philosophy (Medina, 2007).

For both Durkheim (1897/1964) and Weber (1905/1958), values were crucial to explaining social and personal organization and change. Values have played an important role not only in sociology, but also in psychology, anthropology, and related disciplines. Values are used to characterize cultural groups, societies, and individuals, to trace change over time, and to explain the motivational foundations of attitudes and behavior. The application of the values construct in the social sciences during the past century suffered from the absence of a consensus conception of basic values, the content and structure of relations among these values, and reliable empirical methods to measure them (Hitlin & Piliavin, 2004; Rohan, 2000). Recent theoretical and methodological advances (Schwartz, 1992; Smith & Schwartz, 1997) have led to a resurgence in values research.

One of the first authors to define values was Kluckhohn (1951), who argued that "a value is a conception, explicit or implicit, distinctive of an individual or characteristic of a group, of the desirable which inﬂuences the selection from available modes, means, and ends of action" (p. 395). A few years later, Rokeach proposed a belief systems model in which beliefs, attitudes, and values are clearly differentiated (Rokeach, 1973). Thus, the value construct was restricted to that special class of enduring beliefs concerning modes of conduct and end-states of existence that transcend specific objects and situations and are personally and socially preferable to an opposite mode of conduct or end-state of existence. For the first time, the realm of values was clearly differentiated from that of other beliefs and attitudes (Braithwaite & Law, 1985).

Specifically, Rokeach (1973) defines values as "an enduring belief that a specific mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct or end-state of existence" (as cited in Schwartz, 1999, p. 24). Likewise, he defines values as "conceptions of the desirable that guide the way social actors [...] select actions and evaluate other people and events"; in this sense, values are criteria or goals ordered by importance that serve as guiding principles in life.

Dolan et al. (2006) argue that values "are strategic lessons, maintained and relatively stable over time, [that] teach us that one way of acting is better than its opposite to achieve a desired outcome" (p. 28).

Furthermore, various other interpretations and meanings of the value concept exist (see Table 1), alongside classifications by prominent authors in this field of knowledge (Valbuena et al., 2012).

Connecting the descriptive concepts of the selected value, 6 perspectives can be identified for conceptualizing values:

According to Schwartz (2005), values are beliefs inextricably bound to affect. When values are activated, they become infused with feeling.

1. Values refer to desirable goals that motivate action.

2. Values transcend specific actions and situations. This feature distinguishes values from norms and attitudes, which usually refer to specific actions, objects, or situations.

3. Values serve as standards or criteria. Values guide the selection or evaluation of actions, policies, people, and events. People decide what is good or bad, justified or illegitimate, worth doing or avoiding based on potential consequences for their cherished values. However, the impact of values on daily decisions is rarely conscious. Values enter awareness when actions or judgments under consideration have conflicting implications for different values one holds dear.

4. Values are ordered by relative importance. People's values form an ordered system of priorities that characterize them as individuals.

5. The relative importance of multiple values guides action. Any attitude or behavior typically has implications for more than one value. Values influence action when they are relevant in context (and thus liable to activation) and important to the actor.

Research on Individual Values

Despite the popularity of personal values in literature, there is a lack of consensus regarding the nature of values themselves. Indeed, the word values lends itself to misuse and overuse by society, with people generally assigning whatever meaning they desire (Rohan, 2000).

Among other things, values have been viewed as needs, personality types, motivations, goals, utilities, attitudes, interests, or non-existent mental entities (Meglino & Ravlin, 1998; Hitlin & Piliavin, 2004). Ling et al. (2007), for instance, before analyzing the influence of CEO values on performance, use an example to distinguish between personality and values. They argue that an individual may demonstrate the personality trait of introversion (being somewhat withdrawn and shy in social contexts) while simultaneously holding a value that considers being friendly and sociable highly desirable (a value).

This illustrates how many authors must painstakingly define what a value means to avoid confusion with other constructs. In this regard, Hechter (1993) states that the study of values involves four impediments: (a) Values are unobservable, (b) current theories provide little guidance on how values shape behavior, (c) behavioral explanations are unconvincing when the process generating values is unknown, and (d) severe challenges exist regarding value measurement.

When thinking of our values, we think of what is important to us in life. Each of us holds numerous values (e.g., achievement, security, benevolence) with varying degrees of importance. A specific value may be crucial for one person but unimportant for another. The theory of values (Schwartz, 2006) adopts a conception specifying six main features implicit in the writings of many theorists: (1) Values are beliefs inextricably bound to affect. When values are activated, they become infused with feelings. People for whom independence is an important value become aroused if independence is threatened, despair when unable to protect it, and rejoice when able to enjoy it. (2) Values refer to desirable goals motivating action. People for whom social order, justice, and helpfulness are important values are motivated to pursue these goals.

From the theoretical framework of values, Schwartz grounded his value theory in Milton Rokeach's conceptual framework (1973). Rokeach defined a value as "an enduring belief that a specific mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct or end-state of existence" (p. 5) and explained that human values continuously evolve over time, rather than persisting as static personality traits (Simón et al., 2017).

The more recent theory refers to basic values recognized across all cultures. It identifies ten motivationally distinct value types and specifies dynamic relations among them. Certain values conflict with one another (e.g., benevolence versus power), whereas others are compatible (e.g., conformity and security). The "structure" of values refers to these conflictual and congruent relationships. Values are structured similarly across culturally diverse groups, suggesting a universal organization of human motivations. Although the nature and structure of values may be universal, individuals and groups differ substantially in the relative importance assigned to values. That is, individuals and groups possess different value "priorities" or "hierarchies."

Schwartz (1996) considers 10 motivational values: power, achievement, hedonism, stimulation, self-direction, universalism, benevolence, tradition, conformity, and security, related to the goals they target and representative values (Table 2).

The ten values can be reproduced across highly diverse cultures and groups and derive from three universal human requirements (Schwartz, 2004): individual biological needs, coordinated social interaction requirements, and group survival/welfare needs. Schwartz (1992) explains his model through interactions among values. All these relations can be synthesized into a two-dimensional structure composed of four higher-order types (Figure 1), combining different value categories:

1. Self-Transcendence: universalism and benevolence.

2. Self-Enhancement: power and achievement.

3. Conservation: security, conformity, and tradition.

4. Openness to Change: stimulation and self-direction.

Personal Values in Entrepreneurship

The term entrepreneur originated from the French "entrepreneur" and was led by Richard Cantillon in 1755 in his work "Essai sur la Nature du Commerce en Général," where he defined the entrepreneur as "someone who assumes risks to obtain future profits."

Achieving entrepreneurial success across any dimension is affected by various decisive factors, including entrepreneur-specific factors determined by the personal characteristics of decision-makers within a firm.

Thus, understanding values favoring success proves highly relevant at the onset of entrepreneurial activity. Recognizing necessary values facilitates initial self-assessment of personal strengths and weaknesses, enabling entrepreneurs to mitigate weaknesses and capitalize on strengths, thereby navigating entrepreneurial uncertainty.

Individualism, Collectivism, and Types of Entrepreneurship

The term individualism refers to values manifesting emotional independence from groups and organizations. Conversely, collectivism is associated with emotional dependence on groups with which an individual identifies and belongs (Morales et al., 1996).

Furthermore, the prevalence of an individualistic value hierarchy influences entrepreneurial intention. Several studies indicate that entrepreneurs demonstrate a stronger orientation toward individualistic values (Fageson, 1993; Holt, 1997; Thomas & Mueller, 2000). They note that "individualism" reflects the degree to which personal independence and individual needs are emphasized over collectivism, which prioritizes group needs. According to this hypothesis, individualistic cultural patterns align closely with business creation, as individuals seek goals independently without relying on group support. However, relationships between individualism and entrepreneurial behavior remain partially unproven.

The interplay between human values and entrepreneurship has generated growing academic interest. Specifically, the relationship between individualistic values and traditional entrepreneurship has been a central topic of debate and analysis.

Individualistic values focus on autonomy, freedom, and personal fulfillment. According to Hofstede (2001), cultures with high individualism value independence, personal initiative, and self-determination. These values translate into stronger emphasis on innovation, creativity, and risk-taking within business environments (Shane, 2010).

Traditional entrepreneurship involves establishing and managing small and medium-sized enterprises operating within conventional economic sectors. These ventures frequently reflect the values and aspirations of founders. Individualistic values play a key role in launching and developing such firms, as entrepreneurs seek the autonomy provided by business ownership. These values also shape strategic decision-making and approaches to business challenges (Rauch & Frese, 2007).

While individualistic values spur innovation and risk-taking, they can present challenges for long-term venture success. Excessive autonomy and lack of collaboration may hinder adaptation to market shifts and growth opportunities (Acs & Szerb, 2007). Moreover, an exclusive focus on personal achievement may complicate building strong relationships with employees, clients, and business partners, which are essential for firm sustainability (Zahra et al., 2009).

The relationship between individualistic values and traditional entrepreneurship is complex and multifaceted. While individualistic values motivate and guide entrepreneurs during venture creation and management, they may introduce significant hurdles regarding collaboration, adaptability, and long-term sustainability. Recognizing both potential and limits allows entrepreneurs to balance these values effectively.

On the other hand, social entrepreneurship has emerged as an innovative approach to addressing social and environmental challenges through business principles. In this context, the connection between collectivist values and social entrepreneurship has attracted increasing academic interest.

Collectivist values focus on interdependence, solidarity, and common welfare. According to Hofstede (1980), high-collectivism cultures prioritize group belonging, collaboration, and community welfare over individual interests. These values provide a robust foundation for social entrepreneurship by encouraging concern for community well-being and collaborative problem-solving (Dacin et al., 2010).

Social entrepreneurship is characterized by ventures whose core mission is generating positive social or environmental impact. These ventures often originate within marginalized communities or address specific social challenges. Social entrepreneurs embracing collectivist values are driven to contribute to community welfare and engage local stakeholders in sustainable solutions (Mair & Martí, 2006). Collectivist values also influence organizational structure and operations, prioritizing community participation, transparency, and equitable benefit distribution (Battilana & Lee, 2014).

Although collectivist values strengthen the social mission and impact of social enterprises, they can introduce operational and sustainability challenges. Over-reliance on community consensus may impede agile decision-making and efficient strategy implementation (Dees, 2007). Prioritizing collective welfare may also involve financial sacrifices and lower profitability, potentially limiting scalability and impact expansion (Nicholls, 2010).

Conclusions

The relationship between human values and entrepreneurial activity is of paramount importance in academic and practical spheres. Human values—comprising beliefs, attitudes, and principles rooted in morality and ethics—play a crucial role in venture genesis, growth, and success.

Studying human values provides profound insights into entrepreneurs' motivations, choices, and behaviors. Values such as autonomy, innovation, ethics, and social responsibility directly shape how individuals perceive opportunities, assume risks, and navigate obstacles during the entrepreneurial process.

Moreover, understanding human values aids in designing more effective support policies and programs. Recognizing value diversity across society enables initiatives that resonate with individual aspirations, fostering an inclusive and dynamic entrepreneurial ecosystem.

Despite advances in understanding individualistic values in traditional entrepreneurship, further research is required. For example, investigating how individualistic values manifest across different venture lifecycle stages—and how these shifts affect performance—would yield valuable insights. Examining interactions between individualistic values and contextual factors (legal, cultural, and economic environments) will also enrich understanding.

In practical terms, findings encourage entrepreneurs to reflect on personal values and their influence on business decisions. Public policy and enterprise support programs can benefit by fostering environments that balance individual autonomy with collaboration and networking.

Recognizing the link between individualistic values and traditional entrepreneurship is essential for economic and social development. Interdisciplinary approaches will advance sustainable strategies for business growth.

Furthermore, the connection between collectivist values and social entrepreneurship is vital for understanding the social sector's dynamics. Collectivist values centered on solidarity and common good offer the ethical framework for social ventures combining social missions with financial viability.

However, collectivist values present distinct challenges, including resource constraints, diverse stakeholder interests, and balancing social mission with financial viability. Despite these hurdles, collectivist values enhance resilience and adaptability, enabling social enterprises to achieve lasting social impact.

Ultimately, the intersection of human values and entrepreneurial activity is essential for cultivating innovation, sustainable economic growth, and positive social change.
