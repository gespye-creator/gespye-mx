Abstract.

The social approach to public relations is based on the premise that this discipline cannot exist without democracy, as its legitimacy depends on citizen participation and institutional transparency. In this sense, public relations must transcend its technical and corporate dimension to become a critical, ethical, and community-oriented practice. The roles of the professional reflect different forms of interaction with society; some prioritize corporate interests and the status quo, while others promote social change, public debate, and mutual understanding. In Latin America, a distinct model is emerging, characterized by its social and ethical nature, closer to activism than to technical expertise, and integrating social responsibility, inclusion, and sustainability as central pillars. The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) adds a new component that can strengthen transparency, digital inclusion, and active listening, but it also poses risks such as algorithmic bias, information manipulation, and the dehumanization of communication. In conclusion, the social approach requires training professionals capable of being community leaders, ethical activists, and democratic mediators, especially in Latin American contexts where cultural diversity and structural inequalities demand intercultural sensitivity.

Keywords:

Public relations, social approach, Excellence theory, Community activism, Artificial intelligence

Resumen.

El enfoque social de las relaciones públicas se fundamenta en la premisa de que esta disciplina no puede existir sin democracia, pues su legitimidad depende de la participación ciudadana y la transparencia institucional. En este sentido, las relaciones públicas deben trascender su dimensión técnica y corporativa para convertirse en una práctica crítica, ética y comunitaria. Los roles del profesional reflejan distintas formas de interacción con la sociedad, algunos privilegian intereses corporativos y el statu quo, mientras que otros promueven el cambio social, el debate público y el entendimiento mutuo. En América Latina, se perfila un modelo propio caracterizado por ser social y ético, más cercano al activismo que a la técnica, integrando responsabilidad social, inclusión y sostenibilidad como ejes centrales. La irrupción de la inteligencia artificial (IA) añade un nuevo componente que puede fortalecer la transparencia, la inclusión digital y la escucha activa, pero también plantea riesgos como sesgos algorítmicos, manipulación informativa y deshumanización de la comunicación. En conclusión, el enfoque social exige formar profesionales capaces de ser líderes comunitarios, activistas éticos y mediadores democráticos, especialmente en contextos latinoamericanos donde la diversidad cultural y las desigualdades estructurales demandan sensibilidad intercultural.

Introduction

Public relations must redefine its role in a society marked by the automation of communication and the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI). Traditionally, the discipline has been torn between technical and corporate models such as press agentry or public information, and critical approaches that seek to strengthen democracy and public interest (Grunig & White, 1992; Holtzhausen, 2016). However, in the current digital context, AI cannot be understood exclusively as a technical innovation nor as a set of neutral tools. In the field of communication automation, it directly intervenes in the social construction of truth, in the production of epistemic authority, and in the definition of what is visible and verifiable in the digital public sphere (Limia and López, 2026). This raises a central issue: how to ensure that public relations maintain a social and ethical approach in an algorithmically mediated environment? The study of the social approach to public relations is urgent because AI redefines power dynamics in communication. Algorithms decide which narratives are amplified and which are invisibilized, functions that affect democratic deliberation and the plurality of voices. In this scenario, the public relations professional must assume a critical role, capable of mediating between technology and society, as well as ensuring that automation is used to strengthen transparency, inclusion, and communicative justice. In Latin America, where structural inequalities and cultural diversity persist, the incorporation of AI demands an ethical framework that avoids reproducing biases and exclusions. The objective of this work is to analyze how artificial intelligence redefines the social approach to public relations, as well as to identify contributions and risks for the exercise of the profession. It seeks to demonstrate that AI can be a facilitator of democratic dialogue and an amplifier of diverse voices, provided it is used under ethical principles and with a commitment to the community. This analysis is part of the research line Sociology of Professions in communication and public relations at the Universidad de Guadalajara.

Literature Review

To understand the scope of the social approach to public relations, it is necessary to review its epistemological trajectory. Historically, the Western construction of the discipline has been determined by a functionalist matrix, of a pragmatic and Anglo-Saxon nature. From its theoretical origins in the United States in the mid-20th century, research in the discipline was structured under an instrumental logic designed to legitimize and defend the interests of large corporations against the questioning of the environment (L’Etang, 2009). This foundational vision, rooted in linear models of information transmission, reduced professional practice to a mere technique of asymmetric diffusion and persuasion, whose primary objective was the optimization of commercial performance and the securing of the organizational status quo (Heide et al., 2018). As a consequence, dominant literature tended to ignore the social, ethical, and democratic impact of communicative processes, limiting the field to a managerial support function subordinate to senior corporate management.

Despite the hegemony of this global managerialist approach, the assimilation of the discipline in Latin American environments required a critical translation due to the structural realities of the region. In the Mexican scenario, for example, Contreras and Garibay (2020) point out that, although the institutionalization of communication in the country and the rest of the continent was initially influenced by the operational logics of the US market, regional literature began to show a gradual distancing towards approaches with a human and ethical sense, contrary to the utilitarian view that conceives publics solely as consumers or passive receivers. Faced with this, the contemporary Latin American perspective has sought to redefine the role of the discipline, approaching it as an integrated social practice, where public relations are assimilated not as an instrument of corporate domination, but as a platform for the co-construction of identities and social justice in vulnerable communities.

This search for a social and comprehensive meaning as opposed to the Anglo-Saxon utilitarian perspective allows classifying professional practice in the region through a typology of differentiated roles. While pragmatic and conservative profiles remain aligned with the corporate status quo, radical and idealistic roles emerge as a need to generate a closer and more beneficial dialogue for the community (Grunig and White, 1992). These same authors recognize that excellent public relations must be symmetrical, critical, and idealistic, far from asymmetrical or merely technical models that reduce practice to unilateral persuasion. The two-way symmetrical model is conceived as a process of negotiation and compromise, more effective in the long term for organizations and society. The public relations professional can play different social roles, including: the pragmatic role that usually defends commercial interests in a marketplace of ideas; the conservative role as a protector of the status quo and economic and political privileges; while the radical role promotes social change through information and public debate, and the idealistic role seeks public interest, mutual understanding, and collaboration. To these are added neutral and critical approaches, which analyze organizational motivations and question the social consequences of the practice.

Holtzhausen (2016) argues that the professional must act as an agent of change, by denouncing injustices and transforming organizations into more democratic institutions. This approach recognizes that public relations cannot be limited to the corporate sphere, but must address the needs of diverse, multiethnic, and multilingual communities. In this sense, she proposes freeing public relations from its narrow definition as organizational management and turning them into tools for community activism and radical politics.

In the European context, Gregory and Willis (2013) state that the strategic leader in public relations must perform four complementary functions; one of them is the social orientation. Here, the communicator protects the legitimacy of the organization through interaction with target audiences to sustain trust in the brand. The role of corporate navigator is characterized by acting as an advisor to senior management, helping to make balanced decisions that protect the interests of the organization's members and preserve its fundamental values. The third role is known as a catalyst in the value chain, because it integrates all stakeholders, including activists and digital communities. Its goal is to generate effective communication according to their needs and expectations. Finally, the functional implementer role performs technical tasks such as the planning of programs and campaigns, in addition to advising other organizational areas on communication matters. The 4x4 model complements the theory of excellence by offering a more operational and strategic view of the professional's roles. While Grunig and White emphasize symmetry and public interest, Gregory and Willis detail how those principles translate into concrete functions within the organization and in its relationship with publics. Both models agree that the public relations professional must be a critical and ethical mediator, capable of balancing corporate interests with social demands in increasingly complex and digitalized environments.

In the Mexican case, for more than two decades, radicalized social exclusion has impacted the work of public relations professionals (Moreno and Molleda, 2005). According to a study by these researchers, professionals have focused their efforts on working with communities to eradicate this trend. Similarly, public relations professionals in large corporations seek to involve their organizations in social responsibility and sustainable development programs to justify their expansion in highly sensitive natural areas, such as the tourism sector (Jiménez, 2012).

According to Flores et al. (2022), in non-governmental organizations in Mexico, it is evident that the communication and public relations model is characterized by being social and ethical, closer to activism than to technical expertise. As well as integrating social responsibility, inclusion, and sustainability as central axes. Likewise, the authors recognize the tension between the strategic and the operational in seeking to consolidate communicative leadership and incorporate the digital and algorithmic dimension as a new field of action. Thus, this model moves towards a combination of social and strategic roles, where the professional is a critical mediator, community activist, and ethical manager of digital communication.

The validity of these roles faces a new frontier today regarding algorithmic mediation and artificial intelligence. Far from decreeing the obsolescence of the human factor, the automation of operational tasks acts as a catalyst. Férez-Vergara et al. (2024) highlight that, given the proliferation of algorithmically generated content, the true strategic value of the professional lies in their capacity to act as an auditor and guarantor of truthfulness and transparency, managing communication from a social responsibility perspective that prioritizes ethical discernment, community inclusion, and cultural sensitivity. Therefore, academic training and the practice of public relations should not be oriented toward competing with the processing speed of AI, but to strengthen the reflective and interactive dimension that safeguards the authenticity of public dialogue and bonds of mutual trust in society.

In this context, artificial intelligence is transforming the practice of public relations, not only in its technical dimension (automation of tasks, data analysis, audience segmentation), but also in its social and ethical role. Under the perspective of Grunig and Holtzhausen, the professional must be an agent of democratic and critical change. In this context, AI can be both a tool that strengthens transparency and dialogue and a risk if used to manipulate or reinforce inequalities.

Among the positive contributions of AI in the field of communication, and in particular of public relations, the use of algorithms that allow analyzing large volumes of information and detecting false narratives stands out. This has favored accountability (DiStaso & McCorkindale, 2020), in addition to managing automatic translation and accessibility systems that expand the participation of multilingual and disabled publics (Moreno et al. 2021). Active listening also plays an important role in sentiment analysis and data mining that help to better understand social concerns and anticipate conflicts (Wright & Hinson, 2022).

Regarding challenges, algorithmic biases are still evident, as AI can reproduce social prejudices if not designed with ethical criteria (Floridi, 2021), as well as information manipulation when using bots and deepfakes, which calls public trust into question (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017). Another problem is dehumanization; if human dialogue is replaced by automated processes, there is a risk of losing the ethical and community dimension of communication (Moreno et al., 2021).

In this sense, it is insisted that artificial intelligence cannot be understood solely as technical innovation or as a set of neutral tools (Limia and López, 2026), which opens a central debate for the social approach to public relations. That is why communicative automation not only increases operational efficiency, but directly intervenes in the social construction of truth, in the definition of what is visible and verifiable in the digital public space. This means that AI participates in the production of epistemic authority; it decides which narratives are amplified, which voices are silenced, and which contents are legitimized as credible. Consequently, the public relations professional can no longer be limited to managing messages; they must assume a critical role towards the algorithms that mediate communication and that, in many cases, condition democratic deliberation. Thus, the emergence of these disruptive technologies poses a dichotomous scenario of challenges and opportunities; on the one hand, there is the imminent risk of an automation that displaces basic operational functions of writing and content management and, on the other, the possibility opens up of enhancing high-level analytical skills, critical thinking, and strategic information curation. In this way, professional practice in the digital age cannot be limited to dexterity in the instrumental use of AI software, but demands solid digital and info-ethical literacy that enables the communicator to interact reflectively with automated systems (Mullo López et al. 2024). Educational institutions will have to include structural transformation; the communicator's profile must migrate towards critical thinking, with an ethical, social, and humanistic approach to the discipline, which is irreplaceable by machines and which allows them to generate authentic and empathetic connections with audiences. Despite advances in public relations and AI, a gap remains regarding how the Latin American social approach can be integrated with algorithmic mediation from an ethical and democratic perspective.

Methodology

The present research was developed under a qualitative approach of an analytical-conceptual nature and exploratory scope. Given that the phenomenon of algorithmic mediation and its impact on social disciplines are a constantly mutating field, the qualitative methodological design is the most suitable for examining the structures of meaning, the dynamics of epistemic power, and the ethical reconfigurations experienced by contemporary public relations.

To ensure the rigor and representativeness of the theoretical sample, a systematic and critical review of indexed academic literature in high-impact databases (Scopus, Web of Science, SciELO, and Redalyc) was carried out. The inclusion criteria applied for the consolidation of the documentary corpus were determined from three interconnected thematic axes: a) contemporary models and evolution of public relations; b) typologies of professional roles and community activism; and c) info-ethical implications, algorithmic biases, and governance of artificial intelligence in institutional communication processes.

Procedure

The processing and analysis of information were structured based on the qualitative content analysis method, using a structured thematic coding strategy (Krippendorff, 2018). The units of analysis (scientific articles, book chapters, and international normative reports) were examined in light of the social roles proposed by the Theory of Excellence by Grunig and White (1992), the perspective of the professional as an agent of change by Holtzhausen (2016), and the functions of the 4x4 strategic model by Gregory and Willis (2013). The information collected was organized matrix-wise to contrast the technical-instrumental dimensions of AI against the ethical-social demands of the Latin American context, thereby guaranteeing the internal validity of the analysis.

Analysis and discussion

The analyzed data reveal that ensuring a social and ethical approach in an algorithmically mediated environment implies redefining the social roles of the public relations professional and adapting them to the logic of communicative automation. Symmetrical public relations (Grunig & White, 1992), according to the excellence model, state that communication must be two-way and critical. In the algorithmic environment, this means that professionals must monitor how algorithms distribute information and ensure that narratives are not reduced to corporate or political interests. Symmetry translates into algorithmic audits and the demand for transparency in the use of data.

The data analyzed regarding the insertion of emerging technologies reveal a structural tension between operational automation and the need for strategic thinking. This duality coincides with the findings of Mullo López et al. (2024), who identify that the incorporation of generative artificial intelligence in the communicative ecosystem rapidly displaces traditional technical competencies of writing and basic content management.

The results of this analysis demonstrate that the true added value of the professional does not lie in the speed of algorithmic production, but in the acquisition of robust info-ethical and digital literacy. Therefore, the impact of AI in the region is not limited to a substitution of functions, but acts as a catalyst that forces the migration of professional profiles towards roles of greater analytical complexity and critical curation.

Regarding the activist role (Holtzhausen, 2016), the results show that AI can be used to make marginalized voices visible and strengthen public debate. The professional, under this role, must act as a critical mediator, denouncing algorithmic biases and promoting digital inclusion. AI becomes a tool for activism when it amplifies counter-hegemonic narratives and facilitates citizen participation. This social approach demands that public relations serve the public interest (L’Etang, 2009).

In the digital space, this is achieved through the design of strategies that use AI to facilitate democratic dialogue, ensure accessibility, and foster a plurality of voices. The results show that AI can be a strategic ally if it is integrated under ethical principles.

From the Latin American perspective, Flores et al. (2022) show that, in international non-governmental organizations in Mexico, communication and public relations professionals tend to perform roles closer to the European 4x4 model of Gregory and Willis, with a predominance of operational and managerial functions, and less relationship with the social roles of Grunig and White. However, the study confirms that the emerging model is characterized by being social and ethical, closer to activism than to technique, integrating social responsibility, inclusion, and sustainability as central axes. These findings reinforce the idea that, in contexts of inequality and cultural diversity, AI must be applied with intercultural sensitivity, avoiding homogenizing discourses and recognizing the richness of communities. When discussing the ethical dimension and social commitment of public relations against algorithmic mediation, the arguments presented distance themselves from instrumental technological determinism to place the human being at the center of the communicative process.

In agreement with Mullo López et al. (2024), it is discussed that given the proliferation of automated narratives and the latent risk of informative manipulation or loss of trust in the public space, the communicator's function acquires an eminently political and community dimension. This reality validates the urgency of the Mexican model reviewed in this research: the public relations professional must assume the role of auditor and guarantor of institutional transparency. Technology, consequently, must be subordinated to a framework of social responsibility that protects intercultural sensitivity and citizen inclusion, spheres where machines possess no capacity for ethical or empathetic replication.

Conclusion

The analysis developed throughout this work allows us to conclude that the social approach to public relations does not represent a peripheral or merely philanthropic option, but a condition for epistemological and democratic survival in the era of algorithmic mediation. In an information ecosystem where artificial intelligence has the capacity to massively automate content flows and structure visibility in the public sphere, the traditional function of the public relations practitioner as a mere technical operator or optimizer of corporate messages is obsolete. Technology does not displace the human factor; on the contrary, it acts as a catalyst that forces the discipline to migrate towards roles of high analytical complexity, where critical thinking, intercultural empathy, and info-ethical curation constitute the true differentiating elements.

In the Mexican context, the conclusions acquire a nuance of structural urgency. The region cannot limit itself to uncritically importing the managerial and utilitarian theoretical models of the Anglo-Saxon school, which were designed for stable and symmetrical corporate realities. The marked socioeconomic inequalities, multiethnic diversity, and challenges regarding institutional transparency that characterize not only Mexico, but the rest of Latin American countries, demand the consolidation of a distinct and hybrid model. In our regional context, it must be founded on a combination of strategic, radical, and idealistic roles, assuming communication as a platform for counter-power, community activism, and symmetrical dialogue oriented towards social justice.

Faced with this scenario, the need to profoundly transform university curricula in Communication and Public Relations degrees becomes evident. Higher education institutions must go beyond the instrumental teaching of generative AI software and the operational management of digital networks. It is urgent to integrate compulsory subjects focused on Infoethics, Algorithmic Governance, Sociology of Professions, and Latin American Critical Thinking. The training of the future professional must be oriented towards endowing them with communicative auditing competencies, enabling them to identify algorithmic biases, combat information disorder, and protect the humanistic and interactive dimension that sustains public trust. It is concluded that public relations can maintain a social and ethical approach in algorithmic environments when AI is subordinated to principles of transparency, inclusion, and social responsibility.

Future Lines of Research

For the field of contemporary organizations, the social approach demands subordinating the technical efficiency of automated systems to strict frameworks of social responsibility and accountability. Excellent public relations in the 21st century must guide senior corporate management in the design of ethical data use policies, ensuring that algorithms do not reproduce exclusions or marginalize vulnerable communities. Finally, as future lines of research derived from this study, it is suggested to carry out empirical research that quantitatively maps the degree of AI adoption in the communication departments of Mexican organizations, as well as to evaluate the impact of algorithmic audits on the preservation of corporate legitimacy and the strengthening of democratic deliberation in the digital age. A comparative Latin American analysis would be highly pertinent, as well as of algorithmic biases and ethics.
