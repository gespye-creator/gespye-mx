Abstract.

This research analyzes the role of savings and credit cooperatives (COACs) in Ecuador's lower socioeconomic segments (segments 1, 2, and 3) as agents of financial inclusion, based on the structure and orientation of their loan products. The study employs an exploratory and explanatory methodology, relying on primary data collected between January and July 2025 from the websites of 193 COACs—representing a 95% sample of cooperatives in the analyzed segments. This information was supplemented by document review and comparative analysis with private banks. Group loans, agricultural loans, and loans with a gender focus are examined in particular, given their connection to vulnerable populations. The results reveal a loan offering predominantly geared toward consumption, with limited diversification toward inclusive productive products. The conclusions indicate that, despite their relevance within the financial system and their social mission, COACs demonstrate a limited application of credit technologies consistent with deep financial inclusion, highlighting the need for regulatory incentives and institutional strengthening.

Keywords:

Savings and credit cooperatives, Financial Inclusion, Credit, Ecuador.

Introduction

This research analyzes savings and credit cooperatives (COACs) in terms of their role as financial inclusion organizations, considering them a fundamental part of the Popular and Solidarity Financial Sector (SFPS). The nature of this sector dynamically drives a valid strategy to offer greater development opportunities to the most economically vulnerable population, integrating them into the Ecuadorian economy in a symmetrical and democratic manner (Sánchez, 2016).

The 2008 Constitution of the Republic of Ecuador (EC, 2008) marked a milestone in formalizing local regulations regarding the Popular and Solidarity Economy (EPS), both in its real and financial sectors. According to Article 83, the current economic system in the country is social and solidary; that is, it “recognizes the human being as a subject and an end,” seeking conditions that enable achieving “buen vivir” (good living). Furthermore, this same Constitution identifies that the EPS will be “regulated according to the law,” including groups of cooperatives (financial and non-financial), associations, and community sectors. Thus, it can be argued that the conceptual umbrella of the SFPS, of which COACs are part, is the definition of the Social and Solidarity Economy (ESS).

According to Coraggio (2011), the Social and Solidarity Economy is underpinned by a set of economic principles that guide its practices. Among the most relevant are the guarantee of work for all people; access of workers to knowledge and means of production; collective and autonomous management; socially responsible production oriented toward social justice; the absence of exploitation of another's labor; economic sustainability; the promotion of responsible consumption and free initiative together with socially responsible innovation; the recognition of pluralism and diversity; non-discrimination, both individual and collective; the complex and synergistic articulation between ESS organizations and ventures; and its territorial anchoring.

In addition to the aforementioned principles, it is important to note that Coraggio includes others more closely related to the cooperative and economic spheres: i) solidary cooperation, which implies the “social integration of the production system”; and ii) the concept that money does not create money (non-usury), understanding this financial asset as a means rather than an end. This point is a fundamental matter of debate within the context of the problem discussed in this research, for although the precepts of non-usury are (or should be) the foundations of the operation and focus of COACs, reality differs, revealing even similarities with banking entities.

These principles, especially those linked to solidarity, can also translate into the pursuit of goals by COAC members. According to Segovia-Vargas et al. (2023), the administration of microcredits carried out by COACs has a significant impact on local communities, contributing to their development in various ways: by facilitating access to financial services that, in other scenarios, would be difficult to obtain (and without which leads to seeking loan sharks), and by “improving infrastructure and supporting micro-enterprises.” These authors conclude that COACs play a fundamental role in promoting financial inclusion, becoming at the same time primary instruments for sustainable economic development and the growth of micro-enterprises (through credit provision). Such concepts are also shared by the Consultative Group to Assist the Poor (CGAP, 2005), which further asserts that COACs serve markets that are unattractive to banks.

From this perspective, the research problem is developed, wherein COACs are understood as instruments of financial inclusion and economic development for their members. The analysis focuses on evaluating the appropriateness of credit products offered by local COACs in relation to the presumed needs of their target audience, which, according to literature on EPS and ESS, consists of an economically vulnerable population demanding financial products distinct from those offered by traditional banking.

By way of justification, the role of COACs in the Ecuadorian financial market is highlighted; however, before conducting this analysis, it is important to detail the classification of Ecuadorian COACs by size. According to JPRF (2025), SFPS organizations are categorized into five established segments: i) segment 1, comprising organizations with over USD 80 million in total assets; ii) segment 2, with organizations between USD 20 and USD 80 million; iii) segment 3, with organizations between USD 5 and USD 20 million; iv) segment 4, with organizations between USD 1 and USD 5 million; and v) segment 5, with organizations with less than USD 1 million in total assets.

According to an SFPS register compiled by the Superintendency of Popular and Solidarity Economy (SEPS), as of July 2025 the institutional ecosystem of the sector consisted of 393 organizations, of which 44 belonged to segment 1, 64 to segment 2, 97 to segment 3, 136 to segment 4, and 52 to segment 5. Together, these cooperatives reached USD 26,838 million in total assets, a figure representing nearly a third (27%) of the total assets in the country's financial system (ASOBANCA, 2025). This proportion positions Ecuadorian financial cooperativism as a key actor in the national economy; furthermore, COACs also hold significance at the Latin American level.

According to data from Durán (2024), Ecuadorian COACs stand out among their Latin American peers by ranking second in total assets, accounting for 11.19% of the region's cooperative financial system. Unlike countries with larger populations and stronger economies such as Mexico or Brazil, where COAC shares in local financial systems are smaller (1.93% and 4.41%, respectively), cooperatives in Ecuador are key actors in financial intermediation. Three of its cooperatives rank among the top 10 with the highest relative weight in their country's financial system, and 11 are among the 100 largest in Latin America, demonstrating that Ecuador's savings and credit cooperative system can be considered one of the most powerful at the regional level.

This article focuses solely on COACs in segments 1, 2, and 3. The selection of these organizations responds to their dominant share in the savings and credit cooperative system in monetary terms. Although there is a significant number of COACs in segments 4 and 5 (188 organizations – 48% of the total), their share of total assets is minor; according to SEPS (2025), as of March 2025, the combined share of both segments reached only 1.9% of the total, making it statistically non-significant for the sample.

Given these background context, the objective of the research is to analyze the structure and financial inclusion of COACs in segments 1, 2, and 3 in Ecuador based on their credit products, considering primary data collection on the types of loans offered by COACs that are most closely tied to financial inclusion processes.

In addition to the introduction, the article presents 4 additional chapters. Chapter 2 develops a conceptual framework of financial inclusion, including its relationship with COACs and its approach from the perspective of credit placements. Chapter 3 presents the results of the nexus between financial inclusion and credit technologies, based on primary information collected from the websites of COACs in segments 1, 2, and 3. Finally, Chapter 4 presents the study's conclusions.

Financial Inclusion and savings and credit cooperatives

Definition of financial inclusion and relationship with COACs

There is an extensive literature dealing with financial inclusion and the application of this concept in various case studies. In general terms, financial inclusion refers to the level of access to and use of financial services by individuals and businesses in a country, which must be accessible, safe, and sustainable, considering that it is often measured by the number of bank accounts per capita, the use of savings accounts, or the granting of credit (Orazi et al., 2019). Another concept, such as that articulated by the National Banking and Securities Commission of Mexico, links education to financial inclusion; that is, as access to and use of financial services that guarantee consumer protection schemes within a regulatory framework that, in turn, promotes financial education, with the objective of strengthening the financial capabilities of the entire population (Raccanello & Herrera, 2014).

Within the conceptualization of financial inclusion, it is important to present definitions with multidimensional approaches; for example, the concept of the Network of Development Financial Institutions of Ecuador – RFD (RFD, 2025) and that proposed by the Development Bank of Latin America – CAF (CAF, 2020). In the former, financial inclusion is based on seven dimensions: access, usage, quality, financial education, financial well-being, protection, and regulation; whereas according to CAF there are four supply and demand elements that coincide with some of the RFD variables: access, usage, quality, and financial well-being. Ultimately, the RFD definition encompasses the parameters used by CAF, adding the regulatory framework and financial education.

Considering the focus of this research, more specific definitions of the dimensions of access, usage, and quality are presented. The access dimension refers to the availability, proximity, and appropriate cost of formal financial products and services for the population. Usage evaluates the frequency and regularity with which individuals use financial products and services. Quality, on the other hand, is measured through user satisfaction, taking into account aspects such as ease of access, costs, security, and the tailoring of financial products to customer needs.

Regarding the relationship between financial inclusion and cooperatives, Lemus and Rojas (2022) compiled a synthesis of concepts linking COACs with access to financial services. According to their work, COACs are entities focused on low- and middle-socioeconomic segments, specializing in microcredits, which grants them an important role in territorial development by allowing marginalized social groups access to financial services and thereby supporting micro and small economic unit entrepreneurship. Furthermore, in the empirical section of their research, the authors demonstrated (for the Chilean case) that cooperatives have a greater incidence among the population in lower income percentiles (up to the 60th percentile), while bank financing is concentrated among higher-income individuals.

In terms of age, the study by Lemus and Rojas (2022) concludes that, on average, COAC borrowers are older than bank borrowers; additionally, regarding gender, there is a higher proportion of female to male borrowers in COACs than in banks. On the other hand, regarding the regional variable, COACs have a greater incidence in regions outside metropolitan areas.

Financial inclusion and credit products

Returning to the RFD definition, where the quality factor is “the capacity of financial products and services to satisfy the expectations and needs of users” (RFD, 2025, p. 63), the following paragraphs identify several credit product concepts developed to address significant gaps in borrowing needs, particularly for more vulnerable users.

First, group credit technologies will be addressed, which, according to Valero and Pellejero (2017), can take the form of solidarity groups and village banks (also known as banca comunal), and are built upon several key elements. First, group members must know each other beforehand, which reduces asymmetric information risks and strengthens solidarity ties. Second, assessments of payment capacity and willingness are conducted internally through consensus within the group itself. Furthermore, these schemes operate on the principle of joint liability (solidarity guarantee): if one or more members cannot meet their credit obligations, the rest of the group collectively assumes responsibility for full payment to the lender.

According to Morduch and Armendáriz (2005), differences among various group credit models usually lie in loan size, mandatory prior savings, and group size. However, all share a central feature: their solidary nature. Members voluntarily accept joint credit terms and back each other, relying on the responsibility of friends, family, or acquaintances, which helps protect the credit history of those who, for any reason, fail to pay their share. This feature aligns directly with one of the core cooperative principles—concern for community—as group technologies, by executing operations solidarily, contribute to the sustainable development of their communities and promote social responsibility.

Beyond these structural aspects, it is important to highlight how collective financial technologies directly connect to financial inclusion. According to Madise (2019), this type of inclusion must account for the barriers rural populations face in accessing banking services, especially in territories where traditional financial institutions have no presence. In this context, village banks represent a viable alternative, as they operate autonomously and replicate dynamics similar to those practiced by COACs. Their operation falls within the principles of ESS, reinforcing their community-oriented and participatory nature.

Regarding financial inclusion and its link to credit access in rural areas, publications exist addressing this issue in Latin American countries: Reyes and Muñoz (2016) developed a guide to foster financial inclusion in rural areas through community savings and credit associations in Colombia, using Savings and Credit Groups (GACC) as a case study supported by the Multilateral Investment Fund (FOMIN). For their part, Daneshvar et al. (2017) define it as the process by which small rural producers sustainably and appropriately access formal financial services—such as credit, savings, payment methods, and insurance—under conditions adapted to their needs and territorial realities.

Furthermore, the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC/CEPAL) published several studies on the financial inclusion of small rural producers in Latin American countries, including Costa Rica (Vásquez et al., 2017), El Salvador (Argumedo, 2017), the Dominican Republic (Reyes & Daneshvar, 2017), and Mexico (Campos, 2017). All four ECLAC studies conclude that despite various institutional efforts, small rural producers' access to formal financial services remains limited. Structural factors such as territorial dispersion, productive informality, inadequate banking infrastructure, and low income continue to restrict financing opportunities. Although microcredits, state programs, and cooperatives exist, their coverage fails to satisfy real demand or ensure sustainability.

COAC credit products within a financial inclusion framework

The information presented above underscores the importance of an adequate supply of financial products and services to raise financial inclusion levels, particularly in areas with lower provision or supply by social-solidarity organizations and/or microfinance institutions.

In this context, this section analyzes, from the perspective of credit placement, the reality of the current offer of financial products by COACs in segments 1, 2, and 3 in Ecuador targeted toward populations with higher probabilities of financial exclusion—whether due to economic vulnerability, living/working area (urban/rural), or gender, which are three of the variables analyzed in detailed definitions of financial inclusion.

Considering that there is no systematized secondary data on credit products offered by SFPS entities, an exploratory and explanatory study (Hernández et al., 2014) was conducted to determine current credit offerings linked to financial inclusion (according to the aforementioned parameters) across segment 1, 2, and 3 COACs in Ecuador. This involved a detailed search of each entity's offerings through their websites.

Primary data collection was conducted in January 2025 and updated in July of the same year. At that time, there was a universe of 203 COACs, of which 46 belonged to segment 1, 61 to segment 2, and 96 to segment 3. Out of the total registered COACs at the beginning of the year, data was gathered for 193 entities (95%), including 46 from segment 1 (100%), 61 from segment 2 (100%), and 86 from segment 3 (90%).

According to the data above, the number of COACs offering group, agricultural, and/or gender-linked credits represents a small minority among segment 1, 2, and 3 cooperatives. In fact, group loan provision is the rarest among the analyzed COACs, with zero cases in segment 1 and only 4 cases in the other two segments combined. It is worth noting that this credit product is regulated by the Financial Policy and Regulation Board (JPRF, 2023) as part of the microcredit segment, defined as “…granted to a natural or legal person with annual sales equal to or less than USD 300,000.00, or to a group of borrowers with a joint liability guarantee…”.

Considering that group and agricultural credits relate to productive processes, most should fall into the microcredit segment. Although microcredit is important for COACs, it is not their main segment, maintaining an average share of 38% over the last decade compared to 53% for consumer loans4 (ASOBANCA, 2025). This statistic is significant, as it indicates that the credit orientation of savings and credit cooperatives is not primarily focused on the productive activities of micro-entrepreneurs, but rather on consumer lending. This partially explains the limited interest among COACs in developing microcredit technologies distinct from traditional ones (urban and individual). From the figures above, the orientation of banks toward financing large-scale productive activity and COACs toward small-scale production is clear. However, it should be noted that consumer credit share in both banks and cooperatives is very close, suggesting that both types of entities may be financing operations with lesser impact on the inclusion of the most vulnerable populations.

Returning to the analysis of the 3 COAC credit categories with the greatest potential to support financial inclusion, primary study results showed that the following 4 COACs offer solidary group credits: i) Andina Ltda., ii) CoopSalinas Ltda., iii) Uniblock y Servicios Ltda., iv) Uniotavalo Ltda.

In the case of COAC Andina Ltda. (headquartered in Latacunga), group credit, named “Andina Group,” can be granted to groups or associations to finance rural productive activities. The group must consist of a minimum of 3 and a maximum of 10 people, and eligibility requirements resemble those for individual microcredits—for example, presenting RUC, RISE, or supplier/customer certificates, VAT returns, IESS statements, property titles, deed copies, and proof of property tax payment, among others. Although these requirements serve as filters to analyze potential borrowers' payment capacity, they do not fully align with the features of a solidary group technology, where the primary guarantee is mutual solidarity among group members.

For its part, the “credi-asociativo” offered by COAC CoopSalinas Ltda. (located in Salinas de Guaranda, Bolívar province) is a product geared toward associative groups needing capital for tourism ventures, fixed asset purchases for production, and/or patent and trademark registration. However, this product lacks joint liability guarantee features, focusing instead on promoting associative economic activities in its sphere of influence.

COAC Uniblock y Servicios Ltda., headquartered in Latacunga, offers its members “credi amigo,” a group loan with solidary signatures (which does adhere to core group methodology) for up to 12 members. This product is intended for business expansion, working capital, or business premises renovation. Although its requirements include micro-enterprise documentation (RUC, RISE, purchase invoices), these are less demanding than those required by COAC Andina.

Finally, the “unimujer emprendedora” loan from COAC UniOtavalo Ltda. can finance individuals or groups wishing to invest in small-scale production, commercial, or service activities. In this case, beyond the number of co-borrowers, there are no specific features tied to solidary guarantees.

In conclusion regarding group technology credits, all offering COACs are located in the Andean highlands (sierra) of Ecuador: 2 in Cotopaxi province, 1 in Imbabura province, and 1 in Bolívar province. Among the four products, the only one explicitly featuring solidary signature as a product characteristic is “credi amigo” from Uniblock y Servicios Ltda., while the remainder share only the group format, differing in focus (associative vs. non-associative) and requirements.

In short, the collected data on group loans in segment 1, 2, and 3 COACs highlights minimal sector interest in such products—whether due to operating costs, low financial returns, or the market currently served by the country's cooperative institutions, which, as shown in Figures 1 and 2, partially overlaps with the market served by the banking sector. Beyond these factors, there appears to be a gradual decline in COAC interest in placing group credits; based on Oñate-Paredes (2024), in January 2024 there were 2 segment 1 cooperatives still issuing group loans (COAC Virgen del Cisne Ltda. and COAC Cooprogreso), but neither offers these credit technologies today.

Regarding the second dimension analyzed—agricultural credits—the following 18 COACs currently offer such products:

  • Segment 1: Tulcán Ltda., Atuntaqui Ltda., Lucha Campesina Ltda.

  • Segment 2: Microempresarial Sucre Ltda., Alianza Minas Ltda., COOPAC Austro Ltda., Sumak Kawsay Ltda., Unión El Ejido Ltda., Crediamigo Ltda.

  • Segment 3: Migrantes del Ecuador Ltda., Focla Ltda., San Miguel Ltda., San Miguel de Sigchos Ltda., Salinas Ltda., Simiatug Ltda., Pujulí Ltda., Nueva Esperanza Ltda., Santa Anita Ltda.

Regarding agricultural credits, the collected sample shows a diverse offering of agricultural and livestock financial products directed to both individuals and legal entities. Loan amounts vary considerably, ranging from microcredits starting at USD 100 in the case of Unión El Ejido Ltda. (Cotacachi) to USD 100,000 in cooperatives such as Tulcán Ltda. and COOPAC Austro (Cuenca). Repayment terms are also heterogeneous, ranging from 24 months to 10 years depending on the product and requested amount; operationally, some products can be disbursed in relatively short timeframes (48 hours).

In terms of guarantees, most COACs accept personal guarantees up to certain limits (e.g., COAC San Miguel de Sigchos up to USD 10,000 for agricultural credit) and require mortgage guarantees for larger amounts. Certain institutions such as Tulcán Ltda. also make use of the Guarantee Fund for the Popular and Solidarity Economy (CONAFIPS), enabling access without a guarantor. In some cases, an un-shareable equity contribution is required, as in COOPAC Austro, ranging between 1% and 2% of the requested amount depending on the guarantee type.

Interest rates for these products range between 15.50% and 20%, with 18% being a recurring rate among several cooperatives such as Crediamigo Ltda. (Loja), Tulcán Ltda. (Carchi), and San Miguel de Sigchos Ltda. Grace periods, when available, are generally limited to the first two months, and repayment schedules can be monthly, bimonthly, quarterly, or semi-annually. Geographically, most cooperatives are headquartered in highland cities (Imbabura, Cotopaxi, Bolívar, Azuay). Only 3 COACs prominently offer agricultural credits in coastal and Amazonian regions: COACMES Ltda. in Manabí, Lucha Campesina in Guayas, and FOCLA Ltda. in Sucumbíos.

A notable trend is product segmentation by applicant profile. For example, San Miguel de Sigchos Ltda. and Lucha Campesina Ltda. offer differentiated credits for rural women and youth, incorporating an inclusive perspective into their portfolio that sets their products apart by addressing a second vulnerability dimension: geographical location (rural sector).

The third dimension of analysis is gender-linked credits. Primary data collection identified 18 COACs offering this type of product:

  • Segment 1: 15 de Abril Ltda., Indígena SAC Ltda., De la Pequeña Empresa de Cotopaxi Ltda., Lucha Campesina Ltda.

  • Segment 2: Pedro Moncayo Ltda., Fondvida Ltda., San Gabriel Ltda., Sumak Kawsay Ltda., Minga Ltda.

  • Segment 3: 13 de Abril Ltda., Migrantes del Ecuador Ltda., Pushak Runa Ltda., San Miguel Ltda., San Miguel de Sigchos Ltda., Simiatug Ltda., Nueva Esperanza Ltda., Grupo Difare, UniOtavalo Ltda.

In summary, these loans are intended to finance small-scale productive, commercial, or service activities, as well as cover personal or family expenses. Loan amounts range from a minimum of USD 100 to a maximum of USD 60,000. Repayment terms range between 1 and 72 months. Interest rates recorded range from 14.9% nominal to 22% benchmark annual.

Loans can be repaid in monthly, bimonthly, quarterly, or semi-annual installments, depending on the payment plan. Additionally, some cooperatives provide extra benefits such as credit life insurance, rapid approval within 48 hours, or grace periods (such as three months at COAC Nueva Esperanza). Products without guarantors or with solidary guarantees were also identified.

Loan purposes vary: some cooperatives focus on productive investment (Sumak Kawsay, Migrantes del Ecuador, San Miguel de Sigchos, UniOtavalo), others on personal consumption (Indígena SAC), and some combine both. Specific products for female heads of household in rural areas were also found, such as in Lucha Campesina, or for borrower groups with solidary guarantees.

Geographically, most cooperatives offering female-targeted loans are headquartered in highland cities (14) across Tungurahua, Cotopaxi, Pichincha, Carchi, Chimborazo, Bolívar, and Imbabura. Only 4 COACs offer these products in coastal provinces: Guayas, Manabí, and Los Ríos.

Regarding group credits, only Banco Pichincha and VisionFund offer such products. VisionFund offers two distinct modalities: group credit and village banking (banca comunal). The main difference lies in group size per credit operation: group credit targets 3 to 6 people, while village banking serves larger groups of 7 to 25 members. At Banco Pichincha, the sole group credit product requires all members to have at least 6 months of tenure, capped at USD 6,000 per group (compared to USD 10,000 at VisionFund).

Finally, Banco Pichincha and Banco de Machala are the only banks offering loans exclusively for women, with a strictly productive focus to finance inputs, machinery, work vehicles, or business premises. Maximum amounts reach USD 150,000 at Banco Pichincha and USD 80,000 at Banco de Machala, both offering potential grace periods.

In summary, although relatively few savings and credit cooperatives offer group products, and although agricultural or gender-focused credit products remain limited (albeit more prevalent than in banks), comparing these findings with the traditional banking system demonstrates a clear vocation within the cooperative sector to promote financial inclusion in rural areas, addressing economic vulnerability and supporting women.

Conclusions

This research contributes to understanding the role of savings and credit cooperatives (COACs) in segments 1, 2, and 3 in financial inclusion in Ecuador. Operating within the popular and solidarity financial system, these organizations play strategic roles in territories historically excluded by traditional banking. The analysis shows that COACs not only concentrate a significant volume of assets, but also serve as a crucial channel for democratizing access to financial services in rural and peri-urban areas.

The study reveals a concerning homogenization of credit product offerings, marked by a strong bias toward consumer loans and a limited presence of productive loans. While legislation permits and promotes microcredit methodologies such as solidary group loans, their practical application is virtually non-existent. This lack of credit diversification may disincentivize deep financial inclusion processes, which require flexible products adapted to rural contexts and small family enterprises.

Furthermore, the limited implementation of agricultural or gender-focused products confirms a broader trend in which COACs prioritize financial returns over cooperative principles regarding community interest and inclusive development. Although valuable experience was identified in the Ecuadorian highlands, these cases remain exceptional rather than indicative of sector-wide policy. This underscores the need for regulatory and financial incentives to encourage tailored local product design—for example, expanding subsidized state funds via second-tier institutions like CONAFIPS to support COAC placements in vulnerable markets, reducing COAC financial costs, providing technical oversight on social impact, and offering longer-term capitalization to mitigate liquidity risks.

In conclusion, the Ecuadorian cooperative system—particularly segment 1, 2, and 3 COACs—represents an essential tool for consolidating a more equitable and inclusive financial model. However, realizing their full potential requires structural reforms to incentivize appropriate financial products for excluded sectors, strengthen financial education, foster inter-cooperative collaboration, and ensure regulations prioritize the well-being of the country's most vulnerable populations.
