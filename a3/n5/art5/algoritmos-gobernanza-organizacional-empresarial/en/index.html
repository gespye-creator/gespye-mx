Algorithms as Tools and Actors in Corporate Organizational Governance

Algoritmos como herramientas y actores en la gobernanza organizacional empresarial

Abstract.

The article explores the agency of algorithms as both instruments and agents within corporate governance. It examines their impact on decision-making processes, their incorporation into management systems, and their contribution to operational efficiency and sustainability. A qualitative-documentary approach is used, supported by recent literature and specialized reports. The findings reveal that when algorithms are integrated into organizational processes, they do not merely automate technical tasks, but also generate socio-technical dynamics that reshape internal structures and practices. Finally, the study highlights challenges and opportunities for responsible governance.

Keywords:

algorithms; organizational governance; artificial intelligence; algorithmic agency; decision-making.

Resumen.

El artículo explora la agencia de los algoritmos como instrumentos y agentes en la gobernanza corporativa. Se analizan sus impactos en la forma de hacer una elección, su incorporación en los sistemas de gestión y su contribución a la eficiencia y sostenibilidad operativa. Se utiliza un enfoque cualitativo-documental, apoyado en literatura reciente e informes especializados. Los resultados revelan que los algoritmos, cuando se integran en los procesos organizacionales, no solo automatizan tareas técnicas, sino que crean dinámicas socio-técnicas que reconfiguran las estructuras y prácticas internas. Finalmente, se señalan desafíos y oportunidades para una gobernanza responsable.

Palabras clave:

algoritmos; gobernanza organizacional; inteligencia artificial; agencia algorítmica; toma de decisiones.

Introduction

The arrival of artificial intelligence (AI) and, above all, algorithms in the corporate world has brought about a transformation whose economic sustainability is still a subject of debate (Carvão, 2025), representing a revolution in how companies are structured, decisions are made, and organizations are governed. As Katzenbach and Ulbricht (2019) warn, algorithms do not merely automate processes; they create specific forms of sociability, encoding rules, expectations, and coordination mechanisms among actors. In this environment of accelerated digital transformation, algorithms can no longer be considered simple technical tools subordinated to human control, but rather agents that shape interactions, possess their own logics, and, in some cases, become entities that determine how information and power flow within organizations.

In this sense, algorithmic governance presents itself as a process that reconfigures decision-making capacities and agency, transforming labor practices, hierarchies, and relations under modes of control and coordination mediated by computer systems. As Kellogg et al. (2020) point out, algorithmic control at work occurs through mechanisms that guide, evaluate, and discipline workers, transforming management and supervisory relationships. This ambiguity opens the door to essential questions regarding the influence of algorithms on corporate governance and how their integration transforms corporate management and control practices.

The digitalization of integrated management systems (IMS) has shown that incorporating digital technologies, such as AI, improves efficiency, risk management, and transparency in organizations, enabling strategic alignment with corporate sustainability and responsibility goals (Ahmed, 2026). However, this evolution also poses complex challenges in terms of interpretation, transparency, and the ethics of algorithms, which can reproduce biases and inequalities if not properly addressed (Bustelo, 2025; Pi et al., 2025). In this context, algorithmic governance is an area of study that seeks to understand and control how algorithms affect decision-making, fostering responsible, transparent, and fair practices (Katzenbach & Ulbricht, 2019).

From a theoretical standpoint, socio-technical approaches and actor-network theory provide conceptual frameworks to understand the integration of algorithms in organizations. These theories conceive algorithms not as mere technical artifacts, but as actors that co-constitute organizational practices with human agents, creating complex socio-technical networks where agency is distributed between humans and machines (Jianu et al., 2025). This perspective challenges conventional forms of governance, which tend to focus solely on human agency, and allows for a rethinking of corporate governance with algorithmic agency as a constitutive part of organizational relationships, structures, and processes.

The importance of studying the role of algorithms in corporate governance becomes more pressing as algorithmic systems are integrated into essential corporate functions, ranging from risk management and financial decision-making to executive leadership and innovation (Babaei & Giudici, 2025; Ko, 2024; Singla et al., 2025a). Although promising in terms of efficiency and competitiveness, this adoption requires understanding how algorithms interact with human actors, how they impact power dynamics, and how they can be audited and regulated to prevent unintended outcomes, such as algorithmic discrimination or loss of autonomy (Oladele, 2024).

In this context, this research aims to examine how algorithms engage in corporate governance, both as technical instruments and as agents with agency. How do algorithms participate and engage in the practices, relationships, and decision-making processes of corporate organizations as tools or actors of digital change? To answer this question, theoretical approaches from actor-network theory and the socio-technical perspective are adopted, complemented by a qualitative documentary review analysis.

The objective of this article is to contribute to a critical and multidimensional understanding of corporate algorithmic governance, recognizing how algorithms shape the structure, decision-making, and power relationships within the firm. Furthermore, it seeks to compare theory with cases studied in secondary sources to propose a new perspective on organizational governance that integrates algorithmic agency and its ethical and strategic implications, as well as the challenges and opportunities it presents for management in the digital age.

This research joins the body of work that recognizes AI and algorithms as transformative agents in contemporary organizations, offering conceptual frameworks and critical analyses to guide responsible, transparent, and effective governance in an era of accelerated and complex technological innovation.

Literature Review and Theoretical Framework

Corporate governance in the era of digital transformation has been impacted by the growing presence of algorithms and AI systems in business processes. This reality requires in-depth analysis from different perspectives to understand how algorithms function not only as tools, but as agents with agency in organizations. To this end, theoretical frameworks combining socio-technical and actor-network perspectives are drawn upon, alongside recent studies analyzing algorithmic governance from various angles.

Digitalization and Integrated Management Systems

The digitalization of integrated management systems (IMS) serves as a starting point for understanding technology-enabled organizational transformation driven by advanced digital technologies such as AI. Ahmed (2026) conducts a systematic review showing how the implementation of digital IMS platforms enhances operational efficiency, risk management, and ESG (environmental, social, and governance) reporting. This digitalization enables real-time monitoring, predictive maintenance, and automated reporting, outperforming traditional systems and becoming a pillar for Industry 5.0. From a governance perspective, digitalization enables strategic alignment with sustainability goals while improving transparency and stakeholder trust, a necessity for contemporary organizational management.

Algorithmic Governance: Concepts and Ethical Challenges

Algorithmic governance is a form of governance that coordinates actors through rules that encode complex epistemological procedures executed by computational algorithms. This encompasses practices, policies, and legal frameworks designed to regulate, supervise, and justify the use of algorithms in public and private sector decision-making. Unlike traditional regulation, algorithmic governance accounts for the plurality of social order forms—including decentralized and non-state modes—and how algorithms shape and encode social interactions and power dynamics (Katzenbach & Ulbricht, 2019; Srivastava, 2021).

This interdisciplinary field addresses ethical, legal, social, and technical issues arising from automation and AI, such as algorithmic bias, discrimination, lack of transparency, and accountability. Bustelo (2025) proposes periodic bias audits for transparent and fair algorithmic governance, emphasizing the distinction between algorithms, software programs, and data for effective oversight. Furthermore, he argues that algorithmic governance requires legal frameworks and institutional capacity building to rectify automated decisions and build public trust.

Algorithmic discrimination is thoroughly addressed by Pi, Huang, and Zhang (2025), who discuss the causes and dangers of algorithmic black boxes and biased training data. They suggest a metric to measure discrimination risk and recommend collaborative governance involving society, corporations, and government to strengthen legal frameworks and digital literacy to mitigate risks. This perspective is fundamental to analyzing how algorithmic agency affects organizational and social relationships, whether by reproducing inequalities or promoting social justice.

Socio-Technical and Actor-Network Theories in Algorithmic Integration

To understand how algorithms integrate into business organizations, the socio-technical approach is valuable as it explores the interaction between social and technological factors in constructing organizational practices and structures. Jianu, Tussyadiah, and Miller (2025) argue that algorithmic management systems are assemblages co-created through organizational practices. They emphasize that humanizing algorithmic management lies not in the algorithm as an object, but in human agency—in the interpretive and resistant negotiation that reconfigures it to preserve autonomy, discretion, and dignity at work.

Thus, the algorithmic element is understood as an active agent of organizational governance that constructs power dynamics through strategic forms of visibility and invisibility, which shape the conditions under which algorithmic management manifests as more or less humanizing.

Bruno Latour, in Reassembling the Social (2005), proposes a lens that enriches the understanding of algorithms in organizations from the perspective of Actor-Network Theory (ANT). For Latour, actors are not static entities, but rather flows and circulating objects stabilized through numerous interactions and experiments. In this sense, algorithms can be considered non-human agents participating in complex socio-technical networks where humans and technologies co-constitute each other.

This perspective makes it possible to examine how algorithms are not mere instruments, but agents with distributed agency actively participating in organizational decision-making, execution, and control. By flattening the social sphere and highlighting the heterogeneity and performativity of relationships, ANT offers a framework for rethinking corporate governance by incorporating algorithmic agency and recognizing the relational and complex nature of organizational networks (Latour, 2005).

Algorithmic Agency and Decision-Making Models

Algorithmic agency revolutionizes the concept of agency, which has traditionally been associated with human action. With artificial intelligence, algorithms become actors holding real influence over decision-making processes—not as simple tools, but as forces shaping administrative behavior and outcomes. As Peeters (2020) notes, this algorithmic agency extends beyond computer code, influencing human behavior and altering how humans interact with machines. This impacts the scope of human discretion, as automated decisions can restrict professionals' capacity for oversight and override, raising concerns regarding transparency and accountability in public administration as well as in algorithmic work management (Jarrahi et al., 2021).

The technical black box of many algorithms, particularly machine learning models, hinders comprehension and control, opening the door to new forms of "algorithmic governance" or "algocracy" that redefine power and responsibility. As Swarup (2025) asserts, agency must be understood as multi-agent, where human and algorithmic agents define the boundaries of action. Agency deviations occur through the manipulation of beliefs, desires, and plans, requiring cognitive and self-assessment models to identify them. Thus, algorithmic agency does not merely execute decisions; it shapes human and organizational behavior, necessitating interdisciplinary approaches that incorporate transparency, human oversight, and professional training to ensure fairness and equity in AI-supported decisions.

Methodology

This research is positioned within the field of organizational governance and algorithms of digital change, aiming to explore the role of algorithms in corporate organizational governance and how they affect internal relationships, structures, and processes to redefine governance through algorithmic agency. The research question addressed in this study is: How do algorithms participate and engage in the practices, relationships, and decision-making processes of corporate organizations as tools or actors of digital change?

To address this, a socio-technical and actor-network lens is adopted, accounting for the integration of algorithms as technical and social actors in organizations. In this regard, the approach recognizes the co-constitution of organizational practices and algorithmic technologies, granting agency to both humans and machines (Jianu et al., 2025). The methodological design is qualitative and exploratory-descriptive, seeking to build a rich and contextualized understanding of the phenomena studied through documentary analysis.

Documentary Analysis

A review of scientific literature, corporate reports, legislation, and case studies on algorithmic governance, digitalization of integrated management systems, AI risk management, and algorithm participation in corporate decision-making was conducted. This review relies on relevant academic and technical literature, such as Ahmed (2026) on IMS digitalization, Bustelo (2025) on algorithmic bias auditing, and Oladele (2024) on the ethical and governance implications of AI in corporate decision-making. Furthermore, international theoretical and regulatory frameworks as well as regulatory cases—such as California's AI transparency legislation and algorithmic governance in China—are addressed. The documentary review identifies primary theories, practices, and challenges in integrating algorithms into organizational governance, comparing them with existing empirical evidence and current regulations.

Source identification and selection followed a systematic two-phase process. In the first exploratory phase, ResearchRabbit and Consensus platforms were used to map citation networks and identify key authors and thematic interconnections. In the second systematic search phase, findings were verified and expanded through direct queries in Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and SciELO, using terms such as "algorithmic governance", "AI corporate governance", "gobernanza algorítmica", and "agencia algorítmica", among others.

Recent publications (primarily from the last five years) were prioritized alongside foundational classic theoretical references. From an initial corpus of approximately 250 related documents, a set of relevant sources was selected, incorporating core references that directly support the research question, theoretical framework, and discussion.

Data Analysis

Qualitative data collected through documentary analysis were coded and categorized using thematic analysis. This method enabled the recognition of patterns, recurrences, and conflicts surrounding algorithmic agency in organizational practices, comparing emerging categories with theoretical frameworks of algorithmic governance, socio-technical perspective, and actor-network theory. Systematic comparison across categories and sources provides deeper insight into distributed agency between humans and algorithmic artifacts in decision-making, control, and organizational coordination processes.

To enhance analytical validity, information from different types of documentary sources (scientific literature, corporate reports, legislation, and secondary case studies) was triangulated, seeking convergences and divergences among reviewed evidence. This documentary contrast aims to strengthen the interpretative validity of the research and identify potential biases or gaps in the literature. Furthermore, modes of visibility and invisibility in algorithmic decision-making systems were examined, in line with sociomaterial perspectives that question how certain elements are rendered present while others remain absent in organizational governance.

In summary, this qualitative and multidimensional methodology provides a detailed understanding of how algorithms operate and participate in corporate organizational governance as both tools and socio-technical actors, supporting a new framework for governance in the digital era.

Support Tools and Resources

To manage the volume of reviewed information and maintain analytical consistency, specialized digital tools were employed. MaxQDA qualitative analysis software was used to code, categorize, and visualize emerging patterns, systematizing documentary material, comparing categories, and creating thematic matrices. This tool enabled tracking of analytical decisions and strengthened the transparency of the interpretive process. Specifically, MaxQDA facilitated the generation of co-occurrence matrices across thematic categories and the visualization of conceptual frequencies within the corpus, allowing more precise identification of recurring patterns in algorithmic agency and organizational governance.

In addition, AI-powered support platforms were used instrumentally to complement the research process. ResearchRabbit mapped citation networks starting from seed articles, identifying key authors and thematic links that guided the systematic search. Consensus complemented this process by articulating consensus and divergence within algorithmic governance literature. Both tools facilitated locating relevant literature in indexed databases through algorithmic recommendations and citation network analysis.

For corpus management, PDF reference managers were utilized to annotate, highlight, and categorize selected sources. Additionally, generative language models were leveraged exclusively as writing and style editing assistants to enhance clarity, terminology consistency, and manuscript flow, without intervening in the interpretive analysis or synthesis of findings.

These tools did not replace academic judgment but served as supplements that improved workflow and document management. Interpretation, synthesis, and conceptualization remained strictly under the researcher's responsibility, ensuring epistemological rigor and intellectual integrity.

Discussion

This study has addressed the algorithm as an agent in corporate governance, both in its role as a technological tool and as an actor with agency in the dynamics, relationships, and decision-making processes of digital change. Theoretical and conceptual findings are combined with insights and evidence from reviewed scientific literature to critically reflect on algorithmic integration in organizations and its implications for modern governance.

First, the digitalization of integrated management systems (IMS) acts as an enabler that redefines organizational governance, leveraging digital technologies—including AI—to optimize operational efficiency, risk management, and reporting transparency (Ahmed, 2026; Ispas et al., 2025; Tummalapalli et al., 2023). This demonstrates that algorithms are not mere automation mechanisms, but creators of new human-algorithm interaction modes for real-time monitoring and data-driven decision-making. By fostering strategic alignment with sustainability and circular economy goals, IMS digitalization implies an organizational structure and culture transformation that integrates algorithmic agency into corporate governance.

The socio-technical theory and actor-network perspective informing this research align with sociomaterial analyses of algorithmic management systems, where agency is localized neither in algorithms nor in humans, but in their co-constitution within situated organizational practices (Jianu et al., 2025). Humanizing algorithmic systems as an emergent outcome of the interplay between technological capabilities and institutional regulatory frameworks can preserve discretion and worker autonomy, preventing risks of dehumanization and loss of control. Emphasizing algorithms as agents with constrained yet meaningful agency highlights their role in shaping power relations and organizational governance (Catapang & Ashipaoloye, 2024).

In decision-making, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) represents a methodological step toward resolving the classic tradeoff between predictive accuracy and transparency, enabling more informed and responsible decisions (Babaei & Giudici, 2025). Using explanatory models such as Shapley values helps explain the contribution of financial variables in estimating risk and expected returns, serving as a valuable asset for financial governance and strategic management in firms, particularly SMEs. This reinforces the notion that algorithms are not mere calculation tools, but active agents participating in knowledge creation and decision-making, with direct implications for organizational accountability and transparency.

However, introducing algorithms into corporate governance also raises ethical and bias concerns that must be addressed. Literature on algorithmic bias auditing underscores that despite promises of objectivity and efficiency, algorithms can reproduce or exacerbate inequalities and discrimination due to data quality, design choices, and lack of transparency (Bustelo, 2025; Pi et al., 2025). Transparent algorithmic governance requires regulatory frameworks, independent audits, and institutional training to monitor and rectify automated decisions, transforming management and organizational control practices. This ethical dimension is essential to legitimize algorithmic agency and ensure that its role in governance does not compromise social justice and public trust.

Accordingly, algorithmic responsibility and accountability are key elements in digital technology governance, as illustrated by China's government campaign to regulate algorithms and establish a fairer, more transparent ecosystem (Li, Fan, & Nie, 2025). These actions reflect the need to align technological innovation with social protection—a challenge faced equally by corporations, whose governance must incorporate mechanisms guaranteeing fairness, safety, and ethics in algorithm use. Algorithmic governance, therefore, is not merely a technical issue, but the creation of social and political frameworks to govern algorithmic agency for the common good (Schuett et al., 2025).

Conclusions

The study of algorithms in corporate governance reveals that they are not simple instruments, but active agents with agency in organizational practices. This duality impacts relationships, structures, and decision-making processes within organizations, giving rise to a new form of algorithmic governance that must be understood through socio-technical and actor-network perspectives.

First, the incorporation of algorithms into management systems—particularly in digital transformation and integrated management system (IMS) contexts—has enhanced efficiency, risk management, and transparency, supporting operational excellence and corporate sustainability. However, this integration also introduces challenges related to data quality, model interpretation, and the need for an organizational culture geared toward responsible use.

Second, actor-network theories and socio-technical approaches have helped explain how algorithms co-constitute organizational practices alongside human actors, creating power dynamics and modes of visibility and invisibility that affect decision-making and execution. This co-constitution means algorithmic agency is not an inherent property of technology, but an emergent outcome of interactions between individuals, institutional rules, and technological artifacts.

Third, evidence from analyzed documents indicates that algorithms are involved in corporate governance not merely as automation tools, but as actors shaping internal policies, supervising processes, and assessing risks. Yet, this algorithmic involvement requires regulatory frameworks and transparency audits to mitigate the risks of bias and discrimination in automated systems.

Fourth, algorithmic governance is a multidisciplinary field encompassing technical, ethical, legal, and social dimensions, where designers, managers, and regulators share responsibility for mitigating risks and promoting fair, sustainable technological development. In this regard, establishing ethics committees and specialized audit bodies becomes an essential practice to ensure algorithmic agency serves organizational governance.

Ultimately, reformulating organizational governance with algorithmic agency requires reimagining traditional power and control structures, promoting collaborative and adaptive models where AI functions as a partner in decision-making. This perspective opens avenues to research how algorithmic agency can be humanized and contextualized to safeguard human autonomy and dignity while driving organizational resilience and innovation in the digital era.

In summary, this work provides a theoretical framework for understanding human-algorithm interactions in corporate governance and emphasizes the need for comprehensive models that account for algorithmic agency while fostering ethical, transparent, and sustainable governance in the digital age. Further research bridging theory and practice is warranted to translate these insights into public policies and organizational strategies for responsible algorithmic governance.

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to its design and scope that should be considered when interpreting its results. First, the study employs a qualitative, exploratory-descriptive approach based on documentary review. While this method deepens conceptual and theoretical understanding of algorithms in corporate governance, it restricts the generalizability of findings to broader or different corporate contexts, as it lacks quantitative data and statistical analysis to empirically confirm proposed relationships (Ahmed, 2026; Jianu et al., 2025).

Second, source and case selection were limited to literature and evidence available up to 2025; subsequent technological and regulatory developments may alter or expand the dynamics examined. Furthermore, variations in algorithmic adoption and sophistication across sectors and regions may introduce nuances not captured in this study (Singla et al., 2025a; Singla et al., 2025b).

Additionally, the algorithmic black box—biases and lack of transparency—impedes in-depth critical analysis, as many organizations restrict access to proprietary or confidential information, preventing external auditing and evaluation (Bustelo, 2025; Pi et al., 2025). This limitation affects the capacity to test algorithmic governance theory against robust empirical evidence.

Finally, while socio-technical and actor-network perspectives enrich the understanding of algorithmic agency, they may overlook other organizational, cultural, or political factors shaping corporate governance, underscoring the need for future interdisciplinary research combining quantitative and qualitative methodologies to achieve a more comprehensive view (Swarup, 2025).

In conclusion, these limitations suggest that findings should be interpreted as an exploratory conceptualization that paves the way for future research using mixed methods and broader samples to strengthen evidence on how algorithms and organizational governance interact in digital transformation.
