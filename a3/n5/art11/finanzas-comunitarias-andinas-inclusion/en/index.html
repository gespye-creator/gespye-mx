Andean community finance and financial inclusion in Latin America

Abstract.

This article examines the interaction between Andean community-based financial practices and contemporary financial inclusion policies in Latin America. Its purpose is to analyze how two financial systems grounded in different normative foundations relate to one another: one based on principles of reciprocity and cooperation, and another oriented toward economic formalization and individual risk assessment. The study employs a documentary review that integrates anthropological literature on Andean community institutions, research on the Buen Vivir framework, and recent evidence on financial inclusion in the region. The findings show areas of convergence in basic economic functions, such as risk management and resource provision, as well as tensions arising from institutional and cultural differences. The study concludes that effective articulation between the two systems requires intercultural approaches that recognize local forms of economic organization and adapt financial inclusion policies to existing community practices.

Keywords:

financial inclusion; Andean community practices; reciprocity; Buen Vivir; rural finance.

Resumen.

El artículo analiza la interacción entre las prácticas financieras comunitarias andinas y las políticas contemporáneas de inclusión financiera en América Latina. Su propósito es examinar cómo se relacionan dos sistemas financieros basados en fundamentos normativos distintos, uno sustentado en principios de reciprocidad y cooperación, y otro orientado a la formalización económica y la evaluación individual del riesgo. La metodología consiste en una revisión documental que integra literatura antropológica sobre instituciones comunitarias andinas, estudios sobre el enfoque del Buen Vivir y evidencia reciente sobre inclusión financiera en la región. Los resultados muestran convergencias en funciones económicas básicas, como la gestión de riesgos y la provisión de recursos, pero también tensiones derivadas de diferencias institucionales y culturales. El estudio concluye que la articulación entre ambos sistemas requiere enfoques interculturales que reconozcan las formas locales de organización económica y adapten las políticas de inclusión financiera a las prácticas comunitarias existentes.

Palabras clave:

inclusión financiera; prácticas comunitarias andinas; reciprocidad; buen vivir; finanzas rurales.

Introduction

Financial inclusion has established itself as a relevant component in economic development and poverty reduction strategies. Available evidence shows that access to and use of formal financial services contributes to improving savings capacity, reducing vulnerability, and facilitating participation in productive activities. Recent studies confirm that financial inclusion has positive effects on economic well-being and that its advancement depends on institutional, technological, and regulatory factors (Demir, Pesque-Cela, Altunbas, & Murinde, 2022; Demirguc-Kunt, Klapper, Singer, Ansar, & Hess, 2018; Dupas, Karlan, Robinson, & Ubfal, 2018; Feghali, Mora, & Nassif, 2021).

In Latin America, however, access levels remain below the global average, and significant gaps persist between urban and rural populations, as well as between indigenous and non-indigenous groups (Martínez, Guercio, Orazi, & Vigier, 2022; Náñez Alonso, 2024; Orazi, Guercio, Martínez, & Vigier, 2023).

In this context, several multilateral organizations have pointed out that the financial exclusion of indigenous peoples and rural communities cannot be explained solely by income, distance, or infrastructure factors. Cultural, institutional, and territorial elements influence how these populations relate to the formal financial system and condition their willingness to use it (González-Vega, Ariza-Garzón, Mo, & Gómez-Soto, 2024; Villacorta, Azar, & Us, 2025). This situation is especially relevant in the Andes, where community economic practices that perform functions of savings, credit, and redistribution within their own cultural frameworks persist. Recent anthropological studies show that institutions such as ayni, minka, and other forms of reciprocity organize economic relations based on cooperation, co-responsibility, and the sustenance of community life (Faas, 2017; Walsh-Dilley, 2017; Wilhoit, 2017; Wutich, Beresford, & Carvajal, 2017). These institutions continue to articulate economic support networks within rural and indigenous communities and maintain relevance across various Andean territories.

The coexistence between Andean cultural-based community financial practices and formal financial inclusion policies has received little attention in academic literature. Although state strategies and programs promoted by multilateral organizations seek to expand access to and use of financial services, they sometimes do so without considering the normative and relational systems that organize traditional community finance. The absence of an intercultural perspective can generate tensions between institutional objectives of formalization and the logics of reciprocity that sustain community economic relations. In parallel, the Buen Vivir framework and other Andean conceptual frameworks have questioned the limitations of conventional development and propose analyzing the economy based on criteria of territorial balance, sustainability, and social relationality (Gudynas, 2011; Vanhulst & Beling, 2014; Villalba, 2013). Consequently, there is an analytical space to examine how both systems relate and what their areas of convergence and tension are.

The objective of this article is to analyze the articulation between traditional financial practices in Andean communities and contemporary financial inclusion policies in Latin America. A documentary review is presented that integrates anthropological literature, studies on Buen Vivir, and empirical work on financial inclusion. The article identifies the institutional logics that structure Andean community finance and contrasts them with predominant approaches in formal financial inclusion. Based on this analysis, it seeks to explain the conditions that facilitate or hinder convergence between both systems and discuss implications for public policy design.

The article is structured in five sections. The first presents the conceptual framework on the Andean worldview, reciprocal practices, and Buen Vivir approaches. The second describes the characteristics of community financial institutions and their economic functions. The third outlines contemporary financial inclusion approaches based on recent literature and evidence available for Latin America. The fourth discusses convergences and tensions between both systems using documented case studies. Finally, conclusions are presented alongside avenues for future research.

Conceptual framework

Andean worldview and economic principles

The Andean worldview constitutes a relational system in which the bonds between individuals, the community, and the environment organize social life and guide economic practices. Anthropological literature has documented that cooperation, reciprocity, and social equilibrium are central dimensions of this system, expressed through institutions that regulate non-market exchanges and mutual aid practices. Recent studies show that these institutions maintain relevance in various Andean territories and continue to articulate forms of economic organization based on cooperation and community co-responsibility (Faas, 2017; Walsh-Dilley, 2017; Wilhoit, 2017; Wutich et al., 2017).

The principle of reciprocity constitutes a fundamental normative pillar within this system. Reciprocity is not limited to equivalent exchanges; rather, it shapes long-lasting, sequential, and socially contextualized relationships. These relationships generate mutual obligations and ensure the reproduction of the community. Ethnographic research has shown that ayni, understood as deferred reciprocal exchange, and minka, as collective labor for common benefit, sustain processes of cooperation that make it possible to face risks, manage agricultural labor, and ensure resource redistribution in contexts marked by environmental variability and productive constraints (Faas, 2017; Wutich et al., 2017).

The emphasis on relationality and the maintenance of collective life is also present in studies on economic adaptations of pre-Hispanic societies in the Andes. Archaeological evidence suggests that the economic organization of pre-state Andean societies relied on extensive cooperation systems and ritual mechanisms that reinforced mutual aid obligations under demanding environmental conditions (Cruz, 2022). Complementarily, historical analyses of the Inca economic order have noted that state redistribution and organized labor mobilization responded to a logic distinct from individual accumulation, aiming instead to maintain territorial cohesion and collective security (D’Altroy, 2019).

Together, these studies show that Andean economic life is organized around relational principles that structure obligations, responsibilities, and forms of cooperation. These principles are key to understanding the functioning of community financial practices examined in the following subsection and provide an interpretive framework to analyze their interaction with contemporary financial inclusion policies.

Community financial practices

Community financial practices in the Andes constitute functional mechanisms for resource management, risk mitigation, and internal redistribution. These practices emerge from social institutions operating under norms of reciprocity, collective work, and established cooperation networks, forming local financial systems with their own rules. Anthropological literature demonstrates that these institutions organize labor and resource flows that fulfill functions equivalent to savings, credit, and informal insurance in other rural contexts (Walsh-Dilley, 2017; Wutich et al., 2017).

Prominent among these practices are deferred reciprocal exchange mechanisms that coordinate material support and community labor. Ayni functions as a bilateral or multilateral cooperation system in which received labor or resources generate a proportional future obligation. Minka is based on organized collective labor directed toward shared goals, such as agricultural activities, infrastructure maintenance, or support during community events. Both mechanisms sustain local economic reproduction and allow temporary needs to be managed without resorting to external intermediaries. Ethnographic studies show that these practices operate as resource provision networks during critical moments, reducing individual exposure to environmental variability or productive shocks (Faas, 2017).

Other forms of exchange documented in literature include traditional redistributive systems in local fairs and markets. Research in the Peruvian-Bolivian altiplano indicates that exchanges in traditional markets preserve elements of reciprocity and social balance that coexist with monetary transactions, reinforcing the community circulation of goods (Gallardo, 2013). These spaces perform functions complementary to cooperation networks and operate as access mechanisms for goods essential to household sustenance.

Archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence also indicates that financial and redistributive cooperation structures existed in pre-state Andean societies and persisted under subsequent systems. In contexts marked by climate uncertainty, these community networks provided risk management mechanisms and ensured the availability of collective labor and basic resources (Cruz, 2022). Complementarily, analysis of the Inca economic order documents a redistributive system organized from domestic units and administrative centers that guaranteed material security and political cohesion throughout the territory (D’Altroy, 2019).

Although these practices do not constitute formal financial systems, they fulfill similar functions in terms of liquidity provision, resource access, risk diversification, and support network maintenance. The presence of these practices endures across several rural Andean areas and is closely tied to the worldview of indigenous populations prioritizing the strengthening of community ties.

The Buen Vivir approach and alternatives to development

The Buen Vivir approach has established itself as a proposal that critically reviews the foundations of conventional development. Its formulation incorporates discussions on sustainability, collective rights, and interculturality, and has been applied to the study of various public policies across Latin America. Specialized literature notes that this approach introduces well-being criteria emphasizing a balanced relationship between society and the natural environment, as well as community forms of economic and social organization (Gudynas, 2011).

Recent research agrees that Buen Vivir constitutes an expanding discourse challenging traditional notions of economic progress. Comparative studies show that it presents alternatives to models centered on economic growth by incorporating principles of relationality, sufficiency, and ecological sustainability (Vanhulst & Beling, 2014). These works argue that Buen Vivir should not be understood merely as a Western adaptation of sustainability, but as a framework articulating social, environmental, and cultural dimensions from indigenous perspectives.

Other analyses explore tensions between Buen Vivir and predominant extractivist models in the region. Literature has documented cases where policies linked to Buen Vivir conflict with large-scale projects, revealing implementation limits when facing economic structures dependent on primary exports (Villalba, 2013; Villalba-Eguiluz & Etxano, 2017). These studies point out that, despite tensions, Buen Vivir has generated conceptual frameworks that allow for a re-examination of the relationship between development, territory, and environmental governance.

Recent research has expanded this field by incorporating cosmo-political perspectives recognizing the agency of nature and proposing legal frameworks oriented toward comprehensive ecosystem protection. The concept of Kawsak Sacha, developed from Amazonian epistemologies, exemplifies these efforts by proposing alternative forms of environmental regulation prioritizing ecological integrity and community ties with the territory (Lyall, 2023). Such approaches complement prior debates on rights of nature and their articulation with indigenous visions of development.

Together, the Buen Vivir approach and associated debates provide a relevant conceptual framework to analyze the interaction between community financial practices and financial inclusion policies, as well as to understand economic relationships from a territorial perspective.

Western financial practices and contemporary economic-financial rationality

Contemporary economic-financial rationality in Western economies relies on a set of principles that have guided the formation of modern markets and financial institutions. This approach emphasizes the individualization of rights and obligations, the contractual formalization of economic relations, and the interpretation of economic behavior through rational calculations and incentives. Institutional literature indicates that these elements consolidate into formal frameworks establishing stable exchange rules, protecting property rights, and creating regulatory mechanisms designed to reduce uncertainty (North, 1991).

The Western financial system is organized around regulated intermediaries, such as banks, formalized cooperatives, and capital markets. Their operation aims to channel resources toward investment, manage liquidity, and administer risk through specialized instruments. Empirical evidence shows that the development of these formal markets is associated with economic growth processes, investment expansion, and deeper financial penetration (Beck & Levine, 2004).

Within this framework, credit plays a central role. Its provision relies on standardized risk assessments and criteria seeking to guarantee institutional stability. The expansion of formal credit depends on verifiable information regarding repayment capacity, credit history, and defined contractual terms, establishing relationships based on individual responsibilities (Banerjee & Duflo, 2010). These mechanisms facilitate the expansion of financial services, although they may exclude those unable to meet established requirements.

Microfinance and microcredit have extended these practices to segments with less access to traditional banking. Literature indicates that while they have broadened financing opportunities for low-income households, they have also reproduced contractual schemes centered on individual obligations and credit discipline (Roodman, 2012). Complementarily, studies on credit effects on labor participation confirm that these products operate under incentives and structures characteristic of conventional financial rationality (Karlan, Osei, Osei-Akoto, & Udry, 2016).

The historical foundations of this rationality have been analyzed from economic sociology. A classic reading links the emergence of modern capitalism to social and cultural organizational forms prioritizing rational accumulation, resource management discipline, and individual economic planning (Weber, 2001). These elements remain present in current financial structures and guide product design, risk assessment, and operational regulation.

The set of practices described forms a financial system governed by principles distinct from those sustaining the community institutions analyzed earlier. Comparing both allows for a clear distinction between a rationality grounded in relational obligations and one structured around contractual commitments. This contrast is central to examining points of convergence and friction when both systems meet within the same territory, particularly where community financial practices remain active alongside public policies aimed at formalizing and expanding the financial system.

Financial inclusion: contemporary approaches and evidence for Latin America

Financial inclusion has become a key priority within policies aimed at poverty reduction and economic development. Specialized literature notes that this concept encompasses not only access to formal financial services but also their continuous usage and product quality—elements impacting household capacity to manage risk, mobilize resources, and sustain productive activities. Various studies document global progress tied to financial infrastructure growth, digital technology adoption, and payment system innovations (Demir et al., 2022).

In Latin America, however, significant gaps persist in both access and usage of financial services, disproportionately affecting rural and indigenous populations. Available evidence shows that bank account ownership, credit access, and formal payment tool usage remain low among these groups, influenced by labor informality, transaction costs, and physical distance to service points. Regional survey-based research also indicates that financial inclusion is closely linked to sociodemographic traits, institutional trust levels, and digital infrastructure availability (Martínez et al., 2022; Orazi et al., 2023).

Literature also emphasizes the role of technological innovation in lowering access barriers. The expansion of digital platforms, electronic wallets, and remote authentication mechanisms has enabled financial service delivery in areas with limited traditional banking infrastructure. Recent research shows technology can lower costs, facilitate transactions, and broaden accessible financial product offerings for excluded segments, though outcomes depend on institutional capacity to regulate risk and ensure adequate consumer protection (Dhahri, Omri, & Mirza, 2024).

In parallel, financial stability research indicates that expanding financial inclusion requires sound institutional conditions, as bank market structure and systemic regulation influence incentives to serve excluded segments. International evidence suggests more competitive market structures tend to generate more accessible products, provided regulatory frameworks safeguard systemic stability and mitigate over-indebtedness or predatory lending risks (Feghali et al., 2021).

Specifically in Latin America, studies document that financial inclusion correlates with education level, digital access, and institutional trust. Regional trend analyses indicate uneven progress across countries, with indigenous financial inclusion lagging compared to other demographic groups, even when controlling for income and location (Uruena Mejía, 2018). These findings underscore the need to incorporate cultural and institutional dimensions to understand the adoption or rejection of formal financial services, especially in areas with strong community financial traditions.

Available evidence provides a foundation for analyzing how financial inclusion policies interact with pre-existing community practices. The following section examines these interactions, identifying convergences and tensions between both systems.

Convergences and tensions between community financial practices and financial inclusion policies

Comparative analysis between Andean community financial practices and contemporary financial inclusion policies highlights key areas of convergence and tension. Both systems aim to enhance household capability to manage resources, navigate contingencies, and support productive endeavors, yet operate under distinct logics. Financial inclusion literature emphasizes formal mechanisms providing access to regulated services governed by safety and stability standards (Feghali et al., 2021). Conversely, community practices stem from social relationships of reciprocity and cooperation, prioritizing relational obligations and community support.

A primary point of convergence is observed in the economic function both systems fulfill. Community practices like ayni or minka generate mechanisms for resource provision, risk diversification, and mutual support functionally comparable to basic financial products like savings, credit, or informal insurance. Anthropological studies document how these networks manage short-term financial needs and sustain productive activities through community labor and resource mobilization (Faas, 2017; Wutich et al., 2017). From this perspective, financial inclusion and community finance share goals related to vulnerability reduction and economic capability expansion.

Despite functional convergences, literature highlights major tensions rooted in normative and institutional differences. Financial inclusion policies operate within regulatory frameworks emphasizing individual user responsibility, transaction formalization, and supervisory compliance. In contrast, community practices structure around relational obligations extending beyond equivalent exchange, deriving legitimacy from specific cultural norms. Latin American indigenous studies show that the divide between these systems fosters distrust toward formal financial institutions and limits regulated service adoption, even when physically accessible (Villacorta et al., 2025).

Another tension involves risk assessment criteria. In community practices, trust rests on familiar social networks and long-standing reciprocal commitments. In formal financial systems, risk assessment relies on standardized metrics, credit histories, and strict documentation. This divergence can exclude households active in community systems who lack adequate formal records for regulated products. Regional comparative research indicates these gaps disproportionately affect indigenous populations (González-Vega et al., 2024).

Tensions also exist regarding public policy development objectives. The Buen Vivir perspective advocates well-being criteria centered on relationality and social balance, contrasting with policies promoting credit expansion, banking penetration, and economic formalization. Research on conflicts between community practices and state policies shows growth-driven development models can prove incompatible with local territorial visions, particularly when financial programs align with production models unsuited to local traditions (Vanhulst & Beling, 2014; Villalba, 2013).

Recent studies point to opportunities for creating hybrid models linking formal and community systems. Rural development programs such as Haku Wiñay/Noa Jayatai, executed by FONCODES in Peru, have combined productive transfers with local organizational structures, demonstrating positive impacts on autonomous incomes among extreme poverty rural households (Escobal & Ponce, 2016). Village banking methodology—applied by FINCA in Ecuador, CRECER and Pro Mujer in Bolivia, and Microfinanzas Arariwa in Peru—operates on solidarity group principles acting as guarantee and financial discipline mechanisms, involving users in collective credit management (Maclean, 2012; Westley, 2004). In the cooperative realm, the history of Cooperativa de Ahorro y Crédito Jardín Azuayo in southern Ecuador illustrates a social and solidarity finance process anchored in self-management and territorial governance (Salinas Vásquez, 2023). Literature warns, however, that the success of such initiatives depends on adapting to local cultural norms to prevent eroding existing reciprocal practices (Dhahri et al., 2024). These experiences suggest that convergence between both systems requires intercultural institutional frameworks recognizing community economic management forms.

Conclusions

The analysis demonstrates that Andean community financial practices and contemporary financial inclusion policies stem from different institutional and normative frameworks. The former rely on principles of reciprocity, cooperation, and relationality that organize resource access, risk distribution, and community life sustenance. These mechanisms perform essential economic functions and maintain vitality across diverse Andean territories as local financial systems built on distinct cultural norms and strong social networks.

Financial inclusion policies develop within regulatory frameworks seeking to promote economic formalization, expand access to supervised financial products, and ensure user protection through state oversight structures. Available literature shows these policies have helped improve financial access levels across Latin America, though significant gaps remain for rural and indigenous populations. The disconnect between community practices and formal services translates into differing trust criteria, risk evaluation methods, and perceived product functions.

Comparative analysis reveals shared goals regarding vulnerability reduction and resource management, alongside tensions related to normative foundations and underlying social organizational forms. The Buen Vivir approach offers a valuable framework for interpreting these tensions by proposing well-being and economic concepts distinct from growth-centered development models.

Study results indicate that bridging community practices and financial inclusion policies requires intercultural approaches recognizing local economic organization models and adapting institutional tools to territorial realities. Integrating community elements into financial programs and accounting for cultural norms in product design can facilitate alignment between both systems and reduce persistent gaps.

Translating this alignment into practice requires concrete operational directives beyond general recognition. Three priority areas emerge. First, designing hybrid financial products that accept community guarantees (solidarity guarantees and reciprocal commitments inspired by ayni) as valid substitutes for individual collateral required by formal banks. Second, implementing intercultural financial education programs rooted in pre-existing reciprocity and redistribution logics, rather than imposing literacy narratives that tacitly invalidate community financial knowledge. Third, providing legal recognition for entities like community funds, boards, pasanakus, and rondas through adapted legal status frameworks allowing formal system interaction without compromising their organizational integrity.

Future research should deepen comparative analyses across Andean territories, evaluate financial technology impacts on community network dynamics, and examine empirical outcomes of hybrid financial inclusion models. The diversity of indigenous territories in Ecuador and the broader region enables comparative studies based on geographic zones, indigenous versus mestizo population density, and depth of ancestral practices. Such research will provide actionable evidence for designing public policies that embrace cultural diversity and bolster local economic capabilities.
