Neurobehavioral effects of brands on consumer choice

Efectos neuroconductuales de las marcas en la elección del consumidor

Abstract.

The study analyzes the neurobehavioral effects of brands on consumer choice, considering the conscious and unconscious processes that influence decision-making. Experimental research with a quantitative approach and neuroscientific techniques, including eye-tracking, was conducted to measure visual attention, emotional response, and preference toward different brand stimuli. The sample consisted of young consumers with normal vision, exposed to images and logos of well-known brands. The results show that brands activate automatic mechanisms of attention and emotion that influence product evaluation and preference, even without rational mediation. It is concluded that brands operate as neurobehavioral stimuli capable of modulating cognitive and affective processes, reinforcing their role in shaping perceived value and predicting consumer choice behavior.

Keywords:

brands; decision-making; consumer neuroscience; unconscious processing.

Resumen.

El estudio analiza los efectos neuroconductuales de las marcas en la elección del consumidor, considerando los procesos conscientes e inconscientes que influyen en la toma de decisiones. Se desarrolló una investigación experimental con enfoque cuantitativo y técnicas neurocientíficas, entre ellas el seguimiento ocular, para medir la atención visual, la respuesta emocional y la preferencia ante distintos estímulos de marca. La muestra estuvo conformada por jóvenes consumidores con visión normal, expuestos a imágenes y logotipos de marcas reconocidas. Los resultados evidencian que las marcas activan mecanismos automáticos de atención y emoción que influyen en la evaluación y preferencia del producto, aun sin intervención racional. Se concluye que las marcas operan como estímulos neuroconductuales capaces de modular procesos cognitivos y afectivos, reforzando su papel en la formación de valor percibido y en la predicción del comportamiento de elección del consumidor.

Palabras clave:

marcas; toma de decisiones; neurociencia del consumidor; procesamiento inconsciente.

Introduction

Brands constitute one of the main strategic assets of organizations and an essential component in building value and preference in contemporary markets. Beyond their identifying function, brands act as symbols that condense meanings, emotions, and experiences capable of influencing consumer perception, memory, and behavior (Keller, 2013; Aaker, 1996).

Advances in consumer neuroscience have made it possible to understand that purchasing decisions do not originate exclusively from rational processes, but also from automatic and unconscious mechanisms determined by brain activity and prior experience (Ariely & Berns, 2010; Plassmann, Ramsøy, & Milosavljevic, 2012). These findings challenge the classical model of the rational consumer and reinforce the vision of bounded rationality proposed by Simon (1982), according to which individuals make decisions based on heuristics, biases, and emotions rather than deliberate analysis.

From this perspective, brands act as cognitive shortcuts that reduce uncertainty and facilitate choice, influencing the emotional and symbolic valuation of products (Kahneman, 2012; Schmitt, 2012). Recent studies demonstrate that exposure to brand stimuli activates brain areas related to pleasure, trust, and reward, such as the nucleus accumbens and the orbitofrontal cortex, conditioning preference even before the individual is aware of their decision (Reimann et al., 2011; Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015).

In this context, consumer neuroscience emerges as a key tool for understanding the conscious and unconscious mechanisms that mediate between perception and brand choice. This perspective is particularly valuable for organizations, allowing them to design communication and branding strategies based on scientific evidence regarding attention, emotion, and implicit memory (Lindstrom, 2011; Morin, 2011).

The purpose of this study is to analyze the neurobehavioral effects of brands on consumer choice, considering the interaction between conscious and unconscious processes that determine visual attention, emotional response, and preference. Through a neuroscientific experimental approach, it seeks to provide empirical evidence on how brands influence purchasing decisions, contributing to both the theoretical development of the field and managerial practice in competitive contexts.

Methodology

Research Design

The research was developed under a laboratory experimental design with a quantitative, explanatory, and neurobehavioral approach aimed at analyzing the relationship between brands, visual attention, and consumer emotional response. This type of design allows for observing the direct influence of controlled variables on behavior and physiological processes, ensuring a high level of internal validity.

The study is grounded in contributions from cognitive neuroscience and behavioral economics, which explain decision-making as the result of the interaction between conscious and unconscious processes (Kahneman, 2012; Simon, 1982). The selection of this approach responds to the need to obtain objective measurements that complement traditional marketing techniques, reducing self-report bias and providing empirical evidence on the influence of brands on attention and emotion.

Participants

The sample consisted of thirty young adults, men and women aged 20 to 28, all with normal or corrected vision. None of the participants presented visual or neurological alterations that could compromise measurement reliability. The selection was intentional and non-probabilistic, seeking homogeneity in demographic variables and familiarity with the evaluated product categories.

Prior to the start of the study, participants were informed of the general objectives of the research and signed an informed consent form in accordance with the ethical principles of the American Psychological Association (2010). Confidentiality of the data, voluntary participation, and the absence of physical or psychological risks during experimental exposure were guaranteed.

Although the sample size of thirty participants is appropriate for an eye-tracking experimental study due to technical requirements for control, calibration, and individual physiological recording, it is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. Consequently, results should be interpreted as exploratory empirical evidence rather than definitive generalizations regarding the entire consumer population. Likewise, concentrating the sample on young adults aged 20 to 28 defines the scope of the findings and opens possibilities for future research with larger samples, diverse age groups, and differentiated sociodemographic profiles.

For recording physiological indicators, a high-precision eye-tracking device with a sampling frequency of 120 Hz was used, registering ocular fixation, saccadic movements, and pupil dilation. These variables served as indirect indicators of attention, cognitive load, and emotional arousal, aligning with previous studies in consumer neuroscience (Plassmann, Ramsøy, & Milosavljevic, 2012; Wedel & Pieters, 2008).

Experimental stimuli consisted of images of logos and products from recognized and unrecognized brands, carefully selected based on criteria of familiarity, category, and presence in the Ecuadorian market. Each image was projected for five seconds, with an inter-stimulus interval of two seconds to prevent visual fatigue and ensure attentional recovery between exposures.

Procedure

The experiment took place in a controlled laboratory environment with constant lighting and without visual or auditory distractors. Before the main session, a calibration phase of the eye-tracker was conducted to adjust ocular recording accuracy to each participant's individual characteristics.

Each subject was individually exposed to the image sequence on a high-resolution screen. During exposure, eye movements and pupillary variations were recorded and subsequently analyzed to determine attention patterns and emotional arousal. At the conclusion of the experimental session, a structured questionnaire was administered to contrast implicit responses (attention and emotion) with declared responses of brand preference and recall.

Data were processed using SPSS software version 26.0, applying descriptive analyses, Student's t-tests for paired samples, and Pearson correlations to identify significant differences in attention and emotion levels between recognized and unrecognized brands.

Reliability and Validity

Instrumental reliability was verified through pilot sessions with three participants, obtaining a 95% accuracy margin in equipment calibration. Internal validity was ensured by controlling the stimulus sequence, environmental conditions, and constant lighting throughout the procedure. External validity was supported by using real brands from the Ecuadorian market familiar to the participants, favoring the generalizability of results to real consumption contexts.

Compliance with ethical and methodological principles, together with procedural replicability, ensures the consistency of the experimental design and its relevance for the neurobehavioral analysis of purchasing decisions.

Results and Discussion

Attentional and Emotional Processing of Brands

The study results demonstrate clear differences in the attentional and emotional processing of recognized versus unrecognized brands. Participants showed greater ocular fixation and higher pupil dilation in response to familiar brands, indicating sustained attention and more intense emotional activation. These implicit responses confirm that brand familiarity facilitates visual processing and increases consumer affective involvement, reinforcing the hypothesis that brands act as stimuli of high cognitive and emotional salience.

The heat maps obtained from visual tracking show that areas of highest fixation (in red) concentrate on the logos and central elements of the Don Manuelito® and Galletti® brands, evidencing a more intense pattern of attention toward recognized stimuli. These results align with the findings of Reimann et al. The evidence obtained reinforces the idea that visual attention and emotion function as integrated mechanisms in brand valuation.

Brain Activation and Symbolic Value of the Brand

From a neurobehavioral perspective, these results are explained by the activation of brain systems responsible for reward and affective memory. Previous research has shown that recognized brands tend to activate regions such as the orbitofrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens, associated with pleasure and trust (Reimann et al., 2011; Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015). This activation reflects the emotional and symbolic value consumers attribute to the brand, generating an automatic positive response even before conscious choice occurs.

Statistical Significance and Correlations

Statistical analysis confirmed that observed differences were significant in terms of attention and emotional response levels, with positive correlations between ocular fixation time and declared preference. This suggests that longer visual processing is associated with higher emotional valuation, in line with the propositions of Plassmann, Ramsøy, & Milosavljevic (2012), who maintain that emotion acts as a key modulator of decision-making.

Integration of Conscious and Unconscious Processes

The correspondence between physiological data (pupillary and ocular responses) and preference declarations demonstrates that conscious and unconscious responses operate in an integrated manner, rather than as isolated processes. Purchasing decisions, therefore, do not arise exclusively from rational deliberation, but from dynamic interaction among attention, emotion, and memory. This finding reaffirms the model of bounded rationality (Simon, 1982) and Kahneman's (2012) proposals on the coexistence of two thinking systems: one fast, automatic, and intuitive, and the other slow and analytical.

Behavioral and Brand Management Implications

In terms of consumer behavior, results confirm that brands with established presence and recognition generate a more intense and positive emotional response, increasing their likelihood of choice. This translates into higher perceived brand equity and a more favorable predisposition toward repurchase or recommendation. As Ariely & Berns (2010) point out, emotion and prior experience determine a large portion of preferences, even in the absence of conscious reflection.

These findings provide empirical evidence regarding the decisive role of emotion in brand perception and choice, highlighting the importance of integrating neuroscientific metrics into consumer behavior research. Implications for brand management are substantial: understanding the unconscious processes involved in preference enables designing more persuasive communication strategies aligned with actual decision-making mechanisms.

Overall, the study demonstrates that exposure to recognized brands activates neurobehavioral mechanisms associated with visual attention, emotion, and preference, consolidating the relationship between unconscious processing and brand equity building. This knowledge contributes to the theoretical advancement of neuromarketing and provides practical tools to strengthen the emotional connection between brands and consumers.

Limitations

Although the experimental design allowed controlling exposure, recording, and analysis conditions for visual stimuli, the research presents limitations that should be considered when interpreting results. The sample size of thirty participants, while acceptable for eye-tracking studies due to technical requirements for calibration, individual recording, and experimental control, limits the generalizability of findings to broader populations. Furthermore, the sample was composed of young adults aged 20 to 28; thus, results primarily reflect the neurobehavioral behavior of this age group and not necessarily that of consumers of other ages.

The study evaluated a limited number of brands and visual stimuli, which maintained methodological control but restricts the comparative scope of the results. Future research could expand sample size and diversity, incorporate consumers from different generational groups, and compare a larger number of brands, product categories, and consumption contexts. These expansions would allow testing the stability of observed effects and strengthening the external validity of findings.

Conclusions

The study identified empirical evidence that recognized brands generate higher attentional and emotional activation than unrecognized ones, demonstrating the influence of conscious and unconscious processes on consumer choice. Results support the premise that purchasing decisions are not explained solely by rationality, but by constant interaction between emotion, memory, and visual attention.

From a neurobehavioral perspective, exposure to brand stimuli triggers automatic reactions in brain areas linked to reward and pleasure, explaining the preference for brands with an established history or symbolic meaning. This finding reinforces the need to incorporate neuroscientific methodologies into consumer behavior research to achieve a deeper understanding of the mechanisms mediating perception and action.

In brand management, results demonstrate that a brand's strength lies as much in its emotional value as in its cognitive positioning. Companies that connect with the consumer's affective dimension increase their recall capacity and customer loyalty potential. Consequently, marketing strategies should aim to design sensory experiences consistent with brand values capable of triggering positive emotional responses.

Methodologically, the research demonstrates the relevance of using tools such as eye-tracking to analyze visual attention and emotional responses, providing complementary information to traditional self-report techniques. This combination strengthens the validity of consumer behavior studies and opens new avenues for analyzing the relationship between brand stimuli and brain activation.

The study provides empirical evidence on the role of conscious and unconscious mechanisms in brand preference formation and contributes to the development of neuromarketing as both an applied and scientific discipline. Future research could examine comparisons across product categories, consumer genders, or cultural contexts to broaden understanding of how brands influence decision-making in various consumption scenarios.
