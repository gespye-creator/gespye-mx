Abstract.

The purpose of this research was to analyze the performance of the Popular and Solidarity Economy (PSE) production associations in the Ecuadorian Sozoranga canton and their relationship with the cantonal government (GAD), in order to guide the development of an organizational strengthening plan. A descriptive methodology with a mixed-methods approach was used, employing surveys administered to members of four associations and interviews with municipal authorities, complemented by a document review. This information was used to construct the EFE, EFI, and SWOT matrices to evaluate internal and external factors of the associative sector. Among the main findings were a high productive potential and strong community ties, but also weaknesses in administrative management, infrastructure, financial literacy, and marketing. Furthermore, limited intervention by the GAD in directly supporting the PSE was evident. The research concludes that strengthening the sector requires greater institutional coordination, strategic planning, and public policies aimed at consolidating organizational and productive capacities.

Keywords:

Popular and Solidarity Economy, Ecuador, associativity, situational diagnosis.

Resumen.

La investigación tuvo como propósito analizar el desempeño de las asociaciones productivas de la Economía Popular y Solidaria (EPS) del cantón Sozoranga de Ecuador y su articulación con el GAD cantonal, con el fin de orientar un plan de fortalecimiento organizacional. Se empleó una metodología descriptiva con enfoque mixto, recurriendo a encuestas aplicadas a 69 socios de 4 asociaciones y entrevistas a autoridades municipales, complementadas con revisión documental. Con esta información se construyeron las matrices EFE, EFI y FODA para evaluar factores internos y externos del sector asociativo. Entre los principales hallazgos se identificó un alto potencial productivo y arraigo comunitario, pero también debilidades en gestión administrativa, infraestructura, cultura financiera y comercialización. Asimismo, se evidenció limitada intervención del GAD en el fomento directo a la EPS. La investigación concluye que el fortalecimiento del sector requiere mayor articulación institucional, planificación estratégica y políticas públicas orientadas a consolidar capacidades organizativas y productivas.

Introduction

The Popular and Solidarity Economy (PSE) in Ecuador represents a strategic sector for local development, whose emphasis is the improvement of people's living conditions through job creation, social cohesion, and the revival of principles of solidarity, cooperation, gender equity, and environmental care.

However, PSE organizations face multiple challenges that limit their performance and sustainability, revolving around restrictions in accessing continuous training and permanent technical assistance, as well as financing from the national financial system (IEPS, 2018). Furthermore, in the productive sphere, quality and quantity have not been secured, minimizing their bargaining power and appropriate marketing channels. Added to this are the insufficient administrative skills and abilities of management to lead the organizations' operations.

The case study of this research is geographically focused on the Sozoranga canton, located in Loja province (Southern Ecuador). This canton has a population mainly settled in rural areas (GAD Sozoranga, 2020). In the productive sector, corn, coffee, grain, and cereal crops stand out, while livestock farming focuses on cattle and goat breeding. For its part, the existence of financial institutions in Sozoranga canton is limited to the public bank designated for productive activities – BanEcuador – and the savings and credit cooperative CoopMego Ltda.

The PSE in the canton is mainly linked to the non-financial sector of the economy (real sector). This group includes a transport cooperative, several popular economic units of artisans, and sole proprietorships and family businesses, as well as productive associations (GAD Sozoranga, 2020; surveys applied with updated information to 2025).

In Sozoranga canton, associations are the most representative forms of organization, constituting the focus of the study. This aspect supports the analysis of the link between PSE associations and cantonal government administration, with the Autonomous Decentralized Government (GAD) of the canton being the institution fully authorized to promote and strengthen these types of organizations. It should be noted that the main regulation governing the PSE – Organic Law of Popular and Solidarity Economy (LOEPS) – establishes that this level of government is responsible for including PSE social actors in socioeconomic plans and projects (EC, 2011, art. 133).

Another regulatory framework emphasizing this requirement is the Organic Code of Territorial Organization, Autonomy, and Decentralization (COOTAD), which indicates that one of its functions is to promote local economic development processes with an emphasis on the social and solidarity economy sector, in coordination with other levels of government (EC, 2010).

Given these antecedents, the research objective arises: to analyze the economic-productive interaction of PSE associations in Sozoranga canton, considering, among other aspects, articulation actions with the cantonal GAD.

In addition to this introduction, the article consists of six chapters. The second section develops the conceptual framework. The third chapter provides a literature review on the analysis of productive associations and their relationship with GADs. The fourth chapter describes the methodological design; the fifth presents the results; the sixth discusses the study in comparison with prior research; and finally, the seventh chapter synthesizes the study's conclusions.

Conceptual Framework

The Social and Solidarity Economic System in Ecuador

The Constitution of Ecuador (EC, 2008, art. 283) defines the economic system as social and solidarity-based, whose approach, according to Coraggio (2009a), was formulated from a substantive conception of economics. Its objective is to guarantee the production and reproduction of material and immaterial conditions enabling good living (buen vivir). Therefore, the economic system is under construction, integrating a set of institutions, rules, practices, and values that organize production, distribution, circulation, and consumption processes, all bounded by relations of cooperation and care for nature.

The social feature is met when the market recognizes and operates according to social structures, values, and other elements ensuring the material and immaterial conditions aimed at achieving good living for people. Solidarity, on the other hand, comes from relationships of association, cooperation, and mutual responsibility, whose norms affirm the social role of economic actors and their co-responsibility toward citizenship, future generations, and nature (Coraggio 2009b, 7).

The composition of the economic system is specified in the Constitution of Ecuador: "the economic system shall be composed of public, private, mixed, popular, and solidarity economic organization forms, and others determined by the Constitution" (EC, 2008, art. 283). In this context, the public sector consists of state-owned institutions, including "entities that make up the decentralized autonomous regime through decentralized autonomous governments, the main entities responsible for the territorial organization of the country at regional, provincial, cantonal, and rural levels" (EC, 2008, art. 225).

Regarding the PSE sector, Coraggio (2009b) defines it as the set of productive and exchange activities carried out by workers and communities through associative, democratic, and cooperative forms, formal or traditional, whose purpose is to use labor as the main resource to generate goods, services, and income that sustain community life with dignity. In this definition, popular economic units (UEP) constitute the foundation of the popular economy, encompassing family businesses, people dedicated to care activities, and wage earners. Meanwhile, when considering the popular alongside the solidarity aspect, an integration behavior is recognized in which organizational forms practicing reciprocity and complementarity are distinguished, contributing whatever possible without being limited to monetary terms, and receiving from the collective whatever is needed in return.

It is important to mention that interactions can arise among the three sectors of the economy. For the purpose of this research, primary consideration is given to the interaction concerning the public management of cantonal GADs and their support for organizational forms within the PSE sector. In this sense, the view of Coraggio (2009a) regarding public policy formulation for the PSE is taken into account; it must not only address social economic structures but also seek non-destructive forms of competition among sector participants, maintaining coherence between needs and capacities. Consequently, its impact will be noticeable in the local environment. State action is thus conceived as generating spaces that link the operations of political-administrative institutions providing state goods and services with PSE social actors.

Associativity in the PSE

Associativity as a form of organization among workers seeking a common goal implies understanding the PSE as an organizational space. As an alternative form of socioeconomic development for local territories, it relies on promoting and fostering social and solidarity-based organizations.

The collective organizational space where associative initiatives develop—whether in a family, community, or labor environment—represents another element of associativity. The group of associating individuals constitutes social capital, characterized by maintaining social relationships of kinship, trust, mutual help, community work, and solidarity aimed at satisfying common needs (Araque Jaramillo 2018).

One characteristic of associativity is that it arises from the will of its members to address their needs, potentially becoming a strategy to improve productivity in local contexts and rural organizations. In this regard, Terán et al. (2023) mention that collaboration and coordination among different actors participating in the production chain guarantee resource optimization, product and service quality, and thereby foster an environment of economic diversification.

In this context, it is important to understand that an associative presence in communities promotes development, fostering social cohesion, collective work, and reciprocity; generating collaboration networks among actors; providing access to shared services and resources; strengthening cultural identity; and driving people to lead and participate in collective decisions.

Under LOEPS, the associative sector is defined as the set of associations of individuals with similar productive activities seeking to produce, sell, and consume necessary goods and services in a solidarity-oriented manner, as well as to self-manage supply and marketing for their production (EC, 2011). Thus, associative sector organizations can generate surpluses from their economic activity; however, equitable and solidarity-based distribution among associates is essential.

Decentralized Autonomous Governments and the PSE

With decentralization in Ecuador came changes in government administration, including assigning competencies to GADs, formerly known as municipalities. As decentralized institutions, they enjoy political, administrative, and financial autonomy.

This research considers cantonal GADs as the main promoters of territorial development—institutions closest to the community that understand territorial realities. LOEPS establishes that these entities must act according to principles of solidarity, equity, integration, and civic participation. Furthermore, this level of government is tasked with incorporating PSE social actors into socioeconomic plans and projects (EC, 2011, art. 133).

Regarding GAD participation in the development of PSE organizations locally, Caiza (2023) specifies that the regulatory framework and public policy implementation must go hand in hand to ensure effective protection of PSE organizations and individuals. Inaction in promotion, support, and incentive processes negatively impacts the sector's goals.

In this regard, local government administration can help create conditions connecting PSE actors while driving productive associativity, establishing dialogue channels, fostering value chain creation in the territory, and promoting technological innovation—all aimed at enhancing existing production systems and consequently the population's socioeconomic development (Villamarín 2023).

Prior Literature

Literature reviews reveal studies addressing the characteristics of PSE productive associations in Ecuador and their relationships with local GADs.

Villamarín (2023), focusing on agri-productive issues in Loreto canton, Orellana province, highlights cantonal and parish GAD participation with the associative sector through support in implementing tourism and agro-industrial productive projects. Cantonal GAD support occurred via financing machinery and equipment purchases, while parish GAD focused on production through inter-institutional agreements for technical assistance and member training, administrative and legal guidance, and promotion of agri-productive activities at parish fairs in Loreto.

Villamarín's (2023) findings position associations as key actors in local socioeconomic dynamics while highlighting organizational leadership effectiveness in engaging government entities, creating collaborative integration spaces between both actor groups.

While Merino et al. (2024) do not analyze productive association interaction with GADs, their study reveals important managerial characteristics in agricultural associations in La Troncal canton, Ecuador. Results show efficient administrative management characterized by proper strategic planning and member participation toward organizational strengthening. Conversely, low technology adoption and lack of financial training limit association competitiveness and sustainability. Additionally, the study notes positive impacts on local development through job creation and social well-being for members, families, and the broader community—which indirectly yields benefits and development for GADs.

A third study by Naranjo (2015) examines challenges faced by the provincial GAD of Tungurahua, Ecuador, in implementing a management model designed to engage political leaders, youth representatives, and social actors across provincial cantons and parishes. Among other activities, the author notes that the Union of Agroecological Producer and Associative Marketing Organizations of Tungurahua (PACAT), composed of farmers dedicated to agroecological production, proposed a marketing agricultural strategy including the dairy chain to help small producers associate for better sales. GAD intervention involved coordinating courses and training with the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock, and Fisheries on production diversification based on territorial characteristics. Likewise, within the "Work Parliament" driven by administration at that time, a tourism strategy based on an associative model was coordinated with municipal GADs, identifying nine agro-ecotourism routes.

Methodological Framework

General Overview

The research is descriptive, gathering and analyzing data from the studied associations through a quantitative and qualitative mixed-methods approach. Secondary information was compiled through literature and documentary review of theoretical foundations, applying deductive logic to describe Sozoranga canton's situational status. Primary data was gathered via two techniques: surveys administered to members of previously identified associations, and semi-structured interviews with cantonal GAD officials (the mayor, head of financial management, and head of urban and rural planning management).

The collected data enabled the construction of External Factor Evaluation (EFE) and Internal Factor Evaluation (EFI) matrices. A SWOT matrix was utilized for the associative sector diagnosis.

EFE and EFI Matrices

According to David (2017), strategic planning requires identifying external factors using the External Factor Evaluation (EFE) Matrix, which summarizes and evaluates economic, social, political, governmental, cultural, environmental, and technological data while highlighting critical aspects via qualitative analysis.

Matrix construction involves five steps: i) list external factors identified across various domains as favorable (opportunities) or unfavorable (threats); ii) assign a weight to each factor from 0.0 (unimportant) to 1.0 (very important); iii) assign a rating from 1 to 4 indicating current organizational strategy effectiveness regarding each factor; iv) multiply weight by rating to determine a weighted score; v) sum weighted scores to determine total organizational score.

Total weighted scores range from 1.0 to 4.0, with an average benchmark of 2.5 regardless of total factors evaluated. A total score of 4.0 indicates that an organization responds optimally to opportunities and threats, capitalizing on favorable factors while mitigating adverse impacts. Conversely, a score of 1.0 indicates ineffective handling of external opportunities and threats.

The Internal Factor Evaluation (EFI) Matrix evaluates internal organizational behavior, summarizing strengths and weaknesses (David, 2017). Construction follows five steps: i) list strengths and weaknesses; ii) assign weights from 0.0 (unimportant) to 1.0 (very important); iii) assign ratings from 1 to 4 to indicate major weakness (1), minor weakness (2), minor strength (3), or major strength (4); iv) multiply weight by rating to generate weighted scores; v) sum all weighted scores. Total scores range from 1.0 to 4.0, with 2.5 as the average score. Scores below 2.5 reflect internal weakness, whereas scores above 2.5 indicate a strong internal position.

Findings indicate cantonal GAD management focused primarily on the general productive sector rather than directly supporting PSE organizations, despite their interconnectedness. Another issue is limited compliance with LOEPS (Art. 133) mandates regarding budget allocation for PSE development across its various organizational forms. As Caiza (2023) notes, legal frameworks aim to satisfy territorial needs and timely public policy execution to ensure civic welfare and, in this case, strengthen the associative sector.

Nevertheless, efforts to establish a biotienda (eco-store) in coordination with Mancomunidad Bosque Seco (where Sozoranga is a member) are commendable. According to the Mayor, this commercial initiative aims to highlight products from local entrepreneurs, associations, and cultural groups, driving local economic circuits rooted in solidarity, collective work, and identity. However, lack of internal coordination between financial and planning departments was evident; these units showed minimal awareness of PSE participation in the canton's socioeconomic structure, risking repetitive, standardized programming detached from local realities.

This commercial initiative aligns with observations by Coraggio (2009a) and Terán et al. (2023), who emphasize that public policy for the PSE should avoid welfare-dependent approaches, acting instead to catalyze economic dynamics based on individual and collective capacities (collaboration networks).

Reflecting the canton's primary agricultural focus, main products lacked value addition. However, one association has begun processing corn into flour for sango, a traditional Loja dish. They also plan to market packaged horchata, a semi-processed herbal tea promoting ancestral nutrition.

Marketing dynamics experienced fluctuations in output and timing due to weather shocks. Processing associations operated monthly at small volumes. Direct sales were limited, with reliance on intermediaries or distribution to local neighborhood stores and nearby cantonal markets.

In accounting systems, the main challenge was an absence of systematic income and expenditure records, necessitating external accounting services. Sales tracking relied on manual logs. Members understood production costs but lacked accounting tools and skills for regular financial management.

Under equity, associations maintained basic surplus distribution planning. Fixed assets were primarily acquired via public donations (e.g., parish GAD agricultural support agreements). Capitalization mechanisms were weak, with no regular member contributions to increase capital stock.

Regarding financing, no association utilized external credit leverage. While avoiding debt interest, this indicated a lack of investment in equipment modernization to scale operations.

Discussion

Results contrast with findings from Villamarín (2023), Merino et al. (2024), and Naranjo (2015), particularly regarding public institutions, organizational capacity, and territorial impacts of PSE associations.

Institutional articulation between Sozoranga associations and the cantonal GAD was limited compared to Villamarín's (2023) findings in Loreto, where GADs actively funded equipment, technical assistance, training, legal advice, and fair promotions. In Sozoranga, municipal assistance remains ad hoc rather than part of a sustained PSE strategy.

Regarding internal capacity, Sozoranga associations shared tech adoption and financial literacy challenges with those in Merino et al. (2024). However, while Merino et al. noted efficient management in La Troncal, Sozoranga showed persistent administrative and financial management gaps, reflecting varying institutional support across rural settings.

Compared to Naranjo's (2015) analysis of Tungurahua's provincial GAD—which institutionalized multi-level governance, civic participation, and agricultural trade networks (e.g., PACAT)—Sozoranga demonstrated fewer formalized participatory governance mechanisms.

Nevertheless, all studies reaffirm the essential role of productive associations as local development drivers rooted in social capital and community identity.

Conclusions

Evaluation of Sozoranga canton's associations underscores the necessity for strategic alignment with the cantonal GAD. Although current municipal actions address general agriculture, direct support for PSE entities remains limited. Under LOEPS (Art. 133), GADs are mandated to allocate funding to foster PSE organizations.

Municipal finance and planning staff require training on PSE legal frameworks to align local development plans with association needs, collaborating with the Superintendency of Popular and Solidarity Economy (SEPS) and the Institute of Popular and Solidarity Economy (IEPS).

Internally, associations must overcome financial planning deficits, weak capitalization mechanisms, and administrative capacity gaps through policy support that avoids dependency-creating approaches.

Inter-institutional collaboration between the GAD and associations remains weak, lacking formal agreements and dialogue spaces. Enhancing coordination through multi-sector roundtables, academic partnerships, and financial sector engagement will optimize resources and generate sustainable local impact.

Promising initiatives like the biotienda store, created with Mancomunidad Bosque Seco, provide a model for expanding local fair marketing and community eco-tourism.
