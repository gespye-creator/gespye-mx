Latin American Universities and PINTEC: Innovation, Sustainability, and Institutional Challenges

Abstract.

Latin American universities today face a tension that can no longer be ignored: either they connect their academic output with the real needs of their communities, or they will continue to be left behind by the social, environmental, and technological changes sweeping the region. This situation is clearly reflected in the indicators of Brazil's Technological Innovation Research (PINTEC), which show how many companies continue to prioritize purchasing technology over developing their own innovations or establishing strong ties with academic institutions. The University Laboratories for Social Innovation (LUIS) model proposes a different way of understanding and organizing university work. Its approach integrates academic, technological, social, organizational, business, and institutional dimensions, aiming to transform laboratories from isolated entities into spaces where training, applied research, and engagement with the territorial context are articulated, incorporating regional learning and international standards such as ISO 59004 and ISO 59010.

Keywords:

Sustainable Innovation, University Ecosystems, PINTEC Brazil, LUIS Laboratory, Territorial Articulation.

Resumen.

Las universidades latinoamericanas viven hoy una tensión que ya no se puede ignorar: o logran conectar su producción académica con las necesidades reales de sus territorios, o seguirán quedándose al margen de los cambios sociales, ambientales y tecnológicos que atraviesa la región. Esta situación se refleja con claridad en los indicadores de la Pesquisa de Inovação Tecnológica (PINTEC) de Brasil, que muestran cómo muchas empresas siguen apostando por la compra de tecnología antes que por desarrollar innovación propia o establecer vínculos sólidos con instituciones académicas. El modelo Laboratorios Universitarios de Innovación Social (LUIS) plantea una forma distinta de entender y organizar el quehacer universitario. Su propuesta integra dimensiones académicas, tecnológicas, sociales, organizativas, empresariales e institucionales, buscando que los laboratorios dejen de funcionar como islas y se conviertan en espacios donde la formación, la investigación aplicada y la vinculación con el entorno se articulan, incorporando aprendizajes regionales y estándares internacionales como ISO 59004 e ISO 59010.

Palabras clave:

Innovación sostenible, Ecosistemas universitarios, PINTEC Brasil, Laboratorio LUIS, Articulación Territorial.

Introduction

The environmental problems the world is facing—including climate change, the deterioration of ecosystems, and inequalities between territories—have moved past the simple diagnosis stage. Current circumstances demand real, contextualized, and sustainable interventions that articulate knowledge, decision-making, and public or community action. Under this panorama, the university's role cannot be reduced to reproducing theory: according to Arocena and Sutz (2021), universities cannot remain on the margins of their environment, but must actively participate in territorial processes and influence the productive and social dynamics where they carry out their functions.

In many countries of the Global South, promoting innovations with a sustainable approach involves facing persistent structural limitations. Limited funding, infrastructure shortages, and poor articulation between state and private actors complicate the design of strategies that truly respond to the particularities of each territory. For this reason, some authors emphasize the importance of promoting models that combine territorial relevance, inclusion, and economic feasibility (Arocena and Sutz, 2021).

Even so, within the university sphere, tensions persist that hinder its ability to articulate with other actors. One of the most reiterated problems is the poor connection with the productive apparatus. In numerous cases, university laboratories continue to operate in isolation, without sustained mechanisms that allow them to link up with the economic or social needs of the territories where they are inserted. This disconnection makes it difficult for them to evolve into spaces with a real capacity to drive transformations in the regional environment (Veiga Ávila et al., 2023).

Added to this are internal factors that deepen the gap: a shortage of qualified technical personnel, institutional cultures resistant to change, discontinuity in action lines, and an absence of policies with a sustainable approach (Rotondo et al., 2023). These conditions directly affect the ability of universities to respond to development challenges.

This disconnection is also observed from the business sphere. In Brazil, the Pesquisa de Inovação Tecnológica (PINTEC) has consolidated itself as a key instrument for observing the evolution of R&D investment, the adoption of emerging technologies, and the forms of collaboration between different entities. According to the IBGE (2024), companies have progressively increased the use of digital solutions, especially artificial intelligence and cloud services. Although these technologies are usually associated with improvements in productivity and efficiency, they are also beginning to be recognized for their contribution to reducing environmental impacts, especially in sectors with a high demand for resources (Sperotto et al., 2021). This convergence between technological transformation and sustainability opens a space for universities to participate more directly in change strategies.

At the same time, the literature on sustainable innovation insists on articulating organizational openness—open innovation—with internal capacities oriented toward generating, absorbing, and adapting knowledge. In this sense, universities can play a relevant role as spaces for collaborative experimentation, applied training, and technology transfer (Bigliardi and Filippelli, 2022).

Understanding these ecosystems requires broad approaches that integrate organizational, social, technological, and institutional dimensions. Silva Neto et al. (2024) propose analytical models that allow for a more comprehensive approach to these dynamics. In different European countries, it has been observed that training professionals with specific competencies in innovation has become decisive in linking academic work with social objectives of broad and sustained projection over time (Kozirog et al., 2022).

At the international level, various investigations coincide in pointing out recurrent obstacles: lack of planning, lack of environmental teams, weakness in the continuity of initiatives, and institutional resistance (Veiga Ávila et al., 2023). Some recent studies show how public universities try to incorporate sustainable innovation practices while facing tensions between traditional academic culture and the demands of territorial impact (Rotondo et al., 2023). A complementary example comes from the field of software engineering: the teaching of sustainable practices within curricula remains limited due to a lack of resources, pedagogical materials, and teaching commitment (Torre et al., 2017).

In this context, this work takes up these experiences as input for reflection. It recognizes the structural challenges, the incipient advances, and the emerging forms of articulation among the university, the State, companies, and the community. It arises from doctoral research based on case studies, Delphi-type validation, and comparative analysis using indicators from the Brazilian PINTEC.

Based on this research process, the University Laboratories for Social Innovation (LUIS) model is proposed as a malleable methodological alternative. This model seeks to respond to the need to overcome the isolated operation of university laboratories, promoting their articulation with the territorial environment, the productive sector, and social dynamics.

Its structure incorporates technological, organizational, social, and institutional components, allowing it to be adjusted to different academic scenarios and territorial realities. The central axis of the proposal is to show how these laboratories can be transformed into connection points between training, applied research, and the needs of the territories.

From this perspective, the LUIS model is not presented as a distant theory, but as an operational route that seeks to drive sustainable innovations both within universities and in their relationship networks.

Based on this research process, some patterns that are repeated in the different cases analyzed began to appear. In the case studies, it was observed that university laboratories have difficulties sustaining stable links with the productive environment and, in many cases, end up operating in isolation.

Complementarily, the Delphi-type validation allowed recognizing that aspects such as institutional articulation, availability of resources, and organizational continuity are not secondary elements, but necessary conditions for these spaces to function effectively.

For its part, contrasting these findings with the PINTEC indicators showed that collaboration between university and business remains limited and poorly structured. This reinforces the need for models that not only promote these relationships but also allow them to be organized and sustained over time.

Conceptual framework: university laboratories, sustainability, and innovation

Understanding the transformative scope of laboratories requires more than an administrative or technical definition. A conceptual foundation that crosses the academic with the territorial is needed. Notions such as sustainable innovation, inter-institutional cooperation, collaborative openness, and university commitment to the environment constitute the axes that underpin the LUIS model. These ideas allow laboratories to be interpreted not only as facilities or projects but as gears with the capacity for social, environmental, and strategic intervention.

Sustainable innovation, collaboration, and territory

The notion of sustainable innovation is no longer limited to productivity or efficiency indicators. In recent years, its meaning has expanded toward approaches that connect social justice, territorial adaptation, and environmental restoration. From this perspective, innovating implies critically reviewing current development models, responding to global challenges from the local level, and generating coherent solutions with community realities (Bigliardi, Filippelli, and Galati, 2021; Bigliardi and Filippelli, 2022).

This type of innovation does not emerge in isolation within institutions. Various studies show that organizations that build external links—with universities, social networks, companies, or communities—increase their capacities to develop sustainable solutions (Bigliardi and Filippelli, 2022). In these types of links, it becomes more viable to incorporate circular economy approaches, adjust technologies to the needs of the environment, and design productive structures with greater resilience and adaptability.

Recent academic reviews highlight four axes where sustainability and innovation converge: the development of technological capabilities, redesign of business models, fluid collaboration across sectors, and ecological criteria in innovative efficacy (Bigliardi and Filippelli, 2022). But these advances do not depend solely on external alliances; they also require institutions to strengthen internal cultures based on continuous learning, openness to change, and the critical appropriation of knowledge.

Galindo-Martín et al. (2025) show that open innovation can enhance sustainability when collaborative networks and flexible institutional structures exist. Complementarily, (Worakittikul et al., 2025) point out that, in small and medium-sized enterprises, co-creation processes with social actors are key to incorporating environmental practices aligned with circular economy dynamics.

When a university laboratory is inserted into these collaborative networks with communities, productive sectors, and local governments, its role ceases to be purely experimental. It becomes an articulating agent with the capacity to drive sustainable transformations, respond to territorial needs, and generate measurable impacts in its environment.

Innovation ecosystems and the engaged university

An innovation ecosystem is sustained by the interaction of multiple actors who share knowledge, resources, problems, and learnings. Universities, companies, public agencies, communities, and end-users participate in these systems, each with distinct but interdependent interests. In this framework, academic institutions not only produce knowledge but also mobilize, validate, and adapt it to real demands.

Arocena and Sutz (2001) proposed that Latin American universities must link their substantive functions—teaching, research, and relationship with the environment—with the territorial dynamics where they act. Their 'university for development' approach questions vertical models of transfer and raises the need for active commitment to collective challenges. In subsequent works, the same authors warn that many institutions continue to operate under an 'empty academic autonomy,' disconnected from local problems, and argue that the university must assume a strategic role in social and productive systems (Arocena and Sutz, 2003).

Regional context and lessons from the PINTEC survey

When trying to understand how university laboratories can influence sustainable innovation dynamics, it is essential to look toward Brazil, where the Pesquisa de Inovação Tecnológica (PINTEC) constitutes a systematic benchmark for monitoring corporate innovative efforts. The IBGE executes it on a recurring basis, which allows for continuously tracking the evolution of innovation initiatives in the country. This survey collects information on the incorporation of technologies, financing modalities, levels of inter-institutional collaboration, and main barriers identified by companies (IBGE, 2024).

Although its traditional periodicity was triennial, a semiannual version has recently been explored to improve the speed of indicators, although this version is still in the experimental phase (IBGE, 2024). In its classic versions (2005, 2008, 2011, 2014, etc.), PINTEC covers both industries and certain high-technology services, and is based on the Oslo Manual to define product, process, organizational, and marketing innovations (IBGE, 2024; Sperotto et al., 2021).

Trends observed in the PINTEC data

A recurring finding of the PINTEC analysis is that many companies innovate through the acquisition of machinery, training, or outsourcing of services, rather than through internal development (Pastore and Loural, 2017). This trend suggests that external 'pulling' dominates in many Brazilian industrial firms, which limits the creation of endogenous competitive advantages.

Another relevant conclusion comes from studies on 'eco-innovation' within the PINTEC universe. Sperotto et al. (2021) compare companies that have incorporated innovations with environmental impact (eco-innovators) against those that innovate without visible environmental effects. They found that the former show superior performance in basic resources, innovative effort, and innovation outcomes, and also face different obstacles, such as limited financing and insufficient external cooperation (Sperotto et al., 2021).

In addition, studies of university-industry interaction have recoded PINTEC data as a secondary source. Paranhos et al. (2019) used PINTEC data from 2008, 2011, and 2014 in case studies to analyze linking strategies of large companies with universities, identifying that such collaborations are partial, dependent on a few high-profile projects, and not institutionally sustainable (Paranhos et al., 2019).

Policy analysis is also significant. In Brazil, instruments like the Innovation Law and the so-called Lei do Bem have sought to incentivize private R&D investments through tax deductions, financing, and stimuli for university-industry alliances (Campagnollo, 2023). However, although increases in the adherence of companies to these incentives have been reported, their real impact on a national scale remains moderate and geographically concentrated (Campagnollo, 2023).

On the other hand, the recent context shows signs of fragility. During the pandemic, business spending on research fell in proportion to GDP, dropping from 0.58% to 0.53% between 2019 and 2020 (Paiva, 2021). This setback is a reminder that progress is not linear and depends on institutional stability and coherent policies.

Relevance for the Latin American region and calibration for research

These PINTEC lessons offer valuable notes for this doctoral work in Latin America: companies on the continent usually adopt visible innovations (machinery, recycling) rather than disruptive or strategic innovations, which highlights an opportunity space for university laboratories oriented toward biotechnological issues, sustainable land use, or advanced energies.

The difference between 'eco-innovative' and innovative companies shows that it is not enough to innovate: innovation must be oriented toward sustainability, which implies institutional strategies more aligned with financing, cooperation, and risk perception. Additionally, university-industry collaboration, although promoted by public policies, usually depends on isolated projects and lacks institutionality, which coincides with the finding that formal collaboration remains marginal.

For their part, stimulus policies such as tax exemptions can generate specific incentives, but the challenge is to make these measures accessible and effective for universities, small businesses, and less favored regions. In the Latin American reality, structural factors such as financial instability, constant changes in authorities, and the fragility of collaborative networks hinder the continuity of university initiatives. For this reason, the LUIS model incorporates strategies that strengthen institutional resilience and prevent laboratories from depending exclusively on temporary conjunctures or wills.

Model: University Laboratory of Innovation for Sustainability

Based on the findings collected in the case studies, the expert validation through the Delphi method, and the comparison with the indicators of the PINTEC survey, the LUIS model is proposed as a structured methodology that combines six operational dimensions and five successive phases. Each component incorporates specific indicators and application guidelines. The dimensions are detailed below:

Academic Dimension. This axis links the laboratory with the training processes, so that students actively participate in innovation and not only from a theoretical approach. Aspects such as: articulated subjects, effective practice hours, percentage of students involved in projects, interdisciplinary integration, and linked evaluation forms are considered. The reference is nourished by university experiences that have strengthened competencies associated with sustainable development.

Technological Dimension. Includes the technical and digital means available: updated equipment, virtual platforms, specialized software, prototyping tools, sensors, connectivity, and operational maintenance. When these resources are not renewed or remain obsolete, the laboratory loses its capacity to respond to the demands of the environment and disconnects from emerging dynamics that are key to its territorial relevance.

Social Dimension. Evaluates the degree of the laboratory's interaction with the actors in its environment. It considers community participation, co-creation processes with local organizations, the generation of social legitimacy, and alliances with public and social institutions of the territory. This component defines whether the laboratory projects itself as an active agent of the environment or is reduced to an isolated space within the university structure.

Territorial and institutional connection. This axis is determining to prevent the laboratory from operating as a closed space. Its purpose is to ensure that it is inserted into local networks, participates in development agendas, and strengthens its presence in the territory, articulating efforts with subnational governments, productive associations, or community organizations.

Organizational. This dimension covers the internal governance structure of the laboratory, including articulation mechanisms among faculties, departments, research centers, and liaison units. It also includes the official mechanisms through which decisions are managed and institutional processes related to aspects such as administration, budgetary planning, and operational routines are organized. When this organizational base is weak or unstable, the laboratory is exposed to dysfunctions, especially in the face of internal changes or institutional restructuring.

Business. Includes collaborations with companies, incubation of ventures, technology licensing, invitations for joint projects, service provision contracts, and technology transfer. This dimension seeks to make the laboratory act as a real bridge between academia and the productive sector.

Institutional. Corresponds to the university policies that support the laboratory: stable funding lines, institutional recognition, incentives for researchers, alignment with external regulations, and standards such as ISO 59004 and 59010. Institutionalization is what allows the proposal to not depend solely on individual wills.

Operational phases of the LUIS model: the model is deployed through five sequential phases that allow structure and flexibility:

  1. Local contextual diagnosis, where the territorial environment is evaluated, such as local needs, key actors, existing capabilities, human and technical resources available; this allows adapting the laboratory to the specific context of the territory.
  2. Strategic design of the laboratory, where, based on the diagnosis, the laboratory's profile is defined: priority dimensions, thematic lines, organizational structure, potential allies, goals, and indicators.
  3. Operational implementation, which can be construction (or adaptation) of infrastructure, hiring of personnel, acquisition of equipment, establishment of protocols, start of pilot projects, and opening to the environment.
  4. Monitoring and evaluation, where indicators defined for each dimension are applied to measure performance, impacts, challenges, and lessons learned. It is essential to have mechanisms for periodic feedback and adjustment.
  5. Scaling and replication: if the laboratory works effectively, it moves to an expansion, diffusion, and replication phase in other university or territorial contexts, adapting the model according to the lessons learned.

Suggested indicators and estimated budgeting

For each dimension, quantitative and qualitative indicators can be defined. For example: academic, which implies percentage of students with a project in the laboratory / number of integrated courses; technological: level of operational availability of equipment / equipment renewal rate; social, which is the number of co-creation activities with communities / satisfaction level of external actors; organizational, which indicates the number of units involved / clarity of governance structure; business, which implies amount of contracts with companies / number of formal collaborations; and, finally, institutional, which is the percentage of the university budget assigned / permanence of the laboratory in the face of administrative changes.

Critical discussion

Comparison with international experiences

In European and Asian scenarios, there are already university laboratories with strong insertion in innovation ecosystems. For example, innovative 'living labs' in European cities have achieved significant territorial integration by connecting local authorities, businesses, and citizens in urban innovation projects (Ballon et al., 2005; Teli et al., 2017). These experiences show that social legitimacy and participatory design are just as important as technical capacity.

However, in those contexts, the institutional and financial conditions are much more stable. What works in a European university with secured funds and consolidated regulatory frameworks is not always replicable in Latin America. In many cases, laboratories depend on international subsidies, specific projects, or external financing lines, which makes them fragile.

Another lesson comes from the field of sustainable innovation models in companies: business models that adopt the circular economy must combine product design, reverse logistics, supply chain cooperation, and market acceptance (Nosratabadi et al., 2019). In that sense, the LUIS model must anticipate that prototypes are not enough: there must be economic sustainability pathways so that the results can be sustained beyond the period of the thesis or project.

Recent ISO standardization also provides a necessary critique: the ISO 59004 and ISO 59010 standards are new tools for structuring circular economy strategies in organizations. ISO 59004 defines vocabulary, principles, and guidelines to implement a circular economy in any organization (ISO, 2024). ISO 59010 complements that framework by guiding how to transition linear models toward circular schemes in value networks and business chains (ISO, 2024). The incorporation of these standards in the LUIS model is a success, but it requires technical and monitoring capacity that many Latin American universities do not yet possess. If technical, institutional resources, and political will are lacking, relying on international standards can become a burden instead of an enabler.

In operational terms, the incorporation of the ISO 59004 and ISO 59010 standards within the LUIS model is not proposed as a formal requirement, but as a reference guide to guide the internal organization of the laboratories. In practice, these standards allow structuring basic criteria for resource management, decision-making, and tracking of processes linked to sustainability. For example, they facilitate the identification of value flows, the evaluation of internal practices, and the progressive incorporation of circular economy principles into the laboratory's activities. In this way, rather than imposing a rigid scheme, their use within the LUIS model seeks to offer a flexible framework that helps order actions, make them more visible, and, above all, give them continuity over time.

In addition, recent work on open innovation and sustainability warns that innovation processes cannot be 'open' without limits: clear governance is needed to define what data to share, which actors to involve, how to manage intellectual property, and how to guarantee equity in participation (Princepe et al., 2025). In other words, opening the laboratory without clear frameworks can generate internal tensions, loss of control, or conflicts among actors with different expectations.

Risks and limitations of the LUIS model

When analyzing the work with the LUIS model in the university context, it is possible to recognize that its development does not depend solely on the conceptual proposal, but rather, is intrinsically linked to the real conditions under which laboratories operate. In fact, in practice, many of the difficulties do not manifest themselves as obvious errors, but rather as complex situations that build up over time and, consequently, influence the way activities are sustained.

Furthermore, increasing the number of activities does not necessarily imply that the laboratory is in real conditions to sustain them. Although these tensions do not always manifest as immediate interruptions, they do modify the pace of work and the way actions unfold over time.

Finally, something similar occurs when innovation is understood primarily from a technical approach. The results can be visible and valued; however, if constant links with the social and territorial environment are not maintained, these advances run the risk of losing strength over time. Consequently, in these cases, the work carried out depends excessively on initial conditions that cannot always be sustained in the long term.

Necessary conditions for the success of the model

For the LUIS model not to remain on paper, it is essential that certain minimum structural conditions be met: i) clear and permanent university policies that transcend government periods and institutionally recognize the laboratory's role; ii) sustained budget allocated from the baseline, not just relying on external calls; iii) training and education of specialized personnel who can operate, manage, and lead the laboratory; iv) co-financing strategies and public-private alliances that allow diversifying the laboratory's income sources; v) participatory governance and institutional mechanisms that allow the integration of faculties, departments, liaison units, and community units; vi) adequate technological infrastructure and continuous maintenance, considering obsolescence and updating; and, vii) a culture of monitoring, evaluation, accountability, and continuous improvement.

If these conditions are met, the LUIS model can move from the level of an ambitious proposal to a credible institutional reality.

Conclusions

This essay has intended to show that university laboratories do not have to remain isolated entities or individual efforts that depend on the enthusiasm of those who lead them. If well structured, these laboratories can act as integrating bridges between university knowledge, the productive sector, the community, and territorial policies.

The proposal of the LUIS model—with its six dimensions (academic, technological, social, organizational, business, and institutional) and five operational phases—seeks to provide a technical roadmap, but also an invitation to rethink the university's role in terms of territorial sustainability. Its validation with experts and its alignment with international standards such as ISO 59004 and ISO 59010 give it important backing, although they also pose technical demands that must be calibrated with institutional reality.

The lessons of the Brazilian PINTEC survey confirm today what many Latin American universities are already experiencing: scarce university-industry collaboration, concentration on visible issues (waste, recycling), a deficit of qualified personnel, and institutional fragility. These barriers are not anecdotal; they are part of the structural ecosystem of the region. For this reason, the University Laboratories for Social Innovation model must be designed with mitigation mechanisms, not with naivety.

In the same way, it is undeniable that innovation cannot be sustained solely with cold technical results. On the contrary, maintaining a constant and living relationship with the territory, reviewing our own forms of organization, and honestly reflecting on how our activities are developed appear as indispensable to provide the stability that laboratories require. Consequently, rather than looking for closed solutions or perfect instruction manuals (which, in truth, do not exist), it becomes essential that universities actively ask themselves how to truly accompany these processes, what human and material conditions they require to be sustained, and how they can adapt to changes without losing their essential educational meaning. At the end of the day, it's about people working for people.
