Institutional Decoupling: Gap between Formal Policy and Decent Work Practice

Desacoplamiento Institucional: Brecha entre Política Formal y Práctica de Trabajo Decente

Abstract.

The purpose is to analyze the persistence of institutional decoupling in the labor field, that is, the gap between Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) commitments and the actual practice of Decent Work. It is argued that the violation of rights is not solely explained by the pursuit of efficiency, but rather by the deliberate action of power that allows organizations to adopt formal structures. The aim is to overcome the degenerative tendencies of neo-institutionalism that simplify agency and structure. The methodology used is an exploratory literature review that integrates Institutional Theory, Rational Choice, and Power Theory. The analysis demonstrates that decoupling is an exercise of asymmetric power sustained by the ceremonial adoption of structures. It is concluded that agenda power and, fundamentally, ideological power are the drivers that naturalize precariousness, offering a contribution to the management of social sustainability and corporate coherence.

Keywords:

decoupling; decent work; institutional theory; social responsibility; organizational power.

Introduction

Modern administration recognizes that Decent Work constitutes a social and ethical imperative that transcends the mere economic relationship (Atencio, Arrias, Coronel, and Ronquillo, 2020). This recognition drives organizations to adopt formal commitments to Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) and Social Sustainability, assuming policies that align with global standards.

In this sense, Institutional Theory establishes that companies are subject to isomorphic pressures that force them to mimic structures to appear legitimate (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983); without the adoption of these structures, the organization faces the imminent loss of legitimacy (Suchman, 1995). Despite this formal momentum, the persistence of precarious employment globally suggests a failure in implementation, directing us to the phenomenon of decoupling.

However, this social pressure is opposed to economic rationality, which constantly seeks to maximize the benefit of available resources (García-Ruiz, 1999). The conflict between the social "ought to be" and corporate self-interest manifests in a persistent gap between formal policy (the CSR promise) and the actual practice of labor rights. This discrepancy has been termed institutional decoupling, a phenomenon where formal structures are adopted as myth and ceremony to gain external legitimacy, without altering internal operational routines (Meyer and Rowan, 1977).

This article is delimited to the articulation of three frameworks: Institutional Theory (NIS), Rational Choice Theory, and Power Theory (Lukes, 2005), in order to explain this inconsistency. To avoid the degenerative tendencies of traditional institutional literature—which often simplifies the relationship between agency and structure by focusing excessively on passive adaptation (Modell, 2022)—this work seeks to fill this gap by arguing that the initial formulations of NIS underestimated the central role of conflict and power as mediators (Greenwood, Oliver, Sahlin, and Suddaby, 2008). Thus, the radical perspective of Lukes (2005) emerges as the ideal tool for analyzing the active manipulation of the agenda and ideology that sustain the gap of precarious employment.

Therefore, the expected contribution of this analysis is twofold: first, to offer a multilevel theoretical synthesis that overcomes the structural determinism of NIS; and second, to provide a conceptual framework for analyzing corporate coherence in CSR, fundamental for the management of social sustainability. Based on the above, the objective of this work is to determine the analytical capacity of the interaction between Institutional Theory, Rational Choice Theory, and Power Theory to explain the causal mechanisms that enable and sustain institutional decoupling in labor practices.

This work differs from previous approaches to institutional decoupling, such as the seminal proposal of Oliver (1991) or recent critical readings centered on symbolic legitimacy, in that it introduces a multilevel perspective that articulates economic rationality, institutional legitimacy, and asymmetric power as interdependent causal mechanisms. Unlike classic models that explain decoupling as an adaptive or passive response to external pressures, this study conceptualizes it as a deliberate exercise of organizational power, aimed at managing the apparent coherence between the normative discourse and actual practice. Thus, the theoretical novelty of the work lies in the integration of the framework of Lukes (2005) within institutional analysis, allowing decoupling to be observed not only as a structural phenomenon, but also as a political strategy for managing legitimacy.

This analysis is guided by two research questions that orient the literature review: How does the exercise of asymmetric power (Lukes, 2005) articulate with isomorphic pressures (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983) to explain the ceremonial adoption (Meyer and Rowan, 1977) of Social Responsibility policies in the labor field? And to what extent do agenda power and ideological power (Lukes, 2005) actively sustain the gap (decoupling) between formal policy and actual practice in the management of social sustainability?

The article is based on a methodological approach of a theoretical-bibliographic qualitative nature. This selection obeys the central purpose of the research, which is the conceptual integration and the construction of an analytical framework that allows explaining a complex phenomenon. An approach inherent to research that seeks to build theory from the rigorous articulation of diverse frameworks is followed (Eisenhardt, 1989).

To ensure transparency and rigor in the theoretical review, clear criteria were defined for selecting the sources of the conceptual framework. In total, approximately forty academic sources of an indexed nature were analyzed, prioritizing high-impact articles and foundational works linked to Institutional Theory, Rational Choice Theory, and Power Theory. The search was conducted in specialized databases—Scopus, Web of Science, and Scielo—; keyword combinations associated with institutional decoupling, decent work, and organizational power were used. Likewise, inclusion criteria related to theoretical relevance, conceptual coherence, and topicality (last ten years) were applied, excluding texts of an informative nature or lacking empirical backing. This procedure ensured that the selected sources responded to the logic of multilevel theoretical integration that guides this work.

The process materialized in an exploratory and targeted literature review, focused on the articulation of three fundamental theoretical bodies: Institutional Theory (NIS), Rational Choice Theory, and Power Theory (Lukes, 2005). The methodological objective of this review was to construct an analytical framework that rigorously integrates the central role of conflict and power as causal mechanisms.

The resulting analysis consists of the synthesis and interaction of these frameworks, in order to determine the causal mechanisms that actively enable and sustain institutional decoupling in labor practices. The final result of this exercise is the proposition of a Conceptual Model (Figure 1) and a Table of Causal Mechanisms (Table 1), which seek to provide the study of social sustainability with a robust middle-range hypothesis ready to be subjected to empirical testing in future research.

The development of the article is organized in sequential sections. After this introduction, the section begins with the methodological approach of bibliographic review; subsequently, it develops the three key conceptual arguments: the tension between institutional pressure and legitimacy, the conflict between rationality and agency, and the determining role of power as a decoupling mechanism. Finally, it presents a critical reflection on the strengths and limitations of the theoretical model, and concludes with the implications and the central contribution of this analysis.

Conceptual Analysis and Discussion: The Gap Mechanism

Institutional Pressure and the Imperative of Legitimacy

The study of organizations in modern times is not restricted solely to the examination of economic rationality or internal efficiency, but requires a profound consideration of the cultural and normative environment that shapes them. In this context, institutional pressure emerges as a mechanism that guides the decision-making processes of entities towards conformity, establishing an imperative of legitimacy as an essential condition for survival.

Institutions, understood as the "rules of the game" that structure human interaction (North, 1990), determine a framework of action that drives organizations to adopt socially accepted structures and practices. Therefore, the institutional framework plays an important role in the performance of an economy, transcending purely transactional logic (North, 1990).

In this sense, modern institutional theory, developed by key authors, explains the uniformity observed in organizational fields through the concept of isomorphism. DiMaggio and Powell (1983) postulate that the formal structure of organizations converges not only due to competition, but due to a need to imitate or conform to external expectations.

This isomorphism, which can be coercive, mimetic, or normative, forces entities to assimilate their characteristics to those that the environment considers appropriate (De Freitas and Da Silveira, 2021). Furthermore, this need for conformity is intrinsically linked to the idea that the formal structure of institutionalized organizations often functions as a mere "myth and ceremony," rather than as an expression of actual technical efficiency (Meyer and Rowan, 1977).

The core of this dynamic lies in the imperative of legitimacy. Mark Suchman (1995) identifies organizational legitimacy as the generalized perception or assumption that the actions of an entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially constructed system of norms, values, beliefs, and definitions.

More specifically, legitimacy manifests in three forms: pragmatic, based on the self-interest of the audience; moral, based on normative approval; and cognitive, linked to comprehensibility and the taken-for-granted condition of the organization's existence and functioning (Suchman, 1995). Consequently, the pursuit of this social recognition becomes a fundamental strategy to guarantee the flow of resources and stability.

The focus on legitimacy confronts the classic vision of rationality. García (1999) indicates that, traditionally, rationality is associated with "the capacity to obtain the maximum benefit from resources or, as H. Simon says, to the capacity to solve problems by following the shortest path" (García-Ruiz, 1999), linking rationality with economic efficiency.

However, in an institutionalized environment, the adoption of legitimized practices is often imposed over the strict consideration of efficiency. An example of this tension is observed in the modern business environment, where organizations find themselves in the interaction between institutional pressures, which demand socially responsible practices such as the observance of labor rights, and internal decision-making processes, which prioritize efficiency and competitiveness (Alcívar-Pinto, 2025). In this way, institutional legitimacy operates as a constraint or a guide that complicates decision-making.

Finally, the institutional perspective, consolidated over time (Greenwood et al., 2008), demonstrates that organizations are social structures deeply rooted in the cultural and normative order. The success of an entity lies not only in its ability to be profitable, but in its ability to be perceived as fair, appropriate, and necessary by its environment. Thus, institutional pressure does not represent a mere external obstacle, but a constitutive force that defines the shape and purpose of organizational life, transforming legitimacy into the primary value that guides change and ensures permanence.

Rationality, Efficiency, and the Agency of Decoupling

Decoupling is the response to the inherent tension between the diverse institutional logics that shape the organizational field. On the one hand, there is the logic of the market or profitability, which primarily drives organizations to seek efficiency and cost minimization, a perspective that aligns with Rational Choice Theory (North, 1990).

However, it is crucial to recognize that Rational Choice Theory is limited by its focus on perfect rationality, which ignores social complexity and the inherently bounded nature of human action (Bunge, 1995). On the other hand, the ethical or social logic imposes respect for Decent Work and responsible management. The structural conflict materializes when the effective implementation of labor rights is evaluated internally as a direct limitation on economic efficiency.

From a Weberian perspective, this conflict can be understood as the collision between instrumental rationality—oriented towards the maximization of means and results—and substantive rationality, which obeys ethical values and ends (Weber, 2002). In practice, organizations oscillate between both rationalities to sustain their legitimacy.

This tension generates ambivalent decisions, where technical efficiency tends to prevail over institutional values. Habermas (1998) warns that when the economic system colonizes the lifeworld, communicative action and ethical consensus weaken, transforming legitimacy into an empty formality. Thus, decoupling reflects the substitution of ethical dialogue with the logic of efficiency, a symptom of the colonization of the economic system over social norms.

The gap between norm and practice is actively maintained thanks to organizational agency, which is far from being passive. On the contrary, it requires institutional work (Lawrence and Suddaby, 2006) to sustain the decoupling. This work consists of deliberate and strategic actions by actors to maintain the separation between the declared policy and the operational practice.

In this framework, agency not only acts based on economic interests, but also operates as a cognitive and moral construction. Organizational actors justify their behavior through rationalizing narratives (“we comply with what is possible”, “we follow international standards”) that allow them to sustain institutional identity without altering the real structure. Thus, agency becomes the vehicle that translates instrumental rationality into apparent legitimacy, making decoupling a practice sustained by the moral justification of non-compliance.

To illustrate this dynamic in a contemporary context, the business model of certain digital delivery platforms is particularly revealing. In this sector, organizations often promote a public discourse of 'flexibility', 'autonomy', and 'entrepreneurship' that legitimizes their model before society and investors, acting as myth and ceremony. However, in operational practice, the allocation algorithms and rates configure an asymmetric labor relationship that evades formal regulations and shifts operational risks to the worker. Thus, organizational power actively silences discussions about social security or labor rights, prioritizing financial efficiency over social sustainability.

This process is achieved, for example, by subtly redefining the boundaries of Corporate Social Responsibility or manipulating perceptions of what constitutes compliance. Thus, decoupling is conceived as an active strategy that emerges from the intersection of contradictory institutional logics (Thornton, Ocasio, and Lounsbury, 2015), where the organizational actor assumes the responsibility of filtering and justifying the prioritization of the logic of profitability over the social one.

In this way, the rationality of decoupling is not irrational, but selectively rational: it chooses which values to uphold depending on the convenience of the context. This type of “strategic” rationality seeks to simultaneously preserve internal efficiency and external legitimacy, configuring what March and Olsen (1989) termed a “logic of institutional appropriateness.” Such logic allows companies to maintain a normative discourse aligned with labor rights, while internally optimizing their resources through precarious practices.

The analysis of agency is fundamental, since degenerative tendencies in institutional research often stem from a unilateral emphasis, either on the role of pre-existing institutions or, conversely, on human agency (Modell, 2022). The integration of both poles is what allows viewing decoupling as the result of organizational decision-making. Therefore, the choice to ceremonially adopt a structure to guarantee legitimacy (Meyer and Rowan, 1977) is justified internally by economic rationality, but is sustained by agency that actively avoids the costs of actual implementation.

In summary, the rationality, efficiency, and agency of decoupling form an analytical triangle where each vertex supports the other: rationality defines the justificatory framework, efficiency determines the operational incentive, and agency executes the strategy of separation. This triangle explains why the gap between formal policy and actual practice is not an implementation error, but a deliberate architecture of organizational power.

The Determining Role of Power in Maintaining the Gap

The fundamental mechanism that empowers organizations to manage the conflict between legitimacy and efficiency, and to actively sustain decoupling, is asymmetric power. Institutional Theory, in its initial formulations, limited its ability to explain change or inertia because it did not systematically integrate the role of power and conflict as structuring forces (Greenwood et al., 2008). For this reason, the analysis relies on the radical perspective of Steven Lukes (2005), which conceives power in three dimensions, each acting as an engine that perpetuates the coherence gap.

Firstly, decision-making power (the first dimension) manifests through explicit actions that prioritize profitability (Lukes, 2005). This is observed in managerial choices that favor, for example, precarious hiring models or contractual simulation, even at the expense of labor regulations. This manifestation of power ensures that the maximization of economic benefit (North, 1990) materializes in practice.

Secondly, agenda power (the second dimension) operates more subtly, exercising the capacity to silence or deflect critical issues of Decent Work (such as labor formalization or wage improvement) from formal discussion within management (Lukes, 2005). This power limits the reach of isomorphic pressures (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983), guaranteeing that the formal policies adopted only address issues comfortable for management and that the costs associated with real implementation do not reach the decision-making table.

Finally, ideological power (the third dimension) is the most profound and structural (Lukes, 2005). This power refers to the ability to shape perceptions, beliefs, and expectations, achieving the naturalization of precarious conditions. It is exercised through corporate narratives that present the violation of rights as "necessary sacrifices" for the survival of the business, or as an inescapable part of a "culture of effort" (Lukes, 2005).

This process ensures that workers themselves internalize the gap and consider it legitimate or inevitable. In short, the three dimensions of power act as a robust filtering system, allowing the organization to maintain the facade of CSR (legitimacy) while prioritizing economic efficiency without internal confrontation.

In this dynamic, ideological power plays an especially persistent role, by reconfiguring the cognitive frames through which actors interpret inequality as something natural or even functional. In this way, power not only disciplines action, but also thought, consolidating symbolic structures that reinforce organizational obedience. Consequently, decoupling ceases to be an anomaly and becomes a structural component of the contemporary institutional order, where apparent legitimacy is sustained through the social acceptance of conflict and precariousness.

Critical Reflection: Strengths, Limitations, and Opportunities of the Study

The present work, like any academic contribution, possesses a set of strengths and limitations that are crucial to expose, as they guide the opportunities for future research. The main strength of this conceptual approach lies in the proposed multilevel theoretical integration.

By articulating Sociological Neoinstitutionalism with Rational Choice Theory and, crucially, with the three-dimensional perspective of power of Lukes (2005), an analytical framework capable of overcoming structural determinism is offered. This allows conceptualizing decoupling not as a simple passive adaptation, but as an active and strategic exercise of power that manages coherence (Risi, Vigneau, Bohn, and Wickert, 2023)

However, the methodological design of this theoretical review article imposes an inherent limitation: the absence of direct empirical evidence. The work is based on the conceptual interpretation of existing frameworks (Eisenhardt, 1989), which, while guaranteeing theoretical solidity, requires validation in real organizational contexts.

This limitation is related to the warnings about degenerative tendencies in institutional research, which arise from a unilateral emphasis on agency or structure, without managing to articulate them rigorously (Modell, 2022). Therefore, the presented theoretical model demands an empirical application that tests the predictive capacity of power mechanisms in a concrete organizational field.

Precisely in this limitation lies the main opportunity for projecting the study. The developed conceptual model constitutes a robust middle-range hypothesis that must be tested. Future lines of research have the opportunity to empirically evaluate how agenda power and ideological power (Lukes, 2005) manifest in contexts of high coercive pressure, such as the textile sector or global supply chains. Multiple case studies, for example, could unravel the internal narratives that naturalize precariousness and contrast them with formally declared CSR policies (Suchman, 1995), thus closing the cycle between theory and practical evidence in the management of social sustainability.

Conclusion

The central contribution of this work lies in offering a multilevel theoretical synthesis that, by incorporating the radical perspective of power, provides the missing causal mechanism in the initial formulations of Institutional Theory. This conceptual framework transcends the structural determinism of Sociological Neoinstitutionalism (NIS) by integrating economic rationality and demonstrating that decoupling is an active exercise of asymmetric power, and not a simple passive adaptation, to sustain the persistent gap between formal policy (myth and ceremony) and actual practice (precariousness).

As a result, this conceptual integration strengthens Social Sustainability studies, providing them with more robust tools to understand corporate inconsistency and its structural foundations. The Table of Causal Mechanisms (Table 1) and the Conceptual Model (Figure 1) constitute a middle-range hypothesis that articulates how Institutional Pressure (coercive, normative, cognitive) is filtered by the three dimensions of Lukes' asymmetric power. From this perspective, the analysis identifies power as the structural factor that naturalizes the violation of labor rights for the sake of efficiency.

Likewise, from an applied perspective, the analysis acquires practical relevance for Management, to the extent that it demonstrates that organizational coherence depends on actively challenging the three dimensions of power. Business leadership must recognize that organizational coherence depends on actively challenging the three dimensions of power, particularly Agenda Power, which silences critical issues (such as wage improvement or formalization), and Ideological Power, which shapes perceptions and achieves the internalization of precariousness by the workers themselves.

Similarly, for Oversight Bodies and Public Policy—particularly in economies like the Mexican one, where labor compliance is under surveillance—it is suggested that regulations should not focus solely on the existence of formal policies (Decision Power), but also on the surveillance of Agenda Power and Ideological Power, which tend to naturalize precariousness and weaken normative effectiveness, requiring an approach beyond documentary compliance.

For future lines of research, it is considered imperative to transcend the theoretical analysis of institutional decoupling and, therefore, empirically test the proposed conceptual model in order to validate its predictive capacity. In this sense, three axes of empirical research are proposed that operationalize the dimensions of Lukes' asymmetric power (2005) in specific organizational contexts: the evaluation of internal narratives, the study of resistance mechanisms of subordinate actors, and sectoral comparison between organizational fields with differentiated institutional logics.

The three axes presented in Table 2 are not independent of each other, but are articulated as a progressive research program. The evaluation of narratives provides the interpretative basis for understanding how ideological power naturalizes precariousness; the study of resistance mechanisms introduces the perspective of subordinate agency as a force capable of destabilizing the managerial agenda; and sectoral comparison allows contrasting and generalizing the predictive capacity of the model in different organizational fields. Together, these three lines constitute the empirical path to test the middle-range hypothesis proposed by this work, closing the cycle between theoretical construction and practical evidence in the management of social sustainability.

Finally, the analysis underscores that true coherence in Social Responsibility does not lie in the mere documentation of commitments, but in the political will of management to dismantle the internal structures of power that sustain the organizational gap. In sum, this constitutes the real challenge for 21st-century administration, committed not only to economic efficiency, but also to the ethical and structural transformation of power relations within organizations.
