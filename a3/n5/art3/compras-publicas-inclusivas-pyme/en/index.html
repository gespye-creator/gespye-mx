Abstract.

Public procurement in Latin America and the Caribbean represents an average of 8.6% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), while in Ecuador it accounts for nearly twice that figure, reaching 16% of GDP. Within this framework, inclusive public procurement has the potential to address structural barriers and enhance the social impact of public expenditure. The aim of this study is to analyze inclusive public procurement, and its role in fostering the dynamization of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in Ecuador. A quantitative methodology was applied, drawing on primary data collected from 553 companies nationwide between May and July 2025. The main findings indicate that SME participation in public procurement processes depends on ethical perception, firm size, and the capacity of human talent.

Keywords:

public procurement; SME; ethics; human capital; public contracting.

Introduction

Inclusive public procurement has the potential to allow sectors traditionally excluded from the system to participate in this state action. In this field, there are various groups that can benefit from inclusion, such as businesses led by women, local businesses, and those from vulnerable sectors. In this sense, public procurement emerges as a public policy instrument that generates state incentives to revitalize, for example, SMEs, representing a strategic option that, in an integrated manner, can lead these companies to a systemic improvement in their competitiveness.

This state support action, if properly focused, can help reverse the negative effect generated on competitiveness—for example, of SMEs—by the difficulty of accessing the market for goods and services (Llisterri 2002, 3); since "the state procurement system represents a public management tool that transcends the mere obtaining of products and services for government institutions" (Uriña and Alvarado 2025, 1534). In this sense, government action can help the evolution of productive sectors through the promotion of innovation and the correction of market failures (Loría y López 1999, 5).

The objective of this document is to analyze inclusive public procurement and the revitalization of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in Ecuador. For this purpose, a quantitative methodology is used, based on primary information collected from 553 companies nationwide between May and July 2025. In this process, statistical tests are used to determine if there are differences between small and medium-sized enterprises.

The document is composed of the following sections: 1. Introduction. 2. Theoretical Framework, which reviews inclusive public procurement and SME development. 3. Methodology, which presents the database used in the study and its characteristics. 4. Results, showing the main findings of the study associated with public procurement and SMEs, and 5. Conclusions.

Theoretical Framework

Inclusive Public Procurement

Public procurement in Latin America and the Caribbean represents, on average, 8.6% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), equivalent to around $450 billion dollars. This expenditure on public procurement is lower than that of developed OECD countries, where the average reaches 14% of GDP. Within the countries of the region, there is diversity: Guatemala, which represents around 4%; Brazil, nearly 8%; and Ecuador, which accounts for almost double that average, with 16% of GDP (Banco Interamericano de Desarrollo, 2018).

A major problem with public procurement is corruption. Estimates of losses due to corruption for the European Union range between 7% and 25% of the total allocated to public procurement. For Latin America, there are no recent estimates, but considering that Latin American corruption indexes exceed European ones, it could be inferred that corruption in public procurement in the region exceeds 2.5% of GDP (Banco Interamericano de Desarrollo, 2018).

A measure used to avoid corruption and inefficiency in the Latin American region has been the incorporation of electronic means in public procurement (Harper, Calderón y Muñoz 2016). However, the results of this implementation are still diverse in the region and show limitations in the fight against corruption. For this reason, eight elements are recommended in public procurement: i) planning, ii) coordination, iii) competition, iv) digital technology, v) transparency, vi) professionalization, vii) evaluation, and viii) monitoring and control.

Inclusive public procurement has the potential to allow sectors traditionally excluded from the system to participate in this state action. In this process, they can reduce structural gaps by incorporating diverse sectors. Along these lines, they also emerge as a space where transparency results can be improved by incorporating new actors. In this field, there are various groups that can benefit from inclusion, such as businesses led by women, local businesses, and those from vulnerable sectors.

On one hand, an example of inclusive public procurement is the incorporation of businesses led by women. In this sense, it is considered that "government procurement acts as a tool for the recovery of women-led businesses while fostering entrepreneurship in the context of recovery" (ONU 2022, 2). Of the 8.6% of GDP that public procurement represents in the region, it is estimated that only 1% of GDP corresponds to businesses led by women (ITC 2014). According to company size, it is estimated that one in three micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) is managed or owned by a woman, where "women manage between 25%–43% of micro and small enterprises and 26%–29% of medium-sized enterprises" (Banco Interamericano de Desarrollo, 2019, 2).

In this field, gender-responsive procurement policies are important to encourage the inclusion of businesses led by women. This process is deemed necessary to overcome market barriers related to the low participation of women in the productive economy, limited access to financial resources, and limited access to new markets. At the same time, it is necessary to overcome barriers derived from public contracting processes, associated with sufficient legislation and policies, available information, the incorporation of electronic contracting, limited time for calls, and awareness among public buyers (ONU, 2022).

On the other hand, an example of local inclusive public procurement is what the World Food Programme carries out in Latin America. This case allows visualizing a diverse dynamic of incorporating territorial communities in a more national and global context, where the creation of incentives plays an important role. This study finds a tangible impact of inclusive procurement on employment generated in localities by purchasing products from their areas, which represents around 27% in the most conservative scenario. However, inclusive purchases still represent only a quarter of the volume of local purchases, which unfortunately implies a significant setback when compared to figures from ten years ago.

In this process, inclusive procurement has been a "difficult and slow process to implement" (Namdar y Saa, 2024, 8). For this, it is recommended that inclusive purchases adapt to the different levels of development of local producers, identify suppliers with the highest probability of success, establish alliances with private sectors to generate fair collaboration, and understand inclusive procurement as a process that requires progressiveness and sustainability.

In this sense, inclusive public procurement is a key space to address structural barriers for different traditionally excluded groups. There are tangible benefits to their incorporation through public policy, which ultimately improves the number of workers and the sales of companies incorporated into the economic system.

From this, it can be stated that inclusive procurement improves the social impact with economic multipliers for the entire society, where public purchasing power is used for the benefit of the community. This space is a key field for micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises, which represent 99% of the productive fabric in Latin America.

Development and Revitalization of SMEs

Amid the need for state incentives to revitalize SMEs, the mixture of public policies with programs and projects aimed at developing financial and non-financial services emerges as a strategic option that, in an integrated manner, leads these types of companies to a systemic improvement of their competitiveness.

For example, along with a diverse range of business development services—with an emphasis on training and expert advice—the promotion of a specialized financing ecosystem for SMEs could become an enhancer of sectoral competitiveness because there is an operating "interrelated set of actors, instruments, policies, and institutional conditions that facilitate (or restrict) the access of small and medium-sized enterprises to financial resources in their different stages of development" (Oddone y Stola 2025, 6).

Among the specialized financial services to revitalize SMEs are, for example, access to seed resources for entrepreneurs and, later, when the business is already in operation, to risk financing sources that help the growth of companies in both national and international markets. Additionally, credit guarantee funds—due to their structure and purpose—become, from an inclusive perspective, the financial backing so that smaller productive actors can access financing.

In this action from the financial field, it is key that "policies and instruments for financing SMEs should not operate in a vacuum, but be a structural part of productive development policies and strategies aimed at strengthening value chains in a region or country" (Odonne y Stola 2025, 10).

Now, of course, when the State becomes involved, it must do so under an incentive policy that, considering the reality of the productive fabric, acts as a revitalizer of the production and commercialization of goods and/or services that, within the local and national market, can have greater demand thanks to the purchases made by institutions comprising the central government and local governments. This generates spaces to stimulate the strengthening of SMEs; since, for example, in areas like product commercialization, "the inefficiency of distribution channels and concentration problems hinder the competition of SMEs in local markets" (Llisterri 2002, 5).

Hence, the strategic objective that seeks to develop and revitalize SMEs—thanks to public action generated by local governments and the central government—has as its direct tool the public policies that, from financial and non-financial action, stimulate the integrated improvement of quality, productivity, and business innovation. Systemically, this could help understand and act upon three characterizing variables of SME performance: production, productivity, and employment (CEPAL 2001, 11).

And precisely, a line of support, from public policies, is the programs and projects that, for the purpose of state procurement, can be undertaken to generate sales opportunities for small and medium-sized enterprises that, due to their profile and incidence, have a representative presence in terms of the number of establishments and job creation.

This state support action, if properly focused, can help reverse the negative effect generated on competitiveness—for example, of SMEs—by the difficulty of accessing the market for goods and services (Llisterri 2002, 3); since "the state procurement system represents a public management tool that transcends the mere obtaining of products and services for government institutions" (Uriña y Alvarado 2025, 1534).

This highlights the need for the involvement of the State, as a buyer of goods and/or services, under a strategic operational profile different from what it has in traditional public contracting processes (Pérez y Barreto 2025, 78); emphasizing the creation of incentives that, from public procurement, help the consolidation of an inclusive and sustainable national economy (Lozano 2025, 111).

For public procurement aimed at strengthening SMEs to have a greater impact, private or public actors interested in this strengthening must act in two ways: on the one hand, helping business sectors to standardize and improve production in terms of quality, productivity, and innovation; and, on the other hand, supporting negotiation tasks, commercial links, and compliance with requirements on the use of digital platforms and the application of specialized public contracting regulations demanded by national or local government entities.

As can be seen, government action can help the evolution of productive sectors through the promotion of innovation and the correction of market failures (Loría y López 1999, 5) and, thus, consolidate productive and social fabrics that are more inclusive, innovative, and adapted to a local, national, and global market that is in permanent change.

Amid this scenario of support for SMEs, an important point to consider is that state action in productive matters must be based on an exercise of direct knowledge of territorial and sectoral realities. After all, a company located in a rural area is not the same as one in an urban area; similarly, a company dedicated to agriculture is not the same as one in commerce or services, because each type of organization has its own dynamics and needs.

In short, specific knowledge of sectoral needs—like those of SMEs—will allow achieving a greater impact on the living conditions of the local population thanks to the development of productive activities based on territorial economic diversification (Pinargote y Díaz 2021, 37).

In the midst of this scenario of state support for business activities—with an emphasis on small and medium-sized enterprises—it should be highlighted, as a key area of sectoral management, the articulation of government support that reaches, with priority criteria, all companies comprising the productive fabric of the country.

In this prioritization exercise, the correct selection of economic promotion instruments adapted to the specific reality demanded by local and regional productive development is important as a means of fostering competitiveness with a systemic approach (CEPAL 2001, 6).

Here, ministerial entities in charge of the productive, commercial, and investment revitalization areas should be called upon to assume the leadership of a public policy oriented toward the competitive transformation of a nation, naturally accompanied by high doses of social equity. This makes it necessary for this state support action to be based on the conception that "as long as SMEs develop in a healthy and inclusive business environment, they will be able to perform better and contribute more to the national economy" (CAF 2023, 21).

Now, thinking about the synergistic effect of state action, it is important to highlight other key actors that, due to their capacity for incidence, directly complement the government action aimed at the productive enhancement of SMEs.

In the case of international cooperation, as a complement to state resources, it is another promoting actor that emerges as an important lever to achieve a revitalizing inclusive effect, especially in those sectors and territories where the State has remained absent for years. Now, of course, it is important that this international aid is articulated with the application of public resources to avoid duplication of efforts and underutilization of money which, if used well, can achieve more powerful impacts.

Academia, through its core functions—teaching, research, and community engagement—must be highlighted as another key ally of state institutions in charge of productive promotion; since the generation of scientific knowledge can become the guiding contribution to achieving a fit between what companies need and what should creatively be done for an integrated improvement; where "innovation can be an engine of economic progress if its two fundamental propellers, the generation of useful knowledge and the exploitation and commercialization of that knowledge, work and complement each other" (Loría y López 1999, 3).

This shows that when academia gets involved, it can help improve "the productivity of companies because owners and collaborators could apply more efficient and effective measures by having an academic foundation to implement more effective strategies. However, the academic foundation must be accompanied by experience in order to improve results for companies" (CAF 2023, 23).

Local governments, for their part, thanks to their knowledge of the specific profile of the fabric of businesses linked to the territory they represent, are those actors that can lead local productive development under an approach of revitalizing business linkages that, due to their synergistic effect, could increase the competitiveness of small and medium-sized enterprises (Llisterri 2002, 2).

Thinking about business guilds, they also, when interacting with government entities, play a key role within the process of development and revitalization of sectors such as SMEs; since, in addition to offering services for their members, they have a high-impact function called sectoral incidence that, above all, seeks to ensure that laws and public policies serve as a means for that productive transformation that attracts investment, increases productivity, and thereby generates jobs that improve the socioeconomic well-being of the population.

In the construction of this scenario regarding guild action, from the perspective of strategic thinking, for example, SMEs will be able to identify advantages inherent to their territorial location to design and put competitive advantages into action from that local enhancement (Castelletti y Canzanelli 2005, 11).

Another key actor to be considered, as part of the synergistic public and private action to strengthen and revitalize SMEs, are non-governmental organizations (NGOs) specialized in productive support. Thanks to their closeness to various sectors within the business fabric, they can be channelers of commercial opportunities and, in unison, spaces of action aimed at developing productive, commercial, technological, and financial capabilities. They can fulfill this action because NGOs, as the motivating principle of their origin, seek to be that articulating agent between the State, private enterprise, and civil society (Pinzón 2005, 83).

Methodology

This research is based on data collected through the SME Economic Cycle Survey corresponding to the first four-month period of 2025, applied by the Observatorio de la PyME at Universidad Andina Simón Bolívar, Sede Ecuador. This survey, which has been carried out systematically since 2011, aims to analyze the behavior, performance, perspectives, and environment of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in Ecuador, with an emphasis on the industrial sector.

The fieldwork for this research was carried out between the months of May and July 2025, obtaining information from 553 companies nationwide. The sample design was structured with a 95% confidence level and a maximum margin of error of 5%. The majority of the people interviewed were managers, owners, and/or directors of the participating companies. Regarding the sample structure by company size, 66.7% correspond to small enterprises, followed by 22.6% medium-sized enterprises, and 10.3% microenterprises.

For the specific purposes of this research, the rotating questions section was used, a space that in the first four months of 2025 was oriented to gather information on the participation of SMEs in public procurement processes.

In this sense, the following research aims to analyze the factors that influence the participation of these types of companies in public contracting processes considering ethical perception, business size, and the capacity level of human talent. For this, three hypotheses were formulated. The first argues that the ethical perception of the public contracting process becomes a determining element for companies to decide whether or not to participate in these spaces. The second hypothesis states that the size of the company influences participation, considering the structural differences between micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises. Finally, the third hypothesis analyzes whether the capacity level of human talent within the organization conditions the probability of getting involved in these processes.

To test these hypotheses, the aforementioned variables were analyzed taking participation in public procurement as a common axis, which was measured dichotomously (Yes/No). In the first hypothesis, this variable was compared with the ethical perception regarding public procurement processes, a variable also measured dichotomously. For the second hypothesis, it was contrasted with business size, which was classified into micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises. Finally, for the third hypothesis, the relationship between participation and the level of human talent capacity was evaluated, a variable structured into four levels: high, medium, low, and none.

Since the variables are categorical, the Chi-square test of independence was used to determine the existence of statistically significant associations, complemented with Phi and Cramer's V tests to estimate the strength of the relationship in contingency tables larger than 2x2. This approach allowed identifying the influence of these factors on business participation in public procurement processes.

Results

Initially, it is important to highlight that 89% of the surveyed companies reported not having participated in public contracting processes during the last 3 years, while 11% did indicate having intervened in these spaces. Likewise, the results reveal that 28% of companies have considered participating in future public procurement processes, in contrast to 72% that stated not having considered it.

Regarding the main benefits identified by SMEs when participating as state suppliers, the results show that higher income and increased sales (26%) constitute the most relevant benefit perceived by companies. This is followed by stability and sustainability (23%), business strengthening (20%), and access to new markets and opportunities (19%), while business experience and learning (12%) appears as the least highlighted aspect. Overall, these results reflect that participation in public procurement processes is viewed primarily as a mechanism for economic revitalization and financial consolidation.

To analyze whether factors linked to ethical perception, company size, and the level of human talent capabilities influence SME participation in public contracting processes, the following research hypotheses were raised:

  • H1: The ethical perception that companies have about public contracting processes influences the decision of whether or not to participate in said processes.

  • H2: Company size significantly influences the participation of SMEs in public procurement processes.

  • H3: The level of human talent capacity of companies is significantly associated with the probability of participating in public procurement processes.

To test these hypotheses, Chi-square tests of independence were carried out, as well as Phi and Cramer's V tests. These calculations were performed using the statistical package SPSS version 30.

For the first hypothesis, the results of the Chi-square test showed a statistically significant association between the ethical perception of public contracting processes and the participation of SMEs in these procedures (X² = 45.594; df = 1; p < 0.001). The strength of the relationship, measured by Phi and Cramer's V coefficients (0.287), indicates a moderate association. These results allow accepting hypothesis 1, by demonstrating that ethical perception constitutes a relevant factor that conditions the participation of these types of companies in public contracting processes, with negative perception being a barrier to entry into these processes.

Regarding the second hypothesis, which states that company size significantly influences its participation in public procurement processes, the analysis results confirm the existence of a significant relationship between the two variables. Pearson's Chi-square test yielded a value of X² = 19.854; p < 0.001, which indicates that the participation of companies differs according to their size. According to the level of association, the data recorded Phi and Cramer's V values of 0.189, which implies a weak-moderate but statistically significant relationship. These results suggest that company size somewhat conditions the probability of participation in public contracting processes.

Finally, regarding the third hypothesis, which poses that the level of human talent capacity influences the participation of companies in public procurement processes, the results obtained suggest a statistically significant association between both variables. Pearson's Chi-square test reached a value of X² = 10.895 and a significance level of p = 0.012, which allows accepting the proposed hypothesis. The magnitude of the relationship was measured using Phi and Cramer's V, whose value was 0.140, indicating a weak but statistically significant association between the level of human talent capacity and business participation. These results suggest that companies with better levels of internal capabilities in their human talent show a higher probability of participating in public contracting processes.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that the size of companies significantly influences participation in public procurement processes. In this sense, the literature indicates that differences in productive capacities, access to resources, and productivity levels generate unequal conditions to access more demanding markets (CEPAL, 2021, 22). Likewise, it is identified that limitations for commercialization and market access affect smaller companies more (Llisterri, 2002, 3), which makes it understandable that these types of companies face greater difficulties in participating in processes such as public contracting.

On the other hand, the results associated with human talent capacity show a significant relationship with participation in public procurement, which aligns with the theoretical approach that highlights the importance of training and business development services as key factors for competitiveness. In this sense, authors such as Oddonne and Stola (2025) point out that strengthening internal capabilities allows SMEs to improve their performance and access new markets. Similarly, the theoretical framework highlights that the articulation between knowledge and practice contributes to improving productivity and business response capacity, which is consistent with the results obtained.

Finally, regarding ethical perception, the results show a significant association with participation in public contracting processes; the reviewed literature recognizes the importance of transparency, institutional trust, and control mechanisms as basic elements for the proper functioning of public procurement (Harper, Calderón y Muñoz, 2016). From that perspective, it can be interpreted that a negative perception about the ethics and equity of the processes acts as a barrier to entry for companies, suggesting the need to delve deeper into this component for future research and theoretical developments.

Conclusions

Public procurement represents 8.6% of Gross Domestic Product, and in Ecuador, this percentage exceeds 16%. In this space, inclusive public procurement emerges with the social purpose of incorporating sectors traditionally excluded by the system, such as businesses led by women, local ones, those from vulnerable sectors, and SMEs. This inclusion represents an opportunity for these businesses, proposing the correction of structural problems by opening spaces in public procurement processes.

There are Latin American experiences of inclusive procurement that demonstrate the complexity and richness of incorporating excluded sectors. In these processes, some common factors are identified regarding the importance of training the sectors being incorporated, so they can develop products that become part of the productive chain or go directly to sale. Likewise, it is crucial to understand this inclusion process as something that requires progressiveness and sustainability over time, which allows the adaptation of the involved parties and promotes development that lasts.

Public policies supporting the productive sector, focused on public contracting processes for SMEs, must incorporate a component aimed at guaranteeing and demonstrating transparency in these procedures. Only in this way will companies perceive these spaces as reliable and equitable. Reinforcing these institutional elements not only improves the legitimacy of the system but could also incentivize greater participation of SMEs in public procurement, expanding their access to a strategic market for their growth and sustainability, given the relationship found between the ethical perception of these processes and their participation.

It is essential to establish and reinforce differentiated public contracting processes by business size, which will allow real and equitable access to these spaces for companies of different scales. Given that the Ecuadorian productive structure is mostly comprised of microenterprises, which represent nearly 93% of the total productive units in the country, it is indispensable that public contracting mechanisms consider their specific capabilities, limitations, and needs. The adaptation of these processes constitutes a key element in strengthening the smallest productive sector in the country and promoting its effective participation in this market.

The training and development of human talent constitute a fundamental axis for SMEs seeking to participate in public contracting processes. Evidence shows that internal qualification is related to companies' predisposition to engage in these procedures. Thus, it is indispensable that SMEs encourage the hiring and development of qualified personnel—with competencies in the use of digital applications, legal regulations, and paperwork specialized in public procurement—since this strengthening of internal capabilities increases the possibility of effectively accessing this strategic public sector market.

Definitely, as can be seen, orienting public procurement toward the revitalization of the productive fabric with an emphasis on SMEs is a powerful public policy tool for promoting production and local and national employment generation.

Hence, the importance that this tool is used efficiently and ethically, adapted to the development of new technologies that, due to their innovative impact power, can collaborate in consolidating transparency and the inclusion of productive actors who require opportunities to commercialize their products in a timely and continuous manner.

Now, of course, to take advantage of the business opportunity emerging from public contracting, it is fundamental that, thinking in an integrated manner, strengthening programs be designed and executed to improve the quality, productivity, and innovation of potential companies to become part of these types of initiatives. Here, national and local government entities can also help in alliance with other actors interested in improving the competitiveness of the productive fabric of sectors such as SMEs which, on many occasions, are limited because their production and commercialization processes cannot cover the demands of quantity, quality, and continuity of supplies that the buyer generally expects—in this case, of state origin—so that what it does is not paralyzed and, above all, negative effects are avoided in the satisfaction of users of services that government organizations tend to provide in key sectors of society's functioning.

Finally, within this entire set of empowering actions for inclusive public procurement aimed at the revitalization of sectors such as SMEs, an element that must be considered to close the empowering circle of the state contracting system is the meticulous and critical observance directed at learning from good practices present in other countries in the region.
