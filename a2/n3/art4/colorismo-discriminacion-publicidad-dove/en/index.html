Abstract.

Unlike traditional racism, colorism focuses on preferences based on skin color, even within different racial groups. In Mexico, racial discrimination is omnipresent in the media, and advertising favors individuals with lighter skin despite the majority of people having intermediate tones. These advertisements seen on television and social media can influence people's perceptions and acceptance of beauty standards, creating stereotypes. The research focuses on a comparative study of two Dove ads that reveal a pattern: the comparison between light and dark tones presented in a way that questioned the impact of colorism in Mexico.

Keywords:

Colorism; whiteness; discrimination; stereotypes; advertising.

Introduction

Discrimination against people of color has been present throughout history on the basis of notions of inferiority, in which people with dark skin tones were treated as an inferior race by white people, even being associated with impurity, since racial discrimination occurs when a member of a racial group is subjected to different or unequal treatment based on their race (Azabache, 2017).

In recent years, a new term has been added to discussions on racism, one that classifies people by their skin color, without them necessarily realizing that their features and traits cause them to be shut out of opportunities: colorism. Unlike classic racism, this term does not typically use the category of race to legitimize a supposed essential difference between people and groups, but instead uses the category of color; nonetheless, for both concepts, the main points of reference are based on physical features, superficial aspects of the person. In this way, colorism could be called phenotype racism, which, unlike cultural racism or raceless racism, is based on the belief in a supposed superiority of some cultures over others (Tipa, 2020).

According to Hunter (2007) and Jones (2000, both cited in the work of Tipa, 2020), colorism privileges lighter skin tones; unlike scientific racism, this form of discrimination also operates within racialized groups, that is, it classifies people by racialized groups — Black, Latino, Asian, among others — who will experience exclusion and discrimination in general, but within these groups, people with lighter skin tones receive greater privileges (Tipa, 2020).

The fact that colorism is considered an expression of racism is a crucial point. Discrimination based on skin color is a manifestation of racism that operates at subtle and often internalized levels within societies; research and observations have shown that, even within racially diverse communities, skin color can influence how people are perceived and treated; systemic and structural racism also plays an important role in perpetuating colorism.

The terms colorist and phenotype help address racism more precisely; the inclusion of other physical attributes, such as eye and hair color, within the concept of colorist racism underscores the complexity of this form of discrimination, which is not limited solely to skin color but can include a range of physical characteristics. This highlights how the perception of a person's beauty and worth is often tied to conformity with certain standards of physical appearance.

The concept of colorism was first mentioned in 1983 by writer Alice Walker (cited in FundéuRAE, 2020), to describe the social and cultural phenomenon referring to discrimination against and preference for individuals based on their skin tone among people of the same ethnic or racial group. In Mexico, it is easy to identify the problem of colorism in media representation, specifically on television, because even though 66% of the Mexican population has light-to-medium brown skin and 6% have dark brown skin (Campos and Rivas, 2020), advertisements are shown with characters whose skin tones are not representative of this.

In this regard, the Survey on the Consumption of Audiovisual Content (ENCCA, 2022, cited in the Instituto Federal de Telecomunicaciones IFT, 2022) notes that the Mexican population consumes an average of 2.5 hours a day per person of broadcast television and 3 hours of video on internet platforms; additionally, 77% of people continue to use broadcast television as their main audiovisual medium, and among the most-watched content on this medium are newscasts and movies.

It is thus possible to infer that, given the average amount of time a Mexican spends in front of the television, they are exposed to advertisements that show a biased or stereotyped representation of skin tones, to which Tipa (2019a) notes that, in the collective imagination, the people shown in advertisements start from the premise of creating a sense of empathy with the public, thereby promoting certain beauty standards and the vilification of others in order to favor the perception and acceptance of the products being promoted.

Additionally, advertising discourse, understood as the communication used in advertising to convey a persuasive message to a specific audience, with the aim of selling a product or service based on a set of strategies (Yalán, 2018, cited in Monteros, 2021), is used to attract the public's attention and generate interest, so that television creates clichés that typify social strata, origin, race, gender, or any other socially-typified condition, meaning these advertising clichés can have a significant impact on society, as they become behavioral models.

According to Durin and Vázquez (2013, cited in Tipa, 2019a), the content shown in the media is a way of representing how people recognize one another, but above all the expectation of what they are entitled to be or receive, so that the models used for the visual component of advertising play a fundamental role in shaping public perception (Castoriadis, 1989; Vergara, 2002, cited in Tipa, 2019a), which includes what is desired or undesired in terms of physical and bodily appearance, encompassing aspects such as skin tone, eye color, hair color, and other similar characteristics, so that through these imagined representations the population learns and assimilates certain ideas and standards, which it tends to reproduce, such that these imagined constructs become meanings and mental representations that function as a point of reference allowing people to refer to a specific concept or idea.

For Adorno (2009, cited in Rodríguez et al., 2022), these situations become clichés that create "the illusion of duplicated life," where life is duplicated on screen and takes the place of life itself, making it possible to recognize that the media can shape people's perceptions and attitudes by presenting behavioral models that can be adopted or imitated. For Marway (2017, cited in Monteros, 2021), in the case of women, they are influenced to believe that the beauty standard lies in white, radiant skin, and therefore any difference in their skin falls outside the canon of beauty, which is why advertising disrupts their lives in various ways and generates expectations that can even lead them to put their health at risk.

In the case of Mexico, this situation has been called into question by some researchers (Tipa, 2020, 2021, 2023a, 2023b; Chong, 2022; Vargas, 2017), who, given the country's ethnic and cultural diversity, note that advertising faces significant challenges in terms of equitable representation, since colorism displayed in advertising, in addition to being unethical, reflects discrimination based on skin tone.

This situation occurs not only in advertising campaigns for domestic products, but multinational brands, which call themselves socially responsible, also engage in this type of practice, and even when some commercials are localized to the region where they will be shown, the standard of perfect white skin still shows through, as is the case with the DOVE brand, which in recent years has diversified its advertising; nonetheless, it continues to emphasize, in certain commercials, the presentation of light-skinned models in contrast with models with darker skin tones, in which dark-skinned actresses are presented as a "before" and light-skinned and/or lighter-toned actresses as an "after," generating a sense of aspirationalism and a false belief that the population should be white.

In this sense, this paper seeks to describe how the advertising spots "Jabón de ducha" (Shower Soap) and "Real Beauty," launched in Mexico by the Dove brand, promote discrimination through colorism by showing a "before" for darker skin tones and an "after" for lighter skin tones, comparing the content that promotes colorism-based discrimination under the premise of skincare in these advertisements.

State of the Art and Theoretical Framework

Stereotypes in advertising

In the field of advertising, stereotypes manifest as conventional and preconceived representations of various social groups, genders, races, or physical characteristics. These representations are used to convey advertising messages, but can perpetuate mistaken or simplistic ideas. For example, gender stereotypes in advertising tend to portray women as consumers obsessed with beauty and men as dominant providers. Racial stereotype is tied to skin color or race, differentiating between white and Black people, while ethnicity refers to groups possessing distinct qualities and characteristics of life, encompassing cultural, political, and social aspects (Azabache, 2017). In general terms, then, a stereotype refers to a simplified and often rigid belief or representation of a group, individual, or characteristic, based on certain perceived or assumed traits.

When discussing racial stereotypes in advertising, these can be recognized even before advertising content is produced; racial stereotyping in advertising is present from the moment the physical requirements that actors participating in castings must meet are established. Tipa (2020) identifies that the leading roles in advertising content are granted to individuals with light skin tones, a characteristic also associated with social status and purchasing power, a situation that becomes evident in advertising, not only through the leading role but also as a marketing strategy to present aspirationalism, evoking in the public or consumers the desire to see themselves in that position, so that advertising constructs imaginaries around the whiteness of the body, accompanied by symbolic and economic rewards such as status and power. In this way, a person will consciously or unconsciously seek to belong to the imaginary presented by advertising and will be able to modify themselves in order to reach what is reflected by the ads, in which, being a light-skinned individual, one can have better status, economic position, and even power.

For Azabache (2017), colorism in advertising, in addition to the expectation created by whiteness, also fosters discrimination against Blackness; that is, the author found that, in telecommunications advertising in a Caribbean region, the Black race was shown as the target customers for prepaid phone service, while white people were targeted for post-paid plans and services, which is why, for Queiroz (2017, cited in Azabache, 2017), it is not race that drives communications, but rather the aspirations it presents, so these actions reinforce a racist stereotype tied to the purchasing power of one race relative to another, thereby promoting, in addition to discrimination, the construction of prejudices associated both with skin color and with the attitude people are assumed to hold.

Beauty standards in advertising

A beauty standard, in general terms, refers to a set of physical characteristics, aesthetic attributes, or qualities that a society or culture considers ideal in terms of appearance and attractiveness. These standards vary from era to era and from culture to culture, but tend to influence the collective perception of beauty. In the context of advertising, they are used to promote products and services related to physical appearance, such as cosmetics and fashion. However, advertising tends to reinforce or generate beauty standards that can be unattainable or unrealistic, which can lead to the promotion of an unhealthy body image or the discrimination of certain types of beauty.

When it comes to beauty standards, society is accustomed to living with preconceived ideas about how a person's physical appearance should be in order to properly fit in. As Katz (2021) notes, this social construction is formed from representations that condition and legitimize power, inequality, and domination, as well as the idea that consuming goods generates status. This practice is used by some women-oriented brands, such as Dove, which create the idea that women must always look good and, above all, lighten their skin to achieve the image expected and desired by society, since a person with white skin generally represents greater authority or power.

In this sense, Barkty (2015, cited in Quel, 2023) notes that the beauty ideal is a patriarchal construct and proposes that women free themselves from these ideals; nonetheless, in reality this ideal affects women of different cultures, which is why it must be discussed, since these standards and stereotypes have been shaped since antiquity, when a woman was considered beautiful if she had a slim build, white and pale skin, and long, fine, blond hair (Dsigno, 2017, cited in Quel, 2023).

Racialization in advertising

Traditionally, racialization has been understood as an identification process that involves the hierarchization and exclusion of individuals based on their racial characteristics; this process is based on the mistaken belief that skin color determines a subject's supposed essence, and has tangible consequences that influence different areas of their lives. Even though social theories of race claim to have abandoned the biologicist approaches that have historically defined the discussion of race, they fail to free the discourse enough to recognize that race is a condition of the body that manifests in a social context (Pérez et al., 2022). In this sense, historically, and from a young age, society is given ideas about citizens with similar characteristics that can lead to a mistaken perception of them; for example, the idea that simply because someone has a darker skin tone than most, this might mean they are a domestic worker or a construction worker, because, thanks to advertisements or the very ideas individuals form throughout their lives, this mistaken perception is created.

For Mbembe (2016, cited in Pérez Casanovas et al., 2022), race is that which can be kept at a distance from oneself and which can be discarded once it is no longer useful, setting aside the virtue of an essence, a culture, or a way of acting, and instead being granted by virtue of a racial assignment, a naming that comes from outside the self. One's racial assignment is not placed by oneself but by another, in order to make of them an excluded person, an identity imposed upon them. However, this discourse has a flip side: the racialized subject, as history shows, has not remained inert or passive in the face of racial assignment — on the contrary, racialized subjects have known how to leap from imposed external assignment to re-signification, the latter understood by Mbembe (2016, cited in Pérez et al., 2022) as the calling of race, distinct from racial assignment. The former is a political act: it gives rise to what seeks to be rendered invisible; the latter, by contrast, is the act of rendering invisible, of imposing a racial imaginary constructed from the discourse of power.

Discrimination associated with colorism in advertising

Discrimination is a term that, according to the Governing Council of the Revista Mexicana de Orientación Educativa (REMO, 2011), refers to the act of treating a person or group of people unequally or unfairly because of characteristics such as their race, gender, sexual orientation, religion, or other personal attributes; in this sense, discrimination can manifest in various forms, such as unfair treatment in employment, education, access to services, or the denial of opportunities due to prejudices based on specific characteristics; colorist discrimination, on the other hand, focuses on differential treatment based on a person's skin tone or skin color, and is a specific aspect of racial discrimination; in this case, people are discriminated against not so much for their race as such, but for the tone of their skin.

For Azabache (2017), racial discrimination occurs when an individual belonging to a racial group is treated differently or unequally in various situations, based on their membership in a given race; with this, colorism is considered a further evolution or expression of racism, which, according to Tipa (2020), functions under the criterion of color, since it takes into account people's physical characteristics, thereby constituting a way of discriminating based on skin color, as well as eye and hair color, which places it within a type of colorist racism that also includes racist attitudes and practices based on the superiority of the other, and therefore, occupy lower positions in production, social, or power relations, thereby becoming a subtler form of racism, somewhat harder to detect since it is not as explicit, but still following the principles of segregating and demeaning people with darker skin tones according to their physical characteristics, while exalting finer features.

In the field of marketing and the perception of personal-care and household-cleaning products, Rodríguez et al. (2022) found that 20th-century advertising continues to fix the Black condition to a prejudice rooted in slavery in the collective imagination, associating it with dirtiness, so that soap and hygiene are again represented as a conquest of the white man and are explicitly promoted as measures of wealth, civilization, health, and purity for societies. In other words, there is a clear precedent in advertising in which dark skin tones carry the historical weight associated with poverty, slavery, and nonexistent hygiene practices compared to conquering men.

In this sense, advertising content fosters colorism and contributes to the structural racism that shapes everyday interpersonal relations; these color-based media representations naturalize racism, in addition to associating it with the socioeconomic restrictions linked to skin tones (Tipa, 2020). In television content from the early 2000s through the mid-2010s, a marked white representation of the Mexican population can be noted in advertising spots, from toys, medicines, and retail chains to hygiene and skincare products, which left Mexicans with an altered perception of reality regarding their own skin color.

Whitism and whiteness in advertising

The term whiteness is another structural racial phenomenon that marks non-Western ethnic groups as inferior; as Echeverría (2007, cited in Rodríguez et al., 2022) explains, within the development of the discourse of whiteness there is a hierarchy between bodies that deserve development and others that must pay for it; in this sense, someone with a dark skin tone can whiten their image as long as they can afford it, and doing so means adapting to the moral and ethical discourse of capitalism, such that non-white people must spend their resources aspiring to be whiter in order to attain the same privileges, in which, moreover, whiteness is the visibilization of capitalism as identity, determined by racial whiteness, though this is relative, since hierarchized identities do not depend solely on race but do mark positions of disadvantage or privilege based on history (Rodríguez et al., 2022), which contributes to generating unequal power relations in society, reflected in the different forms of interaction among individuals.

With respect to advertising, Tipa (2020) notes that ad content creates an image around whiteness, implying that it thereby involves symbolic and socioeconomic rewards of status, so that advertising pressures women around the world to move closer to the ideal appearance based on the physical, presenting situations that turn desire into something that can be satisfied with an object.

Methodology

For the design of this research, an explanatory approach was selected, whose main objective is to identify the relationships between variables and provide a deeper understanding of the phenomenon investigated. According to authors such as Hernández and Mendoza (2018), this methodology is appropriate when the aim is not only to describe and characterize phenomena, but also to explain the underlying reasons for certain behaviors or outcomes.

A qualitative approach was used for data collection and analysis, given that its reflective nature favors the analysis of the experiences, feelings, and meanings that social phenomena hold for those who take part in them (Pérez, 2002), which made it possible to delve deeper into topics such as racialization, whiteness, and, above all, colorism.

For data collection, focus groups were carried out in order to learn about the perspectives of different groups of people regarding the commercials analyzed in this document; this technique was chosen because it makes it possible to obtain information about the various ideas and feelings participants have (Rabiee, 2004) regarding colorism and whiteness in advertising. A semi-structured interview was used, based on open-ended questions, which, applied through a focus group, makes it possible to obtain in-depth and honest responses that might not be given individually, while also making it possible to narrow down the topic of discussion (Hernández and Sepúlveda, 2022). These were conducted via the Google Meet platform, with 4 people participating in each one, which made it possible to record the sessions after informed consent. Theoretical saturation was used as the criterion for the fieldwork (Ortiz, 2015); this was reached in the fourth focus group, but an additional one was conducted to validate it.

The selection of informants was based on specific criteria to ensure the representativeness and relevance of the voices and perspectives gathered; in this case, Mexican citizens residing in Guadalajara, Jalisco, between 19 and 45 years old, with varied skin tones identified using the color classification developed by the Project on Ethnicity and Race in Latin America (PERLA, see image 1), in order to obtain a general perspective and better appreciation of the variables and content of the ads.

The DOVE brand

The brand's origins date back to World War II, when the American army added moisturizing cream to its soap to clean the wounded, a factor that would later come to identify the brand. In a beauty and cosmetics market, where the trends generated by brands to stand out presented models and beauty stereotypes marked by a certain height, weight, hair type, and flawless skin as if it were a doll, Dove wanted to present itself as the friendly brand, under the idea of "Democratic Beauty," proposing an equal appreciation of each woman's particular and inimitable beauty. From here, they analyzed research from several medical studies on the perception of beauty and its social influence, in order to launch their own study leading to much of what is now the brand's strategy, "real beauty."

While the campaigns born under this premise were accepted by the public, some criticized the strategy, arguing that the "real" women in the campaign did not correspond to the average and that, among normal people, they tend to be fairly attractive. In this sense, as a precedent regarding its pursuit of being a brand that addresses a social issue, the launch of the campaigns discussed in this article — "Jabón de ducha" (2011) and "Real Beauty" (2017) — was called into question, since the interpretation of racism through colorism is present in the pieces shown.

For the selection criteria of the Dove brand's advertising pieces, it was established that the content should present a comparison of color or tone between a dark-skinned person and a lighter-skinned one, implying that changing one's skin to lighter tones is what a person should aspire to in order to meet the standards reflected in the brand's communication, so the advertising spots "Jabón de ducha" (Shower Soap) and "Real Beauty" were chosen, which emphasize the promotion of white skin under the premise of skincare (see images 2 and 3).

Results

The following presents the results for each of the categories of analysis that served to delimit this work.

Colorism

Within this category, which refers to phenotype racism, since it is based on people's superficial physical traits and also rests on the belief in a supposed superiority of some cultures over others: people with light skin tones usually enjoy more privileges simply for that reason — that is colorism.

After the advertisements used for this work were shown, one participant stated: "yes, colorism is being promoted, by the way each model is positioned" (P1, F4), while another participant noted:

Of course they're promoting it, both through their examples of putting someone with much darker skin and gradually lightening it, to someone, well, white; that's what they're promoting, and that type of product should be, and should demonstrate that it's for little spots, in the armpit, the groin, even the elbows or knees, but instead they put someone whose skin color changes completely, and that has nothing to do with what the product is supposedly for, what the product is for (P1, F5).

These comments make it possible to recognize the presence of a hierarchy in which people with dark skin are shown to be encouraged to change their skin color to a lighter one, which, as Tipa (2021) notes, means people within racialized groups are motivated to change their skin tone, because it is believed that even within these groups, those with lighter skin tones tend to receive greater privileges.

Racialization

This category refers to a social construction on scales based on race, assigning value and specific characteristics and behaviors to people, in which those belonging to a "superior" race enjoy privileges. The main finding regarding racialization was that dark-skinned models are always presented in very different ways from lighter-skinned models: "A person with a darker complexion, sometimes they put them with messier hair or in different circumstances" (P2, F5).

For Azabache (2017), this can be associated with racial discrimination, which manifests when an individual belonging to a racial group is treated differently or unequally, though for Tipa (2021), this arises because brown-skinned people are represented in different socioeconomic and cultural contexts.

Additionally, this finding raises a new question: could it be that the inclusion of brands in advertising is associated with the presentation of racial stereotypes, because even though they are included in advertising, their role is presented under unequal conditions compared to those with light skin tones.

Whiteness

This category refers to the association with power and privilege that people with lighter skin have compared to the discrimination and inequality suffered by people with darker skin color; it is a concept that questions and makes visible a social issue, showing what sociocultural structures look like. For participant 1 of focus group 3, speaking about the content of the advertising, she notes that "white, the lightest, the perfect, blemish-free skin is best, that's what the ad wants me to understand" (P1, F3).

In this sense, all the focus-group participants unanimously noted that lighter skin tone grants greater privileges, leading people to aspire to reach that standard: "you're taught to believe that white people are kinder, smarter, more of everything in every sense" (P2, F3).

These testimonies underscore the powerful influence that whiteness standards have in shaping people's perceptions and aspirations, which, for Navarrete (2020), represents a significant connection between skin tone and a country's socioeconomic status; the author notes that those with lighter skin generally enjoy higher incomes, higher levels of education, and occupy more advantageous positions compared to those with darker skin.

Discrimination

This category refers to the act of treating a person or group of people unequally or unfairly because of characteristics such as their race, gender, sexual orientation, religion, or other personal attributes. Focus-group participants repeatedly reflected that these are issues they grew up with regardless of their context, from school to their own home: "I feel like a lot of times since we were little, even the older people would say things like, oh, look how pretty, so fair-skinned!" (P3, F1), which unconsciously leads them to try to look similar in order to gain that same approval, since it is considered that, "if you're not white and blonde, you're not pretty" (P4, F2).

The focus-group participants believe that, if a person receives positive comments and attention for having a lighter skin tone, that will be what they seek their whole life, since they need to feel adapted and belong to that group; they also believe that, unfortunately, this happens from an early age when one is more susceptible; children grow up with this type of idea because of their environment, ranging from television content to comments at home, which reinforce discrimination based on skin tone:

My skin is very dark-brown, so ever since I was little I was kind of like… I want to lighten my skin… because it's not like what I see on TV, it's not what I see in the ads, but I always wanted to lighten my skin to feel prettier, to feel like I fit in, so that if people noticed me it was because, oh, I have pretty skin! So a lot of times… yes, there are certain negative comments directed at you because of your skin color, there's a certain discrimination… [because since childhood] you're left thinking, what's going on here, like, is it wrong that my skin color is brown, or is it wrong that you think having slightly whiter skin is better (P3, F2).

This participant's experience can be explained by what Muñiz (2013, cited in Tipa 2019b) notes, namely that the expectations society has imposed correspond to Western beauty standards, marked by white skin, blond hair, and Caucasian features, so that being outside these standards subjects people to discrimination.

Beauty standards

Regarding beauty standards, which, as already mentioned, correspond to a set of physical characteristics, aesthetic attributes, or qualities that a society or culture considers ideal in terms of appearance and attractiveness, it was possible to identify that, for the participants, beauty standards are very well established, to the point of already being a benchmark in collective thinking; some of the participants have felt self-conscious, since the beauty represented in television advertising has made them believe they are not pretty enough:

Everything has to be perfect and well cared for and in excellent condition because otherwise you don't fit the beauty standard, because now your nose size or the thickness of your lips isn't quite right, so I think the typical American image still predominates (P1, F5).

For another participant, this can be directly linked to Mexican culture:

Mexicans have always had it instilled in us as a culture that having brown, olive-toned skin is bad; in fact, beauty standards always look for, like, whiter, blonder girls (P4, F3).

In this regard, González (2018) notes that if, in the early years, girls are taught that they will be admired, praised, and capable of attracting love to the point that this becomes normalized among women, they will eventually accept the imposed standards, reaffirming the idea promoted by the beauty industry. So if one does not have flawless skin, a certain height, weight, or a light skin tone, one automatically does not fit the canons imposed by advertising, only able to aspire to enter that standard by consuming certain products that promise that, by changing your body, you can be accepted by society. He also states that companies dedicated to beauty products seek to highlight flaws in women's bodies, which will lead them to an insufficient self-image, in order to eventually convince them that they must change according to the products these companies offer, so that a person who has grown up with these messages about perfection through a typically white and flawless model will unconsciously grow up with the idea that the image they see on television is what they must become in order to feel pretty (González, 2018).

Stereotypes

This category refers to certain specific characteristics some people have, mainly shown in the models or actors who appear in advertisements. Stereotypes, like beauty standards, produce a mistaken idea of how a person should look or be in order to be perceived as someone important, or even simply to feel more beautiful or appear to have a higher socioeconomic status; in this sense, the participants acknowledge that: "The stereotypes I've seen in advertising are the typical image we have of the little fair-skinned girl with blue eyes, green eyes, straight blonde hair, tall, thin" (P1, F1); or "being thin is better, light skin is better, straight, long hair is better" (P3, F3).

From Katz's (2021) point of view, this would be explained by the fact that social construction is formed from representations that condition and legitimize power, inequality, and domination, as well as the idea that consuming goods generates status.

Conclusions

This research made it possible to recognize the perception that exists of skin color within Mexican society. While the results cannot be generalized given the size of the study population, comments can be observed that, while seemingly harmless, relate to skin tones but reveal how light skin is exalted over dark skin, which poses a risk, since younger family members are susceptible to repeating these comments, growing up with them, or coming to feel belittled for not resembling the models shown on television.

Despite time constraints, it was possible to conclude that colorism is present in the advertisements analyzed, and that, despite brands' efforts to create inclusion, the same types of models continue to be used, with the typical physical traits of having straight hair, being thin and attractive; but one of the most notable elements, and the one on which this research was based, was skin color — the models are generally light-skinned people. Regarding the Dove brand, the interviewees agreed that the ads shown promote colorism, whether through the actors used because of their physical traits or through the context presented in the ad.

As part of this research, it was possible to recognize the existence of racism associated with colorism, based mainly on a supposed superiority of a white person compared to a dark-skinned person, since the comments made it possible to recognize the perception of how white people receive greater privileges simply because of their skin color.

Although this work focused on commercials aimed at the use of skin-lightening creams, it is suggested that a line of research be opened focused on the representation of different skin tones in other advertising content, in order to identify whether this type of perception related to aspiring toward white skin is present, and how it influences consumers' purchasing decisions.
