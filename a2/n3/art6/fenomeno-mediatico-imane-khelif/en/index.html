Abstract.

Through digital ethnography, we seek to describe the gender stereotypes that fueled the media phenomenon that arose following the participation of Algerian boxer Imane Khelif after her encounter with boxer Angela Carini during the Paris 2024 Olympics, an event that unleashed a series of misinformation by the media that reached the hands of Internet users who took a violent and discriminatory stance towards the Algerian boxer. The results of this research showed the structural violence that continues to be replicated especially on racialized women. In addition, the lack of veracity on the part of the media directly influences the perception of viewers, generating a hostile environment within online communities, which means a setback in the search for social parity. Future lines of research are proposed that delve deeper into the study of the dominant cultures that online communities present, as well as their ideologies, and how these directly affect the perception of the various communication phenomena that are generated from the spread of misinformation in various fields of study.

Keywords:

Hate speech, misinformation, gender stereotypes, social networks, systemic violence.

Introduction

Disinformation on social media has proven to be a powerful catalyst for the creation of hate speech and the rise of discriminatory attitudes toward certain social groups. Although social media has great potential to facilitate global communication, the mass dissemination of fake news and its capacity to influence public perception poses serious challenges. This phenomenon not only affects public opinion, but also contributes to the formation of virtual communities that reinforce discriminatory beliefs, generating a spiral of radicalization.

The capacity of these environments to create echo chambers and distort reality worsens the problem, since users are repeatedly exposed to false narratives that legitimize attitudes of hatred and exclusion, and despite platforms' efforts to mitigate disinformation and hate speech, moderation and regulation mechanisms still face major challenges.

In this sense, the question guiding this research concerns the way in which traditional gender stereotypes contribute to the generation of gender-based violence on social media, taking as a case study the controversy surrounding boxer Imane Khelif in the context of the Paris 2024 Olympic Games, and its objective is to describe the traditional gender stereotypes that contributed to generating gender-based violence on social media around this controversy, in order to make visible the systemic violence exhibited by social media users regarding women's participation in sporting disciplines traditionally considered masculine.

It is important to give visibility to issues of gender discrimination, especially in the sporting field, which has been dominated by men since its inception; although the Paris 2024 Olympics are presumed to be the most balanced yet in terms of the number of female participants relative to men, a certain stigma still exists around women's participation in some sporting disciplines considered masculine, which strongly affects the perception spectators and the media have of participants in these disciplines, as in the case of Algerian boxer Imane Khelif, who was subjected to violent comments and discriminatory discourse fueled by the media and reproduced by hundreds of users across various digital platforms.

This work contributes to research on the systematic violence faced by women within the sporting field and their representation in the media, through the methodology used, which corresponds to a digital ethnography, based on Imane Khelif's online community, analyzing the behavior of users of the X platform surrounding the controversial encounter between the Algerian boxer and Italian boxer Angela Carini, which unleashed a series of violent comments and the reproduction of disinformation motivated by a discriminatory stance seeking to invalidate Khelif's athletic career and condemn her participation in the Paris 2024 Olympics.

While it is true that the first half of the 20th century brought a notable advance in women's rights, including in athletic activities, the press has been the main enemy of women's athletic potential, placing them in the public eye for their physical appearance rather than their abilities, which devalues women's performance in sport and makes it a focus of criticism and the rejection of women's sport in society (García, 2011).

Even though sport is a centuries-old practice that has evolved both in its rules and among its practitioners, gaps remain, well into the 21st century, between male and female athletes, and media coverage of women's sports has been scarce in terms of valuable content, since female athletes have at times been excluded and marginalized, because sports media are a space that prioritizes sports played by men, broadcast by and for men (Salido and Muñoz, 2021).

From a media standpoint, Salido and Muñoz (2021) denounce the lack of representation in media coverage of women who take part in the Olympics; they even note that in 2015 a total of 11 publications were produced, in which, moreover, the pieces lack an equal-opportunity approach, trivializing the athletes' achievements by emphasizing the athletes' physical appearance during their participation, sexualizing their bodies and routines, and reproducing sexist discourse.

Roca and Navarro (2022) propose something similar, noting that the sports press is the main vehicle for rejection and marginalization toward women who practice disciplines traditionally associated with the male gender, undervaluing athletes' performance and achievements, while for Román et al. (2021), this lack of representation is due to an androcentric outlook on the part of the media, in which women do not play an important role, making the transition toward journalism with a gender perspective more difficult — not because there are no women journalists interested in covering these segments, but because it is very difficult for them to gain access to these spaces, and even when they do, they do not obtain the same working conditions as their male counterparts. For Fernández et al. (2022), these acts reflect the violence women experience in the world of sport, since this violence does not refer only to physical spaces such as stadiums, arenas, and podiums, but also to the manifestations that occur outside of them, such as media coverage, in which the canons of beauty imposed on Olympic participants continue to carry weight, along with the criticism they face for not meeting them, since the belief persists that a woman is valued far more for her physical appearance than for her skills and/or performance.

According to Donoso et al. (2022), media coverage suggests a narrative through images and discourses that can either feed the reproduction of or the rejection of stereotypes, mainly those related to gender, ethnicity, and socioeconomic, political, cultural, and/or religious status. In this sense, hate speech can arise within these digital spaces which, thanks to media coverage and the immediacy of information, can have dangerous reach that suggests the stigmatization of the female figure as a social, political, and cultural entity, one that often falls under this dynamic of comparison and inequality relative to men (Salido and Muñoz, 2021; Roca and Navarro, 2022; Fernández et al., 2022).

In this way, the media become a platform where the information spread through them comes to influence the way individuals come to perceive and construct the identity both of themselves and of others, and, therefore, construct standards and stereotypes for those women who are part of the sporting world (Pérez, 2020).

In the current digital era, things have not changed: hate speech is part of everyday life and affects female athletes (Gutiérrez, 2023), particularly on social media, which, unlike traditional media, have become spaces where sexualization, discrimination, and silencing occur en masse, in real time, and often anonymously, a phenomenon that is worsened by disinformation and disproportionately affects female athletes.

The immediacy and easy access to information offered by the Internet and social media have brought many benefits; nonetheless, they are also responsible for many media outlets being prone to spreading news without cross-checking or verifying it, which means some of it ends up being false. Social media has become the best medium for the spread of rumors, which turn into news; nonetheless, this two-way communication process is exempt from controls that assess the veracity of the information disseminated, causing journalistic rigor and fact-checking of its content to disappear (González and García, 2015).

Social media has become the perfect space for the expression of intolerant ideologies to present their opinions and discourses, creating a kind of culture of hatred within the communities on these platforms. They are a space of impunity for the anger and hatred of users with radical ideals; the problem of hate speech on the internet is such a significant issue that many organizations have considered it a matter to address within their agendas (Cabo and García, 2017).

Theoretical Framework

Gender is defined as a "should be," based on sociocultural definitions for both men and women and the different social spheres they are meant to occupy (Martínez and Bonilla, 2000, cited in Melero, 2010); for Melero (2010), gender comprises relationships and processes that form a social construct rooted in the differentiation of the sexes, while Nash and Maure (2001, cited in Melero, 2010) note that social norms come to establish characteristics distinguishing the masculine from the feminine, shaping gender identity and power relations between both sexes. Thus, gender is considered a social construct, derived from a person's biological aspects, delimiting what one should or should not do, defined as masculinity and femininity (Lopez and Güida, 2000, cited in Chaves, 2012). Consequently, these social constructs foster the creation of gender stereotypes and are assigned based on generalized traits attributed to men and women, creating a division that strengthens gender inequality.

According to Castillo and Montes (2014), gender stereotypes are beliefs associated with characteristics that sustain gender discrimination. For Moya (2003, cited in Castillo and Montes, 2014), this represents the set of social beliefs about the attributes of each gender, such that gender stereotypes create a large gap that enables the emergence of inequalities between men and women across different social spheres, and this inequality is understood as the disparity in access to resources and opportunities, often perpetuated by both cultural and social norms, which come to further affect and foster gender inequality (Cortés, 2015).

On the other hand, for Flores et al. (2016, cited in Rodríguez et al., 2022), it refers to any action that places a person in a situation of inequality because they belong to one sex or the other. In the sporting field, Rodríguez et al. (2022) note that when people think about sports, they do so in gendered terms, since society makes a notable division when it comes to sports.

Gender stereotypes, like gender inequality, cannot be attributed to one person, but are instead part of a systematic violence directed toward the female gender. Martínez (2012, cited in Martínez and Cabezas, 2022) defines systemic violence as a type of violence that cannot be attributed to any particular person, but instead originates from a sociocultural system, generating the conditions for its possibility, concealing it, and giving it meaning. For Martínez and Cabezas (2022), this is present through any normative practice or discourse, whether intentional or not, that results in harm to subordinate groups.

In this way, systemic violence is normalized in the media, both traditional and digital, since it empowers individuals to feel free to make violent comments toward vulnerable groups. Pérez (2020) explains that digital media are online platforms that convey information that influences the perception and construction of identity of all those female athletes, and, like traditional media, they not only inform but also shape the social image of female athletes to their own convenience and can perpetuate gender stereotypes, such that digital media run the risk of amplifying already-existing stereotypes.

According to Donoso et al. (2022), media coverage is a space that reflects the invisibility to which female athletes have been subjected, which materializes in the media impact their achievements have compared to those of men. This can influence public perception and the way in which social, cultural, and political problems come to be understood and addressed. The presentation of information can reinforce or challenge stereotypes and inequalities, especially regarding issues related to gender, race, and social class.

Disinformation, meanwhile, understood as the distortion of information that spreads fake news that deceives the end recipient (Cortés, 2015), includes false informational content, created and disseminated intentionally (Wardle, 2018, cited in González, 2019), which can be called an information disorder, referring to that news which is unverified and is false information shared to cause harm, as is misinformation (Gonzales, 2019); for Narváez and Pérez (2022), this type of information is characterized by being designed to provide misleading information through any medium for the purpose of deceiving. In this sense, disinformation can be used to influence the end recipient through the intentional manipulation of data, facts, and events. The popularization of social media has given rise to two major communication phenomena: disinformation and hate speech; although these are not new phenomena, they represent a great danger in today's society (Blanco, 2023).

Hate speech, according to Díaz (2018, cited in Blanco, 2023), is defined as any type of offensive expression directed at a person or group of people based on one of their characteristics, such as their membership in an ethnicity or religion, nationality, gender, or sexual orientation. It is also, moreover, violent communication that can incite both physical and moral violence (Miro, 2016); nonetheless, depending on the ethical and legal implications, the term hate alludes to the emotional and leaves it open to subjectivity (Cabo and García, 2016).

Methodology

This research seeks to answer how traditional gender stereotypes contribute to the generation of gender-based violence on social media, using as a case study the controversy surrounding boxer Imane Khelif in the context of the Paris 2024 Olympic Games. A qualitative methodology was used, through digital ethnography, which, according to Pink et al. (2016), is based on the principles of traditional ethnography using the tools characteristic of modernity, such as social media and digital media, to study and understand these sites as cultural spaces of human interaction, which respond to their own codes and social practices, like any other leisure setting in which different individuals converge and interact with one another.

This research has a basic descriptive nature, since, according to Gay (1996, cited in Nieto, 2018), it involves collecting data to answer questions concerning the subjects of study, and reports how things are.

The corpus of this work was assembled from the content published and shared through the X platform regarding the encounter between Algerian boxer Imane Khelif and Italian boxer Angela Carini at the Paris 2024 Olympic Games, focusing mainly on content reproducing discriminatory and violent discourse toward Khelif, which was aimed at discrediting the Algerian boxer's participation over an alleged biological advantage that the media claimed was due to the African country's competitor being a transgender woman — a situation that, it was claimed, would bar her from taking part in sporting competitions in the women's category — until this rumor was later debunked. Posts from August 1, 2024, the date of the encounter between the boxers, through August 9 of that same year were taken into account, this being the date of their last encounter in the final of the women's boxing 66 kg category, since it was following this first encounter that the first pieces covering this media phenomenon began to appear.

The analysis was based on all publications by media outlets and their respective shares, taking into account those that met the criterion of having been produced by Spanish-speaking media outlets and/or users within the previously established period. To narrow down the information, X's search filters were used, applying the search criteria "Imane Khelif hombre" ["Imane Khelif man"], "Imane Khelif transgénero" ["Imane Khelif transgender"], "Imane Khelif cambio de género" ["Imane Khelif gender change"], and "Imane Khelif hermafrodita" ["Imane Khelif hermaphrodite"] to extract all publications and comments meeting the established search criteria.

With the help of the Export Comments software, 60 search results were selected, of which only 28 were kept after applying a second, manually performed filter, reviewing each of the results, from which all those that did not meet the research criteria or did not contribute to the analysis were removed.

In order to achieve the research objective, and as a result of the literature review, the following categories of analysis were established: a) systemic violence, b) hate speech, c) gender stereotypes, and d) disinformation, to analyze the comments made by users of the social network X regarding the controversy surrounding boxer Imane Khelif in the context of the Paris 2024 Olympic Games.

Based on the data obtained, the presence of the previously established categories and subcategories of analysis was observed, since, in accordance with the observables, the comments show the presence of gender-based violence, expressed through violent communication, perpetuating gender stereotypes as a result of the disinformation surrounding the coverage carried out by tabloid media outlets that engaged in spreading rumors about Imane Khelif, with the aim of discrediting the athlete's career.

The Imane Khelif media phenomenon: discrimination and hate speech as a result of the spread of disinformation through social media

It was determined that the predominant stance found in the results collected is a discriminatory stance, with misogynistic, racial, and sexist overtones, which forms part of hate speech that is, at the same time, closely related to traditional gender stereotypes, since, as Flores et al. (2022) note, minimizing, excluding, sexualizing, and objectifying female athletes are acts of violence, as is imposing beauty canons and criticizing those who do not meet them.

Thus, given that Imane Khelif is a racialized woman who does not conform to European beauty standards, nor to Western stereotypes of femininity, coupled with the fact that she is a high-performance athlete in a sport associated with masculinity — and, according to Roca and Navarro (2022), the press and media are the main drivers of rejection toward women who compete in this type of discipline — topped off by the fact that she competed against a white, European, hegemonic woman who claimed to have been the victim of an injustice on the part of the Olympic committee that accepted the Algerian boxer's participation.

These gender stereotypes made Imane Khelif the perfect target for generating controversy and spreading rumors about her, propagating a large amount of intentional disinformation with the aim of discrediting the athlete's career; it was therefore determined that disinformation through the use of misleading content (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2018, cited in Sánchez and Magallón, 2023) is present among X users, when claims are made that Imane Khelif is a transgender woman or that she is a man, making statements such as "Transgender Muslim…" or "Imane Khelif is a man who feels like a woman…," since at no point during the period of time considered for this research was any official document issued confirming that the Algerian boxer was either a transgender woman or a biological man.

Another manifestation of hate speech present in the comments made by the X users analyzed is that these persist even after International Olympic Committee spokesperson Mark Adams came forward to clarify the situation, noting that the Algerian boxer had been born, registered, and lives as a woman, in addition to clarifying that it was not a transgender issue and that there is scientific consensus on this (Knight & Vandoorne, 2024). This makes it possible to observe that the hate speech present in comments posted after this statement was issued is intended to cause harm and offend the person (Díaz, 2018, cited in Blanco, 2023) — in this case, Imane Khelif.

Members of the X platform's online community who are sports fans were quick to fall for the clickbait of sensationalist tabloid sports headlines, generating a hasty and unfounded judgment about the boxer, unloading all the internalized hatred they hold toward marginalized and minority communities. While it is true that information travels quickly today, disinformation spreads even faster, so it is therefore difficult to determine which user made comments out of being misinformed and which user made comments intentionally, both to generate disinformation and to create hate speech.

The androcentric attitudes and value judgments that still exist in the world of sport and among its fans are not isolated cases; as Rodríguez et al. (2022) note, society makes a notable division when it comes to sports, since it is thought about in gendered terms; added to this, sport continues to be dominated by men, both in its practice and in its coverage and consumption (Salido and Muñoz, 2021), creating an unfavorable landscape for female athletes, since, as Martínez (2012, cited in Martínez and Cabezas, 2022) proposes, systemic violence cannot be attributed to just a few — the violence displayed in spectators' comments is not solely the responsibility of their own cognitive biases, nor solely that of media outlets that monetize disinformation and morbid curiosity; there is an entire dominant structure in the world of sport that, more than two centuries after the Olympic Games were first held, continues to exercise various forms of discrimination and violence toward disadvantaged communities.

The Imane Khelif media phenomenon: discrimination and hate speech as a result of the spread of disinformation through social media

Hate speech and the reproduction of disinformation within a globalized society allow individuals who share discriminatory ideologies to believe that, simply because their beliefs are the most popular, they are correct, minimizing the negative repercussions this has for those who do not share them; the controversy Imane Khelif was involved in against her will is solid proof of this. It should not be forgotten that women as a group are the only ones whose work is dismissed solely because of their appearance, especially racialized women — as on previous occasions with Alexa Moreno, who was criticized and subjected to violence after her first appearance at the Rio 2016 Olympics for being too "fat" and "ugly" to be a gymnast. South African sprinter Caster Semenya, in 2009, was subjected to gender testing after winning a race by a significantly larger margin than the rest of the athletes. Indian sprinter Dutee Chand was suspended in 2015 following a diagnosis of hyperandrogenism, and, likewise, Lin Yu Ting was accused of being a transgender woman at the Paris 2024 edition of the Olympic Games due to a high testosterone count, for which she was deemed ineligible to compete and had her bronze medal revoked.

Clearly these cases are not isolated, and the International Olympic Committee still has an enormous gap to close in terms of equity and gender perspective, since the discrimination these institutions commit is evident, because while a racialized woman with a biological condition that makes her even more suited to her sporting discipline is disqualified and sanctioned, a white, cisgender man with a similarly biological condition that makes him much more competitive in his sport is considered a legend, as in the case of Michael Phelps, who has been proven to have a genetic condition that makes his limbs longer than those of the average human and whose body produces half the lactic acid, giving him a competitive advantage over the rest of his fellow athletes.

These behaviors once again reveal the smoke screen the Olympic Games put up regarding gender parity and diversity across their various communication channels, where women and historically marginalized communities continue to be in a position of inequality relative to favored majorities.

In this sense, Cortés (2015) notes that the opportunities and platforms enjoyed by men relative to women are abysmal, so that these discriminatory and segregating behaviors, despite the Olympic committee's multiple attempts to reverse them and create inclusive spaces, mean equity remains a distant reality, especially for racialized women in traditionally masculinized disciplines, since this systemic violence observed within Olympic sports is not exclusive to them, but rather there is an entire dominant structure that promotes this violence and that has repercussions across different organizations, making the transition toward parity even more difficult.

For Martínez (2012, cited in Martínez and Cabezas, 2022), no single party can be singled out as responsible; rather, it is an entire system that disadvantages marginalized communities — in this case, female athletes who do not belong to the hegemonic norm, that is, white, cisgender male athletes — and this violence is replicated not only by the Olympic Committee, but also by various sports organizations, media outlets, and event spectators, creating a chain that further undermines the integrity of disadvantaged groups, replicating discourse that dehumanizes whoever becomes the protagonist of a media scandal, since within the media the issue is no longer treated as a person but as a news story, and, according to Pérez (2020), the discourse projected by the media resonates directly with the viewer, shaping their opinion and the value judgments with which they identify the protagonist.

In summary, Cabo and García (2016) note that social media has become a space for spreading radicalized and violent opinions, making it possible to recognize that the case analyzed here is not isolated, nor does it represent the reality of a minority, because even though today's society presents a more inclusive and politically correct approach, online society shows polarization in terms of its ideological stances, making it essential to begin making these new realities visible, especially bearing in mind current and future generations who, today, make virtuality part of their reality, and this discourse that eventually ends up on the web will reach them, creating an even greater divide among members of society.

Conclusions

The results of the research offer a view of how online communities are an immediate representation of the values and ideologies on which today's society is built; hatred, discrimination, and the oppression of minorities are not behaviors exclusive to a certain group of individuals, but rather a projection of a worldview belonging to the dominant culture, a culture that has for years been androcentric and misogynistic.

The interactions users have within social media are as real as those that exist outside of it — not only real but also harsher; many people think that, because they are commenting from behind a screen, the impact of their actions is insignificant; the problem lies in the number of people who think the same way, which is why it is time to begin making visible the problems experienced within online communities and also the way in which the media distribute and deliver information to internet users, since news media continue to be opinion leaders and social touchstones, and spreading disinformation is not only unethical but dangerous; the right to freedom of expression and a free press should not curtail minorities' right to exist and lead a dignified life free of violence; it is important to ask ourselves: would Khelif have been subjected to defamation, attacks against her person and her career as an athlete, had she been a cisgender man in a sporting discipline dominated by men? Would the disinformation reproduced by hundreds of media outlets questioning her gender or the validity of her presence at the Olympics have been different had she, instead of coming from Algeria, been a white, European, hegemonic woman? Was her crime perhaps not fitting the misogynistic, classist, and racist agenda that has characterized the Olympic Games for decades?

Finally, it is necessary to note that, during the preparation of this research, limitations were encountered with respect to the resources available to carry out this research, such as time, since the media phenomenon surrounding the Imane Khelif controversy is a fairly extensive topic, so the time available did not allow the research to be developed in its entirety.

Other financial difficulties also arose, since funding for the research was not only limited but practically nonexistent, meaning that the free versions of social-media analysis software offered a limited amount of content to analyze, as well as restrictions on certain functions; because of the lack of funding to purchase licenses for this software, a more extensive analysis of the information was not feasible.

Even though none of these research topics is exactly new, since these are issues that have been present in societies for centuries, media phenomena and the reproduction of this discriminatory discourse within online communities remain a little-explored research topic, so it is suggested that research on discrimination and gender-based violence as a media phenomenon be continued.
