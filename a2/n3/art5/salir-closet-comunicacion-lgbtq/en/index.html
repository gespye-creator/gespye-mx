"Coming out of the closet": communication and conflict among members of the LGBTQ+ community with religious family backgrounds

“Salir del clóset” comunicación y conflicto de integrantes de la comunidad LGBTQ+ con origen familiar religioso

Abstract.

This article explores how religion affects family communication of LGBTQ + people who are students at the University. Using a qualitative approach and a descriptive exploratory design, three focus groups are conducted to analyze the narrative of members of this community about family communication processes in the face of religious prejudices in order to understand the interpersonal dynamics and religious beliefs that influence each individual's interactions with their family members. This work shows that religion has a lot of power in how sexual diversity is viewed and accepted and how individual beliefs can benefit or harm their well-being. With the narrative of the participants, it was possible to demonstrate that addressing issues of gender identity or sexual orientation continue to be topics that parents and some other family members avoid.

Keywords:

Religion, family, communication, LGBTQ + , identity.

Introduction

The following research is aimed at analyzing the communication that takes place within religious families when a member openly declares themselves part of the LGBTQ+ community. The reason for carrying out this work has to do with the premise concerning the influence religion has on the acceptance of sexual orientations and the problems this represents for members of the LGBTQ+ community when deciding to be part of it.

This research seeks to answer how the communication process takes place among members of religious families with at least one LGBTQ+ member, and, at the same time, to recognize how religious beliefs influence the way families approach communication with members of this community. The empirical work to analyze how communication takes place within religious families with LGBTQ+ members was delimited to students at the Centro Universitario de Ciencias Económico Administrativas (CUCEA), since this makes it possible to explore the impact of religious beliefs on the construction of sexual and gender identities and to reveal how religion influences the way religious families of higher-education students address topics related to gender identity, in order to understand how beliefs influence the perception and acceptance of gender diversity within religious households.

Today's generations of young people reveal their sexual identity at a younger age compared to previous generations (Floyd Blakemann, 2006, cited in Joya et al., 2022); for this reason, it is important that this topic be analyzed among young people and that the response within conservative families be recognized. Acceptance of sexual orientation remains a taboo subject within religious settings, a complex issue influenced by various social variables.

For many years, religion has promoted the idea that romantic relationships should be established exclusively between people of opposite sexes. This perspective can generate confusion and difficulties in the lives of LGBTQ+ people. In communities where religion plays a prominent role, family and social support for those who identify as part of this community can be practically nonexistent. This environment can give rise to significant emotional problems, with fear of rejection being one of the main challenges, manifesting in individuals who break with the sexual status quo in various ways, such as anxiety and depression, and even death, which can result from a lack of communication on the part of non-heterosexual people toward those closest to them.

Thus, analyzing the communication process between families and their LGBTQ+ members is fundamental for understanding the influence of family relationships on the emotional well-being of people who do not identify in binary terms; identifying the communication barriers that exist, as in this case is considered to be religion, and how these beliefs can positively or negatively affect communication among family members, given that acceptance or rejection of homosexual children is shaped by how each family interprets and lives its faith.

The intersection between religion and the LGBTQ+ community has been a topic of growing interest in social research. This research examines the effect of religion on family communication regarding gender and sexual identity when a member reveals that they are openly homosexual, as well as its impact on the mental health of the individuals who are part of that family. According to the Instituto Jalisciense de la Salud Mental (SALME, 2023), actions can be implemented within the family setting with the intention of supporting LGBTQ+ people, but these actions can turn out to be extremely harmful to the physical, mental, and emotional health of gay, lesbian, or transgender individuals.

According to Barajas (2021), evangelical groups' opposition to gender ideology has caused one of the greatest crises of identity and values in Mexico and Latin America so far in the 21st century, which is why this rejection on the part of religion and religious groups has a major impact on the acceptance and recognition of people belonging to the LGBTQ+ community by making sexual diversity invisible and rejecting it; for Coyle and Rafalin (2000, cited in Setton, 2015), when trying to reconcile a religious identity and a free sexual identity, young people may suffer internal conflicts, and this affects how they perceive themselves across the different settings of their lives.

As Pereira (2023) notes, the teachings of the Catholic Church have historically influenced the acceptance of sexual diversity in Mexico. Its doctrines, by promoting a traditional view of marriage and sexuality, have made it more difficult to accept diverse sexual orientations, and given that Catholicism is the majority religion in the country (INEGI, 2020), its beliefs have permeated Mexican society, creating a context in which many LGBTQ+ people face discrimination and rejection. In this sense, Jalisco ranks as the fourth state with the largest population identifying as LGBTQ+ in the country, representing around 4.7% of its total population, while at the same time being the second state in the country with the highest concentration of Catholics (INEGI, 2021), hence the relevance of recognizing what is happening in the communication of this segment of the population.

Regarding attitudes toward the LGBTQ+ community, a study conducted by Hatibovic et al. (2017) found that religious people showed a higher rate of homophobia, suggesting that religion influences people toward homophobic and exclusionary beliefs. Nonetheless, it is family members who make possible a better development for young people within this community; Orcasita et al. (2023) note that young people who maintain a good relationship with their families, based on affection and attachment, experience a feeling of acceptance and warmth, and therefore family is important in the acceptance of people's sexuality, since it is the first circle they belong to and where they develop, shaping their personality and identity. Bilbao and León (2024) even note that, at the moment they open up about their identity and sexuality to their families, families' perception of homosexuality changes, and greater family trust and closeness could be observed.

For Rueda et al. (2024), effective communication is essential for all families, but is of greater relevance for those with children who belong to the LGBTQ+ community. By fostering open and respectful dialogue, parents can help their children face the challenges of discrimination and build a strong, healthy community. In these families, children do not usually have an external support network, and family communication becomes a fundamental pillar for providing the emotional support and information needed to combat social stigma.

In this way, it is within the family that a person is trained and adopts a role based on the patterns and behaviors they learn from those around them, that is, there is an influence of family and lived experiences over the years on behavior, thought, and attitudes (Horna et al., 2020), and such patterns are reproduced through intra-family communication.

Nonetheless, religious beliefs are predominant in many Latin American countries, and historically this has been a source of rejection toward homosexual people. The traditional teachings promoted by the church generate a negative view of homosexuality, leading many young people to feel fear of rejection and exclusion by their families, and this rejection can have significant psychological consequences for homosexual adolescents, who often face emotional problems stemming from the fear of not being accepted by their loved ones (Pereira, 2023).

Certainly, the shift in Pope Francis's discourse has opened a new path toward inclusion. His message emphasizes that everyone is a child of God and deserves love and respect, representing progress toward greater openness within the Catholic Church (Pope Francis, 2020, cited in Pereira, 2023). This change could positively influence how families approach the topic of homosexuality, promoting a more understanding and less condemning environment.

Theoretical Framework

The following lines establish the concepts used to theoretically delimit this research. By LGBTQ+ community is understood that which is made up of sexual minorities, different sexual and gender identities, outside the heteronormative system (Restrepo et al., 2020). According to Orcasita et al. (2023), this minority community is surrounded by stigma across the various contexts in which it operates, such as work, school, family, religion, among others. These minority groups can be in an active or passive position, and can direct their disagreement toward inclusion, in support of a norm, or in opposition to it.

Thus, social change is a key objective of influence and manifests both individually and collectively. When influence is oriented toward change, conflict becomes evident and inevitable, since a sense of uncertainty arises in the majority when confronting difference; this conflict is essential to the exercise of influence, as it is the necessary means for generating changes that bring about uncertainty and ambiguity, characteristics intrinsic to conflict.

According to Moscovici (1996), the ideas of a small group can affect a majority, causing acceptance or rejection depending on how religious beliefs are interpreted; Moscovici proposes that minorities are groups or individuals who are seen as different from others and can help the majority discover new, original, and even forbidden ideas, in which communication is crucial for building healthy relationships; different families may choose different forms of communication that affect how sensitive topics, such as sexual orientation, are addressed. Openness and acceptance in family communication are fundamental to the well-being of LGBTQ+ youth, so family communication is conceived as the exchange of viewpoints and opinions among family members and how they express their ideas considering empathy, clarity, respect, and affection, which are even part of how it is expressed (Kerr and Stattin, 2000, cited in Ramos and Olivares, 2019).

In some families, a bias can appear within communication, or, as Montagud (2021) defines it, selective communication, in which people tend to avoid sharing information that challenges their preexisting beliefs, which can generate biased communication within families. These biases within communication can lead to family problems such as disagreements and confrontations among family members that can negatively affect the family environment and the well-being of all its members. If community members feel accepted within their families, acceptance or a sense of belonging within the family context can occur; this process involves both parents' recognition of their children's needs and the possibility for children to contribute to and actively participate in family life (Woodhead and Brooker, 2008).

Additionally, there are religious beliefs, which, according to Durkheim (1912, cited in Pastor, 2016), are what express sacred things and the relationship between them. What gives religion its value is the interpretation of religious concepts, that is, the meaning assigned to an action through personal judgment; in this case, the value personally given to religious actions within families. With this, certain heteronormative rules are generated, which, according to Cruz (2020), are defined as a way of seeing the world based on the existence of only two genders, and even though religious beliefs are presumed to be personally adopted by each person (Diez, 2017), family religious dogmas lead to the imposition and influence of religious beliefs.

From a theological standpoint, family love cannot be separated from the capacity for procreation, since the sexual aspect is not what is negative, but rather how people perceive it, in which homosexuality is not sinful but the acts carried out are. Consequently, homosexuality becomes a delicate subject requiring careful attention, since it challenges established conventions and demands open minds and hearts (Pacheco, 2012, cited in Guaman et al., 2019).

These rules affect the construction of a gender identity, which, as the Instituto Nacional de las Mujeres (INMUJERES, 2024) describes it, refers to each person's individual experience, which may or may not correspond with biological sex, in addition to the feelings involved across their whole environment, which, in the presence of religious dogmas, presupposes a family conflict, given the imposition and non-acceptance of sexual preferences and identities.

Methodology

As mentioned earlier, the research is based on the qualitative paradigm, with the purpose of investigating the experiences and viewpoints of individuals belonging to the LGBTQ community with respect to family communication in a religious setting. An exploratory and descriptive design is used, which, according to Ramos (2020), this type of research makes it possible to analyze the narrative of a human group's perception of a phenomenon that, in this case, had not been previously researched, and which, in line with the objective of this work, makes it possible to understand the interpersonal dynamics and religious beliefs that influence each individual's interactions with their family members.

The technique used to collect the data is focus groups, since they make it possible to gather a variety of ideas and emotions group members have about specific topics (Krueger and Casey, 2000, cited in Rodas and Pacheco, 2020), in addition to making it possible to foster conversation among participants while at the same time examining and observing the different narratives that emerge within the group. To ensure the relevance and experience of those who took part in the focus groups, the following requirements were established:

  • Being an active student at the Centro Universitario de Ciencias Económico Administrativas (CUCEA).

  • Being part of the LGBTQ+ community: it is essential that people be recognized as members of that community. This includes individuals recognized as lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, or queer.

  • Being between 18 and 25 years old; this age range was selected since, being adults, they have the legal power to give their consent to participate in this study.

  • Participants come from families with religious beliefs, since the research focuses on examining the impact of religion on communication and views on sexual diversity within the family unit.

For data collection, a structured interview is used, which, according to Rodas and Pacheco (2020), makes it possible to obtain qualitative data, so it is necessary to start from clear, standardized questions across all groups.

3 focus groups were conducted, with 4 participants each; a saturation point was reached in the third focus group, upon noting that participants gave very similar answers and expressed the same ideas. Given scheduling difficulties, these were conducted virtually to ensure participants' involvement. A private and comfortable setting was established to foster openness and honesty among members. The session was guided by a member of the research team, who, through open-ended questions related to the following topics, fostered participation:

  • Communication within the family after an LGBTQ+ identity is revealed.

  • Influence of religious beliefs on attitudes toward homosexuality and gender diversity.

  • Views on family support and its impact on mental and emotional well-being.

The sessions were recorded (with prior authorization) to ensure the preservation of all relevant information, and notes were taken during the conversation to have a record. The data obtained were analyzed through transcriptions of the recordings, and recurring patterns were identified within participants' narratives with the aim of extracting key themes related to changes in family communication following an LGBTQ+ disclosure, the impact of religious beliefs on family relationships, and the emotional consequences of family support or rejection.

Results and Discussion

To preserve participants' anonymity, the letter P will be used to refer to the participant number and G for the number of the group to which they belong.

Regarding the sexual preferences of those who took part in the discussion groups, some participants indicated that they do not communicate their preferences to the rest of their family for fear of negative reactions; they avoid revealing this part of their life to their families out of fear of negative reactions, such as rejection, discrimination, or loss of support. This silence, which can be either active (that is, avoiding the topic entirely) or passive (not providing complete information), emerges as a strategy to preserve family harmony and avoid conflict.

Well, for my part it's a bit harder since my parents don't know, I mean, it's like the fact that I've given signals, not that, oh she's pretty or I don't know what to call it, but like some interaction with a friend, it does look kind of suspicious and it seems like they suspect something, but they still haven't gone out of their way to ask or for me to tell them anything (P4 G2).

As Bilbao and León (2024) note, there are different attitudes among relatives of LGBTQ+ community members that can generate a feeling of rejection; for this same reason, as the evidence shows, people belonging to the community can end up repressing their identity and avoiding sharing certain information because of it.

When discussing family conflicts, participants noted that these were more common at the beginning of their "coming out" process. However, they emphasize the importance of time and open communication in allowing their families to adapt and accept their identities.

Once my mom told me, she literally said it to my face, she said I was sick, no, I'm not sick, so now I laugh about it, but at the time I didn't laugh, it really hurt me that she said I was sick (P2 G1).

I think the strongest comment I've heard is my mom saying that the only thing she regrets is that it doesn't go away until you die. So, well, at the time I heard it and it was a topic of debate that ended badly, precisely because of the clash of ideas that she didn't come to terms with (P4 G3).

I think my parents, even so, although it was hard for them at times, or they weren't really rude, but what actually made me more uncomfortable were the ideas they had about people in the community, like my mom. It's been really hard for her to get out of her head that not all gay men are rapists or pedophiles, or that trans people are prostitutes, I don't know, I think it's more that those negative stereotypes that they sometimes share with me (P3 G1).

When family conversations about gender-identity topics come up, participants' families tend to show some rejection, and ideas instilled by past generations that were socially accepted tend to predominate.

When you start a conversation it can seem totally normal, but when the topic of morality comes into play, or what they perceive as correct, that's not necessarily fine. And they take it in the most conservative way possible (P4 G3).

Well, the ideas each of us has sometimes clash, and today I try to understand my parents' ideas, but they don't try to understand ours (P4 G1)

When I'm with my partner out on the street, when I'm here at home and there are conflicts with family visiting or things like that, well, that's happened a lot of times, and it bothers me that my family doesn't understand it, and no matter how much I communicate it, they just can't put themselves in my shoes, and they're like, oh, just ignore it, or it's not a big deal, or it wasn't said that badly, and it's kind of like, well, you don't live it, right? You don't understand (P2 G3).

As Orcasita (2022) notes, rejection and refusal from one's surroundings can affect LGBTQ+ community members in different areas, such as psychological well-being, acceptance, self-esteem, among others; for this same reason, it is important to have a safe environment that provides trust and security when trying to hold conversations about identity.

The degree of acceptance parents show toward their children's sexual orientation is affected by their lack of knowledge about the LGBTQ+ community, which reinforces what Bilbao and León (2024) state, namely that the lack of information and knowledge family members have about the LGBTQ+ community and sexual identity makes it harder to achieve full acceptance, leading young people to be discriminated against and excluded.

Participants' family members tend to be influenced by religious beliefs when addressing topics of gender identity, which affects participants' well-being and how they accept themselves.

At first they told us that if you have this it's wrong, and it wasn't even that they were going to get upset, it was that God was going to get upset, so from there this whole upbringing started and it really affected the perspective they had when I came out, they thought it was wrong because religion said it was wrong and they were even thinking about taking me to a priest so he could talk to me (P2 G1).

Well, I think the influence is huge, or well, but with some of them, because, well, when I brought up the topic of my sexuality it was like, no, these are sins, you have a demon inside you, right? And I was like, wow!. So they took it to a really, really extreme place. But now I think that by setting limits, that kind of thing doesn't affect me as much anymore (P3 G3).

Religion mostly influences things like this being a disease, that it's unnatural, or the typical, you're going to go to hell, you're not doing what God wants or what God needs for your life (P4 G2).

According to Rojas et al. (2023), religion can impact people's mindset, generating discrimination toward the LGBTQ+ community and promoting homophobic attitudes. This study confirmed that there is a connection between religion and the thoughts and prejudices people hold about the LGBTQ+ community, since there is a belief that being part of the community is sinful. Some family members refuse to accept participants' identity out of fear of what others will say, and because of the impact this difference has on the standards already established by society.

The results show that young people face considerable pressure when trying to reconcile their sexual identity with the expectations imposed by conservative families and predominant religious norms, which are reflected in internal conflicts and self-image. One of the main conclusions of this study is that the young focus-group participants experienced internal conflicts stemming from the need to reconcile a free gender identity with an imposed religious identity. This finding aligns with what Coyle and Rafalin (2000, cited in Setton, 2015) propose, namely that, when trying to reconcile these two identities, young people can suffer a deep dissonance that affects their self-perception and their relationship with the various circumstances of their lives.

It is clear from the testimonies that many of the participants had experienced these conflicts, especially when dealing with family members. For example, one participant mentioned how the pressure to maintain an image that fits religious expectations can be a source of emotional distress: "I feel like religion is one part, but so is people's interpretation of it..." (P1 G3). This inherent tension resulting from a strict interpretation of religion reflects the difficulty of finding coherence between religious teachings and one's sexual orientation.

The results also show that, in conservative families, rejection of a young person's sexual identity is a common response, which can create a process of self-exclusion or delay "coming out of the closet." Some participants noted that certain family members (such as grandparents or parents) have negative and stigmatizing attitudes toward their gender identity, for example: "My grandfather is a bit sexist, so he doesn't like it... and that's where my dad picked up those kinds of comments" (P4 G1). This type of comment reflects resistance to change and to embracing new forms of gender identity, which makes young people feel rejected and uncomfortable with their identity.

This rejection can have a direct impact on how young people see themselves, especially when family members make derogatory comments about other LGBTQ people. As one member noted: "It was like it made you inhibit yourself from sharing that kind of thing with your family" (P1 G3). This negative reaction clearly reflects the heterosexist norms embedded in family structures that create spaces of exclusion for those who deviate from traditional expectations of gender and sexual orientation.

As the testimony emphasizes, religion plays a crucial role in young people's gender-identity conflicts. Although some participants argued that religious teachings such as the commandment to "love all people equally" (P3 G1) did not necessarily condemn gender diversity, family members' subjective interpretations of faith created an atmosphere of conflict. One participant noted how personal interpretations of religious texts can reinforce stereotypes and prejudice: "People give it their own interpretation, that's what generates stereotypes for us..." (P1 G3). This individualistic interpretation of religion perpetuates negative and exclusionary views of non-normative sexuality and gender identity.

As Diez (2023) notes, families perpetuate gender ideologies by associating everything related to the LGBTQ+ community with the feminine and with perversion, generating a negative perception of people who may identify with this community and segregating those who do not fit the imposed ideology.

The influence of religion on young people's parents was also mentioned as an obstacle to acceptance. One participant explained how his deeply religious parents expected him to adopt a traditional attitude in line with religious expectations: "My parents and my siblings are very religious... they wanted me to become, like, a little priest..." (P1 G1). This pressure to fulfill a traditional religious or gender-based mission can lead not only to family conflicts, but also to a sense of fragmentation in family relationships, as mentioned in the testimonies, in relationships with parents affected by differences in religious beliefs.

The data analysis also revealed that families' negative reactions to non-normative identities are deeply rooted in broader social and cultural expectations. In this study, young people often expressed concern about what others would say and fear about the impact of gender differences on family reputation. The pressure to conform to heterosexual norms and conservative social attitudes perpetuates a culture that excludes LGBTQ+ community members, making it harder for them to integrate into families and communities. As Vaggione (2008) notes, heteronormativity continues to be something that governs us unconsciously, since it is something rooted in the legal and juridical principles by which the country is governed.

Conclusions

This work shows that religion has a lot of power over how sexual diversity is viewed and accepted. The teachings each participant holds can either benefit or harm their well-being. Thanks to the participation reflected in these testimonies, it was possible to demonstrate that addressing issues of gender identity or sexual orientation continues to be a topic that parents and some family members avoid because of the conservative beliefs they hold, which leads to a notable lack of communication in which community members end up avoiding disclosing their identity out of fear of rejection.

These findings align with various studies already conducted, which show that homophobia is a deeply rooted issue in society and is something that can be instilled both through the education provided by religion and through an inheritance of thinking passed down across several generations.

At the same time, this research highlights the importance of good communication within families, since, by managing to maintain open and constant communication with their families, participants showed greater well-being.

On the other hand, thanks to the results obtained, it was observed that, in most cases, mother figures accepted their children's homosexuality better than father figures. Based on what each participant shared, it was possible to conclude that this is because mothers typically build a stronger emotional bond with their children, which facilitates communication and therefore leads them to better understand their children, while fathers are more influenced by old beliefs and ideas about masculinity.

Another aspect we confirmed is that communication among LGBTQ+ community members within religious families is a complex and highly tense issue, as could be confirmed by the different reactions of the members interviewed. The results showed that participants often avoid sharing their sexual orientation or gender identity with family members out of fear of rejection and of what others will say, after indirectly receiving homophobic comments.

It is worth highlighting that, according to this study, given the negative reactions of the aforementioned family members and the comments that occurred frequently among participants, they preferred to use passive strategies to reveal their identity through small hints, thereby trying to maintain harmony within the family unit and avoid as much conflict as possible.

Additionally, it was observed that some participants went through an adaptation process within their families, shaped by open communication and ongoing dialogue; this process shows the importance of effective, empathetic communication that helps reduce conflict and create a supportive environment.

Finally, in looking back on this research it is important to note that some limitations arose, such as participants' openness to discussing the topic; some showed a certain discomfort speaking about their identity and preferences within their family context, which could be due to some trauma or to the stigmatization surrounding it, so for future research it would be advisable to explore strategies for fostering a safer, more confidential environment that allows participants to express their experiences openly and honestly. This could include the use of relaxation techniques, guaranteed anonymity, and the creation of smaller, more homogeneous focus groups. Additionally, it would be very helpful to collaborate with LGBTQ+ community organizations to find participants and validate the research instruments.
