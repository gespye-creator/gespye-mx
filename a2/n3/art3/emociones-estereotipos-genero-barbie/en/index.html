Emotions surrounding female gender stereotypes represented in the Barbie movie

Abstract.

The work addresses gender stereotypes centered on the influence of the image of Barbie. The influence of the film on Mexican university women is analyzed, highlighting how it reflects and affects gender perceptions in society through emotions. A Jungian word association test methodology and Plutchik's wheel of emotions are used. The main findings identify that the emotions that arouse the stereotypes of autonomous and professional women are anticipation and surprise; the stereotypes about despotic and feminist women arouse trust and disgust; while the stereotype of submissive-empowered women is associated with anger and fear.

Keywords:

Gender stereotypes, emotions, Barbie the movie.

Introduction

To begin with, it is necessary to acknowledge that, over time, some gender stereotypes have been successfully modified; however, others remain deeply rooted in society, given that they are culturally pre-configured behaviors. In this sense, and given the controversy generated in recent years by the movie Barbie (Gerwig, 2023), which shows both sides of the coin — the traditional stereotype centered on feminine beauty standards, but also a narrative oriented toward the idea that women can be whatever they want to be — this research paper focuses on analyzing the emotional effect that the representation of female gender stereotypes in the movie Barbie (Gerwig, 2023) has on Mexican university women. It seeks to understand how these representations impact students' gender perception, self-esteem, and emotional well-being.

This film was chosen as part of the object of study not only for its controversial content, but also for the reach its worldwide release has had, given that cinema is one of the art forms considered to have the greatest cultural, social, and economic influence, and is a medium that reflects and shapes society (UNESCO, 2021), playing a role as a transmitter and replicator of stereotypes.

This research paper uses a qualitative methodology, since the aim is to identify the emotions that arise from the stereotypes presented in the movie Barbie (Gerwig, 2023). The fieldwork for the research is carried out through focus groups, using open- and closed-ended questions to identify characteristics of scenes from the film and the stereotypes present in it.

In this sense, it is recognized that gender stereotypes are a categorization created by society that establishes a series of expectations differentiated by sex, regarding the behavior that must be fulfilled as part of society (Galán, 2021), such that they translate into generalized opinions and prejudices assigned to a person with specific attributes that differentiate between the masculine and the feminine.

This phenomenon occurs from a very early age through play, where toys play a very important role in shaping stereotypes. The impact toys have on boys and girls influences who they want to be and who they want to resemble, and therefore leads them to act like them. In the case of the Barbie doll, girls grow up with this image of an ultra-feminine woman with a perfect body and skin, thus influencing, as a determining factor, the socialization process of gender roles with an unrealistic body image and a perfect lifestyle (Uncu, 2019), which in some way forces them to conform to unrealistic beauty, social, and economic standards today, which also affects the aspirations and capabilities women want to develop, limiting gender identities and conditioning sexual orientations, so that the stereotype developed around the Barbie doll has contributed to social and cultural pressure on Mexican girls and women (Aksu, 2005).

Barbie was created as a toy associated with fashion; the doll presents the image of a woman with a perfect body, always well-groomed and attractive, so that, since Barbie's first creation in 1959, it has fostered a childhood stereotype in which women must always be beautiful to satisfy men's taste or desires; however, faced with social demands regarding female objectification and women's rights and equality in recent years, the brand has introduced new products that include racial diversity and professional characterization of the doll, modifying its narrative and advertising around it (Mattel, 2024); the current message the Barbie brand seeks to convey is "girls can do anything," which alludes to the idea that women are capable of overcoming any obstacle or making any decision in life.

Added to the above, in 2023 the movie Barbie premiered, directed by Greta Gerwig, which for the first time is portrayed by actors rather than using animated characters, and which achieved unprecedented success by grossing more than a billion dollars in its first three weeks of release (Muy Interesante, 2023), going on to reach streaming platforms later that same year, breaking playback records as well and being nominated for and winning various film and music awards.

In the movie, Barbie, the main character, is represented under the traditional stereotype associated with feminine perfection, which assumes that appearance and feminine submission are the most important value a woman can have, and emphasizes the character's social heteronormativity. The plot is set in a place governed by female characters, where everything is the object of perfection and the male characters appear as secondary.

As the plot progresses, Barbie's existential questioning opens the door to rethinking some feminine stereotypes; she does not recognize herself or the place she is in, questions her own identity, her self-esteem is affected, and her interaction with the rest of the characters is altered, so she embarks on a journey to the real world to understand what is happening to her. On her journey through the real world, Barbie faces harassment, violence, and repression while trying to be returned to normal, without getting answers, while Ken, the male character, learns about patriarchy and returns to his world to impose masculine hegemony and feminine submission.

Faced with patriarchal alienation within the world of Barbie, the main character turns to a woman from the real world, who, through a speech about the expectations society as a whole has of women, helps her regain confidence in herself and decide to break with the alienation of the patriarchal system imposed on her world; ultimately, she becomes aware of the failings of her former world, proposing a space of greater equality for the male characters, and for those who do not embody the standard of perfection. Barbie decides to leave her fictional world and become a woman in the contemporary world.

State of the Art and Theoretical Framework

The following presents the literature review that makes it possible to recognize both the evolution of this research topic and the concepts that serve to delimit the theoretical framework used to analyze the phenomenon described above.

Regarding gender stereotypes, the economic, social, cultural, and historical context plays an important role in their construction, which causes them to be produced and reproduced in a subjective way, making them generators of prejudice and discrimination, since they correspond to a belief or idea that differentiates what men and women should be and do (Tijoux, 2022), constituting an obstacle to achieving true gender equality, since they also promote expectations about the people who make up particular social groups (Council of Europe, 2015).

For Fernández (2016) and Tijoux (2022), stereotypes are created from traditions, ideologies, and religions that arise from social interactions, and are therefore created within a collective. Meanwhile, for Eagly and Kite (1987) and Eagly and Wood (2012), gender stereotypes are defined as a dynamic influenced and differentiated by the roles men and women play in society, which originate in the physiological and physical differences between them and are reinforced by culture, since they force individuals to represent specific social roles. In the case of stereotypes accepted as part of Mexican culture, women have mainly been characterized as confined to a maternal and domestic role; they are expected to have a strong sense of family and care for their loved ones, and are often perceived as religious (Ojeda and González, 2018). In this way, stereotypes and gender roles are a mechanism of social heteronormativity that fosters inequality and the false belief in women's inferiority when it comes to carrying out public, political activities and social practices, given that they have been designated the role of mother, wife, and homemaker (Morondo, 2023), which has an effect at both the collective and individual levels, limiting women's freedom of choice and opportunities, and justifying social hierarchy.

The media are also an important factor in reproducing gender stereotypes about women, given that their content fosters and influences people's desires, interests, and aspirations, which can modify their personality (Bonavitta and Garay, 2011). For Alfarache (2003), the feminine stereotypes represented in the media stem from an androcentric model and are the result of a prejudice rooted in patriarchy, in which both media and film content create and distribute ideals of beauty for women's bodies, minds, and feelings; failing to meet these stereotypes generates frustration, disappointment, anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem (Serret, 2008), which continues to decline the more such content is consumed (Grabe et al., 2008), where this concern over body image has psychological, emotional, and physical consequences (Tiggemann, 2014).

In this sense, movies, like toys, constitute a learning moment for the construction of the self (Jane et al., 2023), since they contribute to the internalization of norms and expectations of oneself (Signorella and Liben, 1984), and come to universalize beliefs and opinions that become structured as social rules (de la Vega, 2010), so that the stereotypes represented in films, consciously or unconsciously (Plaza and Delgado, 2007), can limit potential and freedom of choice, since they are capable of significantly influencing the attitudes, values, and opinions of their consumers (Morales, 2015), and normalizing what is presented there in order to develop prejudices based on what is not accepted, recognized, and/or reflected in films. For Palominos (2006, cited in Oberlin, 2021), individuals are affected by the messages conveyed in audiovisual content, which shapes their socialization process and confers upon them the behaviors accepted by society.

With respect to gender stereotypes in film, these shape the type of relationship between the sexes, in which the masculine is considered superior to the feminine and sustains its discriminatory gene, which justifies the situations of inequality women have experienced in every society (Amurrio et al., 2012); women are underrepresented in leading roles, whose parts have less complexity in plots and narratives compared to men (Maryann, 2015), and even though they have gained greater visibility, their representation does not keep pace with social demands (Ojeda and Cabezas, 2023).

Today, audiovisual content continues to present limited and differentiated roles, stereotypes, and characters regarding the masculine and the feminine, seemingly unaware that these representations are capable of modifying society's habits and customs (Ferraro, 2022). For Pérez (2020), gender stereotypes shown in film negatively affect both men and women, since they are used to condition, in a certain way, individuals' freedom to step outside what has already been established by society, and stereotypes of women carry less prestige than those of men (Vélez et al., 2012); women continue to be portrayed as tender, weak, submissive, and dependent, while men are associated with reason, authority, independence, and toughness.

For Glick et al. (2000), exposure to sexist content directly influences the attitudes of people, whether men or women. Additionally, it has been shown how female viewers of film and television productions can become susceptible to constructing a fantasy ideal of the female body through still and moving images (Mills, 2002), and how this affects their body satisfaction (Leahey et al., 2011).

For Ferraro (2022), linking each gender to characteristics specific to its own identity encourages its association with the role, so that the main function of stereotype representation is to sustain what she calls "the patriarchy of consent," a social system in which gender norms and roles are established and maintained through women's conformity and consent, rather than through violence, encouraging women to fulfill assigned roles; women who do not are made to feel guilty.

Regarding the stereotypes historically associated with the Barbie doll, as already mentioned, these include perfection and an idealized beauty standard that conforms to values, characteristics, and behaviors traditionally accepted in the West: homemaker, model, or princess, with attitudes of passivity, submission, or dependence on the male character Ken, reinforcing the idea that women have a lesser capacity for autonomy, are superficial, and are more concerned with their physical appearance than with deep reflection and complex problems — so much so that the movie's plot begins when Barbie starts having thoughts about death, with the consequence of getting cellulite and being unable to walk on tiptoe (Marañón, 2023).

For Brody (2023, cited in Bristiel, 2023), this situation depicted in the film calls into question an emotional aspect of women and the ideal of what happiness should be, where everything fits into a perfect and full life and nothing is lacking; however, when Barbie goes to the real world, she becomes aware of the contradictory emotional burden, the naturalized sexuality imposed on her body, and that the perfection of her world — which grants power and autonomy to the Barbies — is a construction shaped by a masculine vision of an ideal world, in which female characters have been designed to be the protagonists but with total dependence on the Kens, and having a different sexual orientation or breaking with the assigned stereotype leads them to exclusion, marginalization, or even banishment. From Tinat's (2024) point of view, this is explained by the fact that cinema is always directed at a masculine subject, regardless of whether the viewer is a woman or has a different sexual orientation, since visual pleasure is created from women and narratives built to turn them into a project for men, as happens in the scene where Barbie apologizes to Ken for making him feel bad and not paying him enough attention, but Ken, despite the chaos he created in a non-social setting, assumes he is in the right and does not apologize.

Nonetheless, the emotional effect of the movie Barbie varies depending on each individual's personal context, but carries the latent risk of negative effects due to the unrealistic gender stereotypes it presents; for example, even though the film's protagonist — Margot Robbie — is recognized for her beauty and ranked among some lists of the most beautiful women in the world, she has a crisis from feeling she does not live up to what is expected of her, which promotes self-demand among women to seek perfection and meet standards in order to feel accepted by society, which, according to Heras (2023), this self-demand contributes to the emergence of depression and anxiety.

Another aspect that has been analyzed with respect to the construction, creation, or conception of stereotypes has to do with women's social status. According to Torres and Izquierdo (2022), gender stereotypes have the power to change women's social conditions, which is why the study and tracking of gender-stereotype representation — both visual and discursive — is essential for determining the possible evolution of women's social situation, making the classification of gender stereotypes necessary in order to assess how they have changed over time (Ibid, 2022); unfortunately, works such as those by Pérez (2020), Marañón (2023), Bristiel (2023), and Tinat (2024) are not encouraging in this regard, since, upon reviewing the narratives and discourses surrounding women in the audiovisual field, negative views and the persistence of traditional archetypes predominate, because women tend to be represented in minor ways, limited to everyday and intimate aspects, so that no significant evolution in gender stereotypes has been identified despite the increase in the production and distribution of audiovisual content.

Even though film constitutes a powerful cultural instrument that significantly influences the attitudes, values, and opinions of its consumers, the critical capacity to identify negative influences and expose them in favor of equality between men and women has not developed; daily life remains steeped in sexist roles and stereotypes that erode this social objective (Morales, 2015). For Molina et al. (2024), the film industry must be aware of the social responsibility and transformative power it holds, since the representation it makes of women can help generate new meanings from individual thoughts and feelings, and create a symbolic world without presenting sexist stereotypes.

From a theoretical standpoint, Goffman (1976) recognizes that media content is responsible for a symbolic representation of gender and how this reflects and perpetuates the social norms and expectations assigned to gender, thereby shaping attitudes, prejudices, beliefs, and social predispositions, whether positive or negative, toward the topics addressed in the content, which translates into the reinforcement of individuals' preexisting attitudes (D'Adamo and García, 2007). In this sense, the theoretical premises proposed by Goffman (1976) are used to explain how the stimuli received through the gender stereotypes represented in the movie Barbie carry over into real life and have emotional effects among young Mexican university women.

Additionally, the proposals of Bussey and Bandura (1999) are drawn upon to explain how gender roles and stereotypes are constructed through the interaction between cognition, the social environment, and the media, such that the unequal representations made of men and women in popular films perpetuate androcentrism, reinforcing the masculine representation as dominant over the feminine (Lauzen and Dozier, 2005), and this influences the emotions of women who are viewers of the movie Barbie.

Finally, to analyze the emotions, the proposal that Hochschild (1990) draws from Goffman's (1981) work is used; he notes that emotions are the result of the everyday feelings of individuals, and are therefore just as important as what is thought or done, and are the result of social influence, though the importance lies not in knowing the social factors that provoke the emotion, but in how individuals act in response to the emotion itself, so that emotions are related to experience, management, norms, structures, and ideologies such as that of gender (Hochschild, 1990).

To characterize the emotions, Robert Plutchik's wheel of emotions is used (Plutchik and Kellerman, 1980), which is made up of eight basic emotions and their opposites: Joy – sadness; Anticipation – surprise; Disgust – trust; Anger – fear, followed by 24 compound emotions in 3 dyads, as shown in figure 1.

Methodology

The research paper has an exploratory scope with qualitative data collection and analysis, since it seeks to answer the research question: What is the emotional effect of the representation of female gender stereotypes in the movie Barbie on Mexican university women? This research design follows from the fact that the empirical scope and dimensions of the problem under study have been little studied, since it is difficult to formulate precise hypotheses or generalize the results given how recent the phenomenon is (Sabino, 2006). As for the qualitative nature of the design, this allows different research questions to be formulated in order to explore and understand phenomena from a contrasting perspective between the researcher and the research participants (Ramírez and Arbesú, 2019).

A triangulation of techniques is used for data collection: first, the participants' perceptions, opinions, and emotions regarding the movie Barbie are discussed in a focus group, after which a word association test proposed by Jung (1910) is applied, in which a stimulus word associated with the film's gender stereotypes is presented and the words associated by the participants in response to each stimulus are recorded.

Three focus groups are conducted via the ZOOM platform, since it allows information to be gathered about participants' perceptions, opinions, and experiences. It also makes it possible to generate novel ideas during the interview and focuses attention on the plurality of responses obtained from a group of people, with the aim of obtaining data through the perception, feelings, attitudes, and opinions of groups of people until reaching information saturation (Cortés, 2008); 6 women participated in each one, since this number allows for effective interaction between participants and moderators (Powell and Single, 1996).

Each session began with a welcome and introduction about the purpose of the study, followed by an explanation of the confidentiality of the responses provided, and concluded with expressions of thanks for attendees' participation. On average, each focus group lasted 1 hour. In addition to being Mexican university women, the participants were between 19 and 24 years old, since the aim was to obtain a deeper perspective on the attitudes, preferences, and needs within the main age group the film is aimed at.

During the focus groups, excerpts from the movie Barbie were shown, previously selected because they represent the gender stereotypes identified in Table 1. Table 2 presents a description of the film excerpt and the stereotype it was associated with.

Regarding Jung's (1910) word association test, the technique consists of presenting scenes from the movie Barbie as a stimulus, so that the focus-group participants, individually, can associate them with the emotions present in Robert Plutchik's wheel of emotions (1980). During the focus group, a link to a Google form is distributed containing a total of 17 questions related to the scenes that present the feminine stereotypes. This word association test was chosen because it makes it possible to explore the emotions that the stereotypes present in the film spontaneously arouse in the participants, without their responses being consciously prefabricated.

To analyze the information obtained, the contributions within the focus groups were transcribed and a narrative analysis was carried out focused on the stereotypes presented, as well as on the emotions associated with each of them. To present the results, word clouds were used, and the emotions were grouped around each stereotype analyzed.

Results

The empirical results obtained are presented simultaneously with a discussion of both other authors' findings and the theoretical premises that delimit the research. They are presented in order according to the female gender stereotypes analyzed, and for each one, the emotions the study participants associated with the scenes presented are described. For the excerpts of participants' contributions retrieved, the letter F is used to indicate the number of the focus group in which it was presented, and the letter P, for the internal identification of the participant who said it.

The first stereotype analyzed is that of the autonomous woman, corresponding to the scene in the film that shows the protagonist greeting women represented in various professions, such as doctors, lawyers, politicians, construction workers, among others, where the participants used related words such as admiration, inspiration, and empowerment (see image 2), implying that, for the women who were part of this research, seeing the autonomous-woman stereotype represented evokes in them an emotion of anticipation and surprise, because even though it is not recognized as an innovative representation — it is not close to the reality women live — it makes them question the dependence in which they have been represented for many years. For Ortiz et al. (2016) and Casique (2004), this type of representation allows women to recognize their capacity for autonomy and motivates others to exercise leadership that contributes to their self-esteem, so this stereotype can be associated primarily with positive emotions.

Image 2. Emotions related to the autonomous-woman stereotype

With respect to the professional-woman stereotype, emotions were analyzed based on the film excerpt showing a sequence of scenes alluding to the phrase "be whatever you want to be," in which Barbie is shown able to choose her profession, owns a car and a house, and shows financial solvency, where once again the participants used related words such as: empowerment, aspiration, and security (see image 3). This suggests that, for the participants, seeing the professional-woman stereotype represented evokes admiration, which can be beneficial for building self-esteem. In this regard, one of the participants noted that: "Barbie's influence on self-esteem can be positive or negative, depending on how her image is interpreted. On one hand, Barbie can inspire empowerment by representing various professional roles and fostering creativity." (F1, P6)

Image 3. Emotions related to the professional-woman stereotype

For Godoy and Mladinic (2009), the fact that the transformation in women's professional lives is recognized modifies their role in the workplace and family, in which they need not depend financially on a man, but above all it allows them to recognize their capacity to develop autonomy and economic independence, improving their living conditions.

The third stereotype analyzed was that of empowered submission. For this analysis, the scene from the film was used in which Ken takes over Barbie's Dreamhouse and tells her that all her nights are now boys' nights, even telling her to take her belongings, and Barbie cries over the treatment she receives. For this scene, the young women used terms associated with frustration, anger, and inequality, so the emotion this stereotype evokes in them is associated with anger, but also with fear, of finding themselves in situations of violence because of their feelings (see image 4). Nonetheless, some works, such as those by Climent (2013) and Madero et al. (2021), note that when women become aware of this type of situation, it allows them to break free of emotional dependence and enables them to reorganize their lives, even allowing them to stop replicating traditional gender roles and stop meeting social expectations, as one of the participants notes:

The movie tells us that we can never keep all of society satisfied with our image and that we don't have to meet external expectations, and she believes that understanding this message can help women with their self-esteem and understand that they don't have to fit the impossible standards society demands (F3, P17).

Image 4. Emotions related to the empowered-submission stereotype

When discussing the emotions tied to the film excerpt representing the despotic-woman stereotype, in which the Barbies are shown starting a plan in which they take advantage of their empowerment, being smarter and making the Kens believe there are Barbies in distress in order to divert attention and make them believe they hold the power, so that when they let their guard down everything goes back to functioning as before, with the Barbies empowered, the participants used the terms intelligence, dignity, awakening, respect, and justice, as shown in image no. 5, suggesting that, for the women involved in this study, the representation of a despotic woman evokes an emotion of trust and disgust, since it translates into aversion, but at the same time admiration. For Lupano and Castro (2008), this despotic attitude assumed by women arises out of obligation, when they have to masculinize themselves in order not to be rejected by their male peers.

Image 5. Emotions related to the despotic-woman stereotype

When discussing the feminist-woman stereotype, the emotions associated with the film excerpt depicting the president noting that nothing should be as it was before, and that no Barbie or Ken should live in the shadows, proposing equity between the sexes, the participants used terms related to equity, change, justice, peace, and security (see image 6). This suggests that, for those present in the discussion, the feminist proposal generates emotions of disgust related to the status quo reflected in social interaction, but at the same time, its antagonistic emotion, trust in an equitable world.

These emotions can be explained as a result of the feminist movement, which promotes women's rights with the aim of ending feminine subordination as a product of the patriarchal system (Gamba, 2007), in which they are aware of how complex it is to reach this point, so they consider the only way to accept it is to use satire as a political tool:

I loved this movie, I went to see it in theaters and I think it talks about the reality of women and how nobody realizes how hard it is to want to meet people's expectations or simply be a woman, it talks about it in a very funny and extreme way about what machismo is so that it's easier to see (F2, P9).

Image 6. Emotions related to the feminist-woman stereotype

Overall, some of the participants noted that the film has a positive influence by showing that women do not have to aspire to perfection and do not need to conform to preset stereotypes, but can simply highlight their authenticity, while others mentioned that the film fosters mutual understanding and admiration among women, promoting an environment of growth and support. Additionally, they note that the film demonstrates that women are capable of matching or even surpassing men, which can boost their confidence in themselves and their abilities. They also recognize that the film captures feelings repressed by many women, which could contribute to greater self-acceptance and empowerment (see Table 3).

In this sense, it is possible to infer that, upon witnessing the gender stereotypes in the scenes from the movie Barbie shown during the focus groups, university women experienced a range of both positive and negative emotions resulting from their own personal histories, so these emotions have an effect on their attitudes and behaviors in daily life. Exposure to feminine stereotypes influences self-perception, self-esteem, and emotional well-being, and when the traditional representation of women is challenged and questioned, they feel identified with it, because in their daily survival they play various roles that challenge the traditional expectations perpetuated across past generations, which ultimately contributes to the redefinition of gender stereotypes.

Conclusions

Ultimately, the thorough analysis of the movie Barbie in the context of university women reveals that there is an interesting and complex interaction between the female gender stereotypes represented, perceptions, and, consequently, the emotions aroused in them. Through the tools used — Jung's word association test and Plutchik's wheel of emotions — it is possible to explore the emotional reactions and interpretations the participants had in response to the scenes that challenged women's gender stereotypes.

Upon witnessing these representations in the scenes, university women experienced a varied range of emotions and thoughts, ranging from empowerment to frustration. At the same time, these types of emotions not only affect their immediate emotional state but also attitudes and behaviors toward gender roles in their daily lives. However, despite the changes beginning to occur in film content, the reality of social interactions does not follow the same pattern; some even resort to dark humor and satire as representational devices, rather than confronting the social reality reflected.

Certainly, there is greater diversity and redefinition of gender stereotypes over the years; women are no longer only at home, as was traditionally the case — now they challenge these expectations, demonstrating to themselves and to the world that there is no limit when effort and dedication are involved, and although the film shows gender stereotypes, it also suggests questioning and challenging them.

This project explores how the gender stereotypes represented in films impact the emotions of young women; nonetheless, the results cannot be generalized due to the size and characteristics of the research participants, as well as the limited time available to complete this work, so it is suggested that this line of research be continued — it could even be possible to analyze changes in emotions regarding gender stereotypes cross-sectionally, in order to associate them with each woman's own lifestyle and personal history, since there is undoubtedly a need to raise awareness about the influence that the representation of women in film has on self-perception and, above all, on the need to build a more inclusive and just society in which women and men can develop fully without being limited by differentiated gender expectations.
