Abstract.

This paper aims to analyze a Mexican brand's advertising campaign to explain the role that femvertising and menvertising strategies have in the modification of traditional stereotypes and the construction of new masculine and feminine identities. A content analysis methodology is used based on the principles of femvertising proposed by Becker-Herby (2016) and adapted by Pando-Canteli & Rodriguez (2021) to analyze menvertising, as well as the characteristics of feminine and masculine identities (Montesinos, 2014; Montesinos & Carrillo; 2010). The results obtained in this research identify the representation of Mexican men assuming responsibility for household and care activities, as well as a narrative that aligns with the new masculine practices that are socially promoted and with the feminine representation that continues to be linked to submission and domestic work. Among the limitations and social implications and the main contributions of the work, it is recognized that there is a small amount of empirical research that discusses the advertising strategies of femvertising and menvertising and their role in the configuration of feminine and masculine identities, however, from the social role of advertising, the influence in the modification of traditional stereotypes associated with men and women and therefore in the construction of identities is recognized, so that both strategies can be considered ProFem.

Keywords:

advertising strategies, ProFem, new identities, gender

Introduction

The inequalities generated by the gender stereotypes used in advertising are one of the issues that has drawn academic reflection and discussion across different disciplines since the 1960s. The ways in which women and men are represented are strongly criticized, but so is the overgeneralization built on the dissolution of the feminine into the masculine and the over-specification in the pursuit of needs, attitudes, or interests directed at a single gender (Viedma, 2003). Recently, this discussion has also come to include the effects of advertising sexism on the rise in gender-based violence, translated into harassment, abuse of power, and even the murder of women, which is perpetuated through the imposition of roles between men and women tied to attitudes, activities, and contexts, both public and private, in which both operate.

Social role theory helps explain how the stereotypes used in advertising are configured, based on the role each group assumes within the social environment, that is, the beliefs, socialization processes, and individual processes that favor differentiated behaviors between men and women, which limits people's full development in relation to their aspirations, motivations, capacities, and mental and physical states, among others (Castillo Mayén and Montes Berges, 2014). In this sense, from the early twentieth century onward, the construction of stereotypes has centered on socially assuming clearly exclusionary dichotomies between men and women, in which women are advertised as lacking control, emotionally unstable, passive, submissive, tender, dependent, irrational, and with limited intellectual development, as sexual objects and taking on the role of mother, wife, homemaker, or perfect or ideal woman; men, however, are shown with self-control, dynamism, emotional stability, aggressiveness, courage, rationality, objectivity, and in dominant roles as head of the family, athlete, or executive (Carpio Jiménez, 2020; De Francisco Heredero, 2019; Sau, 1989; Viedma, 2003).

However, it is possible to recognize that, over the past decade, advertising has undergone a transformation with respect to the representation of men and women in its spots and advertising messages; some brands have joined in confronting traditional stereotypes as part of a social-responsibility campaign, but also to align themselves with segments of young people, women, and men who hold a different conception of life, dismissing patriarchal frameworks and the stereotypes attributed to both sexes. In this way, femvertising, dadvertising, and menvertising have emerged as advertising strategies that appear to be linked to the growing social demands of movements such as #MeToo, the 8M marches, Black Lives Matter, and the demands of the LGBTQIA+ community to break with traditional stereotypes and roles, which is why some international and local brands alike have jumped on this trend in an attempt to gain legitimacy with consumers.

At this point it is important to note that we agree with Leader (2019), when he points out that femvertising, dadvertising, and now menvertising serve commercial rather than cultural or political goals, but also with Schroeder and Zwick (2004), in that gender representations are socially constructed, and it is in this way that advertising campaigns, given their mass reach, modify gender stereotypes through the images and iconography that represent men and women, which is why femvertising and menvertising are promoted as allies of the social demands for equity that have intensified in recent years and can be considered pro-feminist advertising strategies (ProFem).

To the ProFem advertising defined by Menendez (2019), which refers to the ultimate aim of femvertising, we add the strategies of dadvertising and menvertising; we consider that the latter two are also shown to be in favor of feminism, insofar as they attempt to represent men in spaces removed from power and strength, in archetypes associated with fatherhood, inclusion, and equity in domestic work. All three strategies, then, seek to reconfigure the image of men and women shown through their advertising messages; that is, while femvertising is presented as a tool that promotes women's self-esteem and self-fulfillment, so-called dadvertising and menvertising seek, through their content, to modify the traditional representation of masculinity and bring men closer to domestic contexts in which emotions and household and family-care activities are present.

This research thus contributes to the discussion on the representation of stereotypes and roles of men and women in ProFem advertising, since its objective is to analyze the advertising campaign of a Mexican brand to explain the role this type of advertising strategy plays in constructing new masculine and feminine identities that seek to modify traditional stereotypes. The work is situated at the regional level, given the role that culture, values, the economy, and sociodemographic characteristics play in the construction of gender stereotypes (Connell and Messerschmidt, 2005), and a particular dairy brand is chosen: La Villita, which since 2017 has launched a series of advertising campaigns identified as ProFem.

Femvertising strategy, stereotypes, and feminine identities

Femvertising is a term coined and made known in 2014 by the agency SheKnows Media; this advertising strategy refers to advertising that seeks to empower women and girls, challenging the traditional and negative gender stereotypes and roles generally associated with them and replacing them with pro-woman messages that show them in real situations and settings (SheKnows Media, 2014; Åkestam et al., 2017). Femvertising seeks, through communication and advertising, to show women under a reality that stands in contrast to patriarchal paradigms and to serve as a spokesperson for equality and female empowerment (Carrillo, 2016). Likewise, according to Becker-Herby (2016), advertisements with a femvertising focus must contain the following elements:

  1. Diversity of female talent

  2. Pro-feminist / pro-women messages

  3. Breaking with the traditional roles and stereotypes attributed to women

  4. Minimizing sexuality (not using women as sexual objects)

  5. Authentic representation of women

We endorse the clarification made by Menendez (2019), when he notes that a truly feminist advertising cannot exist, given the ambivalence between feminism and advertising as a mercantilist strategy; however, we consider that femvertising is an advertising strategy in favor of women, which, even without intending to, can contribute to changing stereotypes and constructing new feminine identities that move away from the inherited patriarchal model (Montesinos, 2014).

In this sense, advertising messages are observed that contain identity-related elements linked to these new femininities, moving away from a simplistic and traditional representation of the female leads, giving rise to a more egalitarian representation of professions between women and men, a less physically-segmented distribution of job opportunities, and more equitable families in the distribution of roles and responsibilities — in general, more real and less perfect women, reflecting the social transformation of women in advertising (Montesinos & Carrillo, 2010; Castillo & Montes, 2014; Jarava & Plaza, 2017; González et al., 2018). Likewise, within these new femininities women retain traditional traits and roles, such as housework or childcare, but at the same time undergo changes in their actions within the public sphere (Montesinos & Carrillo, 2010), that is, they fulfill both functions and are not portrayed as a radical entity.

In this regard, Montesinos (2014) identifies new feminine identities resulting from the cultural and social change of recent years, highlighting women in positions of power who hold decision-making and leadership roles; he establishes the existence of at least six new feminine identities: 1) The autonomous woman: economically independent, generally with few children and with the capacity to decide whether or not to continue her relationship; 2) The professional woman: with a career that empowers her, without the patriarchal ties of the past; 3) The subdued empowered woman: economically independent, yet emotionally dependent on a man; 4) The despot: exercises her power in an oppressive manner over men and women, taking advantage of her empowerment; 5) The feminist: seeks relationships based on respect and equity, with the capacity for reflection and analysis; and 6) The fundamentalist: tends to view men as an enemy to defeat, holding positions that conceal her misandry.

Even though there are evident changes in advertising messages and new identities are being documented, gender gaps remain present in Mexico, despite significant progress in women's political empowerment and access to education and health; women's participation in the labor market does not exceed 49.1%, which places it below the regional average (59%), causing continued disparity in terms of income and wages, and access to senior positions (World Economic Forum, 2021).

Menvertising and dadvertising strategies, stereotypes, and masculine identities

In recent years, progress has also been observed in modifying the representation of men in advertising, with masculine stereotypes being the most resistant to change (Pando and Canteli, 2019). Research agrees that advertising content maintains the hegemonic masculine representation in which white, heterosexual, middle-class men predominate in masculine settings such as the office, nightclubs, or sports activities (Gentry and Harrison, 2010; Humphreys, 2016; Leader, 2019; Zayer et al., 2019); nonetheless, this research highlights the relevance of showing men in more egalitarian and more neutral roles to facilitate the necessary transition toward equal gender roles represented in advertising (Gentry and Harrison, 2010; Leader, 2019), owing to men's lack of identification with the way they are represented in advertising (Gentry and Harrison, 2010), which forces brands to seek new strategies to reach these audiences (Zayer et al., 2019).

Men's participation as fathers is a current topic in advertising (Sosenki, 2014), and their representation was one of the first changes made to advertising content (Rey, 2018). Some brands seek to transform the archetypes of the fatherhood role with representations of affectionate men (Leader, 2019) who are physically close to their children (Morales and Gallardo, 2021), focused on showing their emotions (Humphreys, 2016), and thereby presenting a new masculinity (Leader, 2019). Out of these strategies emerged the term dadvertising, first used in 2015 in a digital advertising context, which seeks to show the commodification of fatherhood in promotional media and which arose as part of the commodified trends of feminism (Leader, 2019); however, this is not the only masculine representation that has been modified in advertising content.

Recently, Pando and Canteli (2019 and 2021) revived the term menvertising, to refer to advertising that "proposes visual and discursive models that consciously move away from hegemonic masculinity and that aspire to construct a masculine image oriented toward achieving greater gender equality" (2019:16). The authors acknowledge that gender constructions are not rigid; however, despite changes in traditional gender models that even move away from the binary, the hegemonic masculinity described by Connell and Messerschmidt (2005) remains present, which creates a tension between the femvertising and menvertising strategies, since the former allows women to identify with advertising content through positive messages, while the latter challenges power relations and the heteronormativity of masculinity (Pando and Canteli, 2021).

Regarding the advertising content that can be identified as menvertising, Pando and Canteli (2021) build on Becker-Herby's (2016) proposal on the five aspects that femvertising campaigns must include, and formulate a tool for analyzing the visual representations and discourses on masculinity presented in this type of advertising. They note that, for a campaign to be considered menvertising, it must include:

  1. Intersectional male talent, moving away from the representation of white, young, heterosexual, middle-class men.

  2. Pressuring the gender stereotypes of heteronormative/hegemonic masculinity, with neutral spaces and roles removed from those traditionally associated with men.

  3. Real men doing normal activities and non-idealized bodies.

  4. Positive messages that reclaim the privilege of participating in cultural spheres from which men have been excluded, such as caregiving and the home.

  5. Minimizing sexuality, both in implicit and explicit content.

We agree with Connell (1997) when he notes that the concept of masculinity carries an implicit individual and cultural connotation, and is therefore inherently relational, involving one's position within gender relations and its effects on bodily, cultural, and personality practices, such that masculinity does not represent a single man nor only reflect the negative aspects of power, but rather presents various models of idealized masculine behavior within social processes linked to their own local, regional, and global framework of interaction (Connell and Messerschmidt, 2005). Connell (2005) identifies a typology of masculinities: 1) Hegemonic, which corresponds to the predominance of the patriarchal system and reproduces the dominant dynamic between the sexes, represented by middle-class, middle-aged, highly educated, heterosexual white men who are successful at work, the household's main providers, and prestigious; 2) Subordinate, represented by non- heterosexual men who are considered feminine, and therefore hold a subordinate relationship; 3) Complicit, represented by men who do not fit the hegemonic model but feel comfortable with its existence, do not abuse their power in relation to women, but are not interested in generating significant change; 4) Marginalized, represented by men who belong to ethnic groups with less privilege and power than white men.

Recently, some studies have documented alternative masculinities (Carabi and Amengol, 2015), emerging masculinities (Pineda, 2000; Messeerchmidt and Tomsen, 2012), and egalitarian masculinities (Lamont, 2015), which share in common a questioning of the dominant gender hierarchies that make possible the emergence of positive masculine practices — practices that recognize there are various ways of being a man, that question the benefits the patriarchal system has granted them, that oppose violence as an expression of power, and that promote social gender relations based on equal opportunity and rights for all people.

For Montesinos and Carrillo (2010), emerging masculinities in the Mexican context can be identified as: 1) The post-traditional man, who plays the role of provider without taking on violent behaviors, dependent on the role the traditional woman plays in the home, by mutual agreement; 2) The man in crisis, men who, due to economic circumstances, are confronted by their partner, leading to a breakup or a conflictive day-to-day relationship, and who therefore face cultural change in a state of permanent conflict; 3) The domesticated man, present within a relationship of equality because he is at an economic disadvantage relative to his partner, where the male figure is placed in a position of inferiority, but consciously recognizes his partner's merits and accepts ceding financial control of the family; 4) The modern man, men who unquestionably hold the idea of gender equality, value their partner simply for who she is, and are willing to participate in all productive and reproductive activities; 5) The carefree man, referring to men who benefit from the presence of empowered women, which allows them not to worry about the family economy — they are not interested in keeping a job or improving working conditions and are willing to help with household chores only if unemployed; and 6) The power machine, used to describe men who are constantly ready to seduce a woman and who present themselves as conquerors, invoking women's sexual liberation.

At this point it is important to recognize that, although there are new advertising strategies that promote a change in the representation of men in their content and messages, the heteronormative masculine figure continues to dominate advertising, so that masculine identities continue to revolve around power and economics, which is reflected in the reality of Mexico, where wage inequalities between men and women and unequal access to spaces of decision-making and power persist (World Economic Forum, 2021).

Method

This paper aims to analyze the representation of men and women in the advertising campaign "Juntos lo Hacemos Mejor" ("Together We Do It Better"), by the Mexican brand "La Villita," and its role in the construction of stereotypes and identities. To assess the advertising campaign, a content analysis was carried out based on the principles of femvertising proposed by Becker Herby (2016) and adapted by Pando-Canteli and Rodríguez (2021) to analyze menvertising, grouped into five dimensions of analysis (Table 1).

We agree with Pando-Canteli and Rodríguez (2019) when they note that femvertising and menvertising do not work in symmetrical terms. Femvertising seeks to motivate women to break with the roles and stereotypes of domestic life, while menvertising promotes men's inclusion in this private space, which, from the standpoint of dominant masculine roles and stereotypes, is foreign to them; nonetheless, this analytical model was chosen because it allows both advertising approaches to be compared within the same campaign; from femvertising, it allows an emphasis on women as the focus of the empowering message (Rodríguez and Gutiérrez, 2017); and from menvertising, the adoption of domestic roles that modify hegemonic traits of masculinity (Leeader, 2019), which is why this advertising can be considered ProFem Advertising, whose messages invite reflection on feminism from advertising (Menendez, 2019).

Based on the principles of femvertising and menvertising, a series of questions was established to analyze the representation of women and men in advertising (see Table 2); subsequently, the discussion of these results is approached critically from the national context, in order to recognize resistance rooted in hegemonic masculinity, as well as the importance of using ProFem Advertising to modify gender stereotypes and roles and to envision an equitable utopia between genders.

The questions were applied to an advertising campaign, which serves as a case study, since this method makes it possible to answer how a ProFem advertising strategy is developed and why it is present in current advertising content, in addition to allowing for a deeper exploration and new knowledge on femvertising and menvertising, which are advertising phenomena recently incorporated into scientific discussion (Carazo, 2006).

The brand: La Villita

La Villita is one of the dairy-product brands of Grupo Sigma, with exclusive distribution in Mexico, which starting in 2017 launched a series of advertising spots for television, out-of-home media, and digital media (Martínez, 2019), with a ProFem approach aimed at making visible the household activities carried out by women. The first campaign was titled "La deuda más grande de México" ("Mexico's Biggest Debt"), in which all the actions performed by housewives without pay are described, from nurses to economics experts, psychologists, chefs, historians, among others (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rG6zARFkTtg&t=9s). By 2018, the campaign was called "Yo no trabajo" ("I Don't Work"), in which married men were asked, "What does your wife do?", and most answered: she doesn't work, or she doesn't do anything. The spot again covers all the activities carried out by women dedicated to the home (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c5u9HCNlzKo). In 2019, the brand launched "Naturalmente creemos en ti" ("We Naturally Believe in You"), showing what everyday life would be like without women (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vEcsLnTl4D4&t=2s). Subsequently, in 2020, the brand changed the context of the message, emphasizing that men should learn to do and share household chores. The campaign is titled "Juntos lo hacemos mejor" ("Together We Do It Better"), while the spot is called "La Escuela del Hogar" ("The Home School") (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GNXQJgTJwAE); by 2021, the advertising spot "Detector de la desigualdad" ("Inequality Detector") was added to the campaign, continuing to promote egalitarian and equitable actions in the home for both men and women, but now including not only husbands but also sons and daughters (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yDO9B7_UXfs).

Case study: Advertising spot: La Escuela del Hogar ("The Home School").

The ad is 1 minute and 56 seconds long, and is divided into three parts: the first, the introduction, shows domestic spaces; the second, central part, is set in an academy; the third, the closing, returns to domestic spaces with the campaign slogan. The product does not appear at any point in the advertising spot. The commercial opens with a woman asking for help doing household chores, followed by three more scenes in different homes, where women ask the male members of their family to carry out some household task. The ad continues with a series of reflections voiced by the female leads: "So you don't have that skill?" (0:08) "Do you think we were born knowing how?" "Well no, like everything in life, this can also be learned" (0:23). This is followed by a scene showing a classroom auditorium whose students are exclusively men, with the line "Welcome to the home school. A school designed to put an end to every kind of excuse" (0:35). The following scenes show men taking cooking lessons, using a washing machine, feeding a baby, cleaning the house, and ironing clothes. The ad shows men doing household chores and invites them to "Show how good you can be" (1:39). The commercial closes by saying that the best part of this school is that there's no need to attend, because household chores are practiced at home "because the home is nobody's duty, it's everybody's task" (1:49), closing with scenes of women and men in different homes carrying out caregiving activities.

Main findings and discussion.

The results of the content analysis are presented by ProFem strategy within the advertising spot; the first section presents the characteristics of menvertising and the second, femvertising, followed by a discussion of the role these strategies play in the construction of identities, both in women and in men.

Femvertising Analysis

Note: Available at https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GNXQJgTJwAE

1. Diversity of female talent

The La Villita ad shows women of various ages, body types, and skin tones, as well as different social strata, which can be identified by the homes in which they are portrayed; they are also depicted in different occupations (professional woman, mother, homemaker), which are identified by the type of clothing and accessories they wear (Becker-Herby, 2016).

2. Pro-women messages (pro-woman)

The commercial seeks to foster men's participation in household chores: "social change begins at home" (0:01). The women's discourse portrays them as empowered, conveying a message of strength, in which they ask men to carry out some household activities, affirming that if they can do it, so can men: "Do you think we were born knowing how?" (0:21), "well no, like everything in life, this can also be learned" (0:24), "because the home is nobody's duty, it's everybody's task" (1:49). The discourse seeks to eliminate the gender stereotypes and roles traditionally associated with women, replacing them with pro-woman, inspiring, and empowering messages (Becker-Herby, 2016; Abreu, 2016; Abitbol and Sternadori, 2016).

3. Breaking traditional stereotypes (challenging what a woman/girl is commonly expected to be)

Although the commercial seeks to foster empathetic, responsible, and cooperative behaviors in men regarding household work, it maintains the representation of women as the ones responsible for and in charge of household chores, being the ones who know how to do household tasks and who must ask men to do things — to "help." This reinforces the roles and stereotypes traditionally assigned to women. Three roles assigned to the female figure can mainly be distinguished: working woman, mother, and homemaker, which coincide with the stereotypes identified by García (2005) and Rimbaud (2013) as the most common when representing the female figure in Mexican advertising.

4. Minimizing female sexuality

The commercial lacks any sexualization of the female figure; women's bodies are not used suggestively or as sexual objects, nor are they used to promote the product. The message seeks to promote household activities as a responsibility shared by both men and women, in line with the form of representation described by Becker-Herby (2016) and Bayone and Burrowes (2019) as characteristic of femvertising ads, in that they do not sexualize the female figure.

5. Authentic representation of women

The situations presented in the ad revolve around everyday household activities; women are shown authentically, without posing, in realistic situations and traditional settings (Becker-Herby, 2016; Paszek, 2018), expressing through their discourse a commitment to gender equality and setting aside the male gaze as the axis or center of the ad.

6. Identities

The ad questions patriarchal practices regarding traditional feminine identity, in which women are the ones responsible for household duties and do not work, and are therefore not financially independent. It fosters the construction of new feminine identities, portraying women in a position of leadership and decision-making, which coincides with the characteristics of the new feminine identities identified by Montesinos (2014).

Menvertising Analysis

Note: Available at https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GNXQJgTJwAE

  1. Diverse masculine talent.

Works such as those by Schroeder and Zwick (2004), Luyt (2012), and Leader (2019), examine hegemonic masculinity as represented in advertising and its close relationship with power and hierarchy among men, which does not always imply the structural dialectic of class, gender, and race, but rather determines the relationships among men themselves, which can be analyzed at the local and regional level and which give meaning to cultural reality and explain dominance within society (Conwell and Messerschmidt, 2005). Thus, the La Villita ad presents various masculine models across different generations: older men, adults, and young men; from different identifiable social strata based on the homes shown: middle and upper class; whose explicit message is that household chores are everyone's responsibility and men are not excluded on account of their sex or income. A particular feature of the commercial regarding race is that it shows the common features of Mexicans: brown skin tone (in varying degrees), straight, dark hair, brown eyes, high cheekbones, wide face, and sparse beard (Serrano, 2004), so that the implicit message shows Mexican men taking on responsibility for household and caregiving activities, which contravenes the traditional representation of Mexicans in advertising, which shows male dominance and the celebration of men for simply being men (Plana and Silva, 2020).

  1. Messages in favor of new masculinities

The commercial is a good example of how the images generated by the media can be modified in order to transform the traditional model of masculinity associated with violent behavior or displaying male supremacy, replacing it with alternative masculinities that present men with responsible and empathetic behaviors toward domestic work. The commercial's explicit message centers on generating ways of life in which gender roles are demystified, beginning with the equitable distribution of domestic work among household members: "Sharing household chores is something practiced at home, because the home is nobody's duty, it's everybody's task" (1:48), accompanied by affirming scenes of interaction among family members, which promotes the celebration of this type of masculinity (Pando-Cantelli and Rodríguez, 2021).

  1. Heteronormative limits of gender stereotypes.

The advertisement breaks the limits of hegemonic masculinity's gender stereotypes, through the representation of men in domestic settings not traditionally associated with dominant masculine traits, such as the kitchen within a home rather than public spaces of power such as offices or driving a sports car. Men are shown cooking, washing dishes, and ironing clothes, challenging traditional gender roles and stereotypes. It refrains from reinforcing male hegemony and sends a positive message by reclaiming participation in areas from which men have been culturally excluded (Pando-Cantelli and Rodríguez, 2021).

  1. Minimizing male supremacy

The ad directly challenges the sexualized male gaze and becomes an invitation to construct a new masculinity derived from a current social construction, in which household activities are de-feminized and become a shared responsibility, so that today, more than ever, men are being encouraged to contemplate images of other men in order to transform themselves (Paterson and Elliott, 2002). The commercial promotes equity in domestic work between men and women, calling on them to better themselves and become the best ironers in their household.

  1. Real men

Although the situations presented do not revolve around the product (dairy products), they do revolve around activities associated with it, such as cooking and food preparation, but what stands out is the representation of men in natural, everyday situations, such as dirty dishes in the kitchen or freshly washed clothes; men even appear wearing conventional clothing without any emphasis on normative ideas about their body or physical appearance, adopting an empathetic narrative that seeks identification with consumers around new masculine practices (Padilla, 2016) and breaking with the idealization of bodies as a normalized element of body-image change (Paterson and Elliott, 2002).

  1. Identities

The spot bets on a shift in masculine identities, questioning paternal practices developed within traditional masculine identity and sustaining the emerging discourse of fatherhood (Sosenski, 2014) and involvement in childcare and upbringing. It even shows sons involved in household chores such as washing dishes or ironing clothes, reflecting a changing social landscape that draws less rigid distinctions between the feminine and the masculine (Bartholomaeus and Tarrant, 2016; Wentzell, 2013a, 2013b).

Discussion

Among the main findings, it is possible to note that even though the advertising spots do not openly declare themselves femvertising or menvertising strategies, they meet the principles proposed for the analysis, in that they show age, ethnic, and body diversity in both men and women; they promote positive messages about equity in caregiving and household activities; they show men and women performing the same domestic activities, without a power relationship or sexualization of the spot's protagonists; the situations revolve around domestic activities, through an empathetic narrative, without being framed from a male perspective, thereby questioning the traditional identities of men and women, showing emerging masculinities centered on the idea of gender equality and femininities oriented toward decision-making, in which the idea of the domestic and private as part of the assigned sexual division of labor is deconstructed.

In analyzing the femvertising strategy, we agree with the pillars identified by Becker-Herby (2016): women are represented in real contexts and with diverse physical characteristics, such as age, body type, and skin tone, with inspiring messages that promote change in the reality of domestic work as lived in Mexico; however, it does not modify traditional roles, since it presents women in the traditional stereotypes associated with their representation in advertising: homemaker, mother, and working woman (García, 2005; Rimbaud, 2013). What is evident, however, is the minimization of female sexuality in the representation of women in the spot (Bayone & Burrowes, 2019), where, although it is true that the advertised product is not the axis, neither is the female figure. Regarding feminine identity, the commercial questions traditional patriarchal practices around it, fostering the construction of new feminine identities, positioning women as human beings capable of making decisions, which coincides with the characteristics of the new feminine identities identified by Montesinos (2014).

The results on menvertising made it possible to engage in discussion with the works of Schroeder and Zwick (2004), Luyt (2012), and Leader (2019), who note that masculine representation in advertising is linked to power; nonetheless, our results allow us to identify a representation of Mexican men taking on responsibility for household and caregiving activities, accompanied by affirming scenes of interaction among family members, promoting equity in domestic work between women and men, an empathetic narrative that seeks identification with consumers around new masculine practices (Padilla, 2016), and questioning paternal practices around traditional masculine identity while supporting the emerging discourse of responsible fatherhood (Sosenski, 2014).

Conclusions

During the selection and documentation of the advertising corpus for this research, it was possible to identify that the campaign's advertising spots, in addition to being available on the YouTube platform, were broadcast on open television during nighttime hours during telenovela programming, with very limited placement in sports events; this means the message is received mostly by women rather than men, and awareness of the need to transform roles and stereotypes does not arise under the same conditions, which can lead to conflicts due to the gaps generated in social-transformation processes, in which women demand greater equity and men do not identify or recognize the origin of these demands; in this sense, we agree with Patterson and Elliot (2002) on the need to question advertising's true intent, as well as the reach of femvertising and menvertising, since by increasing the feminization of masculinity reflected in advertising campaigns, far from readjusting the counter-hegemonic forces of the patriarchal structure, they seem to protect the dominance of consumer society, and can therefore be considered advertising strategies that seek brand image, joining femverwashing and menverwashing.

Regarding the role of advertising in the construction of new masculine and feminine identities and stereotypes, we consider that it plays a strategic role, since advertisements influence how we think about masculinity and femininity (Goffman, 1979); nonetheless, we also believe it is high-risk to assume that sex-differentiated strategies can modify heteronormative conceptions of identity, traditional gender roles, and limit the consumer's body to new forms of masculinity and femininity.

Certainly, both masculine and feminine identities cannot be considered a sexual or bodily characteristic, nor even a personality trait (Connell and Messerschmidt, 2005; Montesinos, 2014), since these are the result of the configuration that arises from social interaction and cultural influence, which is why gender identities differ across particular settings (Connell and Messerschmidt, 2005) and interact with the consumption of images, products, and desires represented in advertising; these representations therefore play a central role in shaping conceptions of masculinity and femininity (Schroeder and Zwick, 2004), and advertising strategies, even though they may be questioned, produce and reproduce social signifiers — hence the importance of modifying the representation of men and women in advertising spots, since it has the capacity to be a tool that not only promotes consumption but also modifies dominant gender preconceptions.

At this point, it is necessary to acknowledge the limitations encountered during this research, such as the small number of empirical studies preceding this one, given that the femvertising and menvertising advertising strategies have only been included in the research agenda over the past three years; additionally, the content analysis was carried out on the advertising campaign of a single nationally distributed Mexican brand and covers only the most recent spot; nor was the collection of empirical consumer data included among the research objectives to analyze how these advertising strategies contribute to modifying identities among women and men, since there are no scales available for measuring this, so the design of measurement scales for these variables can be flagged as a pending research agenda item.

Regarding the practical and social implications of this research project, it is possible to recognize that analyzing the content of advertising strategies that promote gender equity in roles and the division of labor, as well as the modification of traditional stereotypes associated with women and men, makes it possible to identify the implications these have for the formation of new femininities and masculinities, which move away from an unequal distribution of power and move closer to showing spaces of equity between both, so that they can be used by those responsible for designing advertising campaigns, making it possible to deconstruct the reach of advertising and its message in favor of a fairer, more equal society.

Finally, we can recognize that the main contributions of this research lie in analyzing femvertising and menvertising within a single advertising content, given that both can be considered ProFem strategies, since they promote a shift in paradigms regarding stereotypes and roles of women and men, but above all in favor of equity; nonetheless, the possibility remains to discuss these advertising strategies as a brand-washing tool, seeking to legitimize brands' social responsibility.
