The digital age in post-COVID-19 Higher Education: Innovation in the classroom

Abstract.

The COVID-19 pandemic abruptly transformed higher education worldwide, creating an urgent need for innovation to ensure academic continuity. The accelerated shift to online teaching, supported by virtual platforms and videoconferencing, revealed both opportunities and limitations within the educational system. This study aims to identify the main approaches adopted by higher education institutions during this adaptation process, with a focus on the integration of educational technologies, the implementation of hybrid learning models, the strengthening of digital competencies, international collaboration, and continuous faculty training. A qualitative documentary method was applied through a review of specialized literature. Results indicate that the health crisis fostered the widespread use of technological tools and highlighted the critical importance of digital inclusion. It is concluded that, although considerable progress was achieved, challenges remain regarding equity in access, faculty development, and the long-term sustainability of these innovations.

Keywords:

digital age, higher education, educational innovation

Introduction

We live in an era characterized by rapid technological evolution, which has generated multiple sources of knowledge and more efficient access to information, simplifying tasks and facilitating communication. In the context of higher education, the incorporation of digital platforms has profoundly transformed teaching and learning methods, directly impacting the development of key competencies in students. However, this transition has also revealed a series of challenges that must be overcome to ensure equitable access to technology, among which resistance to change, the improper use of Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs), and the uncertainty generated within the educational ecosystem stand out — all factors that influence the quality of knowledge transmission between teachers and students (Arriaga-Cárdenas & Lara-Magaña, 2023). The health crisis caused by COVID-19 accelerated the integration of digital technologies into all areas of daily life; although their incorporation into education was already significant, it became indispensable not only to ensure continuity of teaching-learning processes in virtual environments, but also as a mechanism for adapting to global lockdown. The World Health Organization (WHO) declared the COVID-19 outbreak a global pandemic on March 11, 2020 (Cucinotta & Vanelli, 2020); from that point on, the world faced one of the most severe crises in recent history, marked by mandatory lockdown, social distancing, and the global interruption of in-person activities.

The pandemic affected not only public health but also social and economic spheres, driving a profound reorientation of values and priorities across multiple sectors. In the case of higher education, this created a need to adopt flexible, innovative, and adaptive learning models capable of ensuring competitiveness both nationally and internationally. In countries such as Mexico and other Latin American nations, this situation accelerated the development and implementation of new educational strategies centered on the intensive use of technologies that would strengthen educational systems and their long-term sustainability.

Context

The health crisis caused by COVID-19 disrupted educational systems around the world, affecting nearly 1.6 billion students in more than 190 countries (UNESCO, 2020). The closure of schools and other learning spaces impacted 94% of the global student population; faced with this situation, educational institutions were forced to implement alternative forms of teaching, developing virtual and hybrid models according to local conditions, which posed new challenges for students, teachers, and even families.

Although the health emergency spanned multiple dimensions (health, economic, and social), the educational crisis manifested itself as a "silent crisis." Di Gropello (2020) warns that its consequences, although not always immediately visible, could shape a bleak scenario in the medium and long term. The pandemic had structural effects on the economy and education, with far-reaching global impacts; one of the most significant effects was the sudden closure of schools, which could represent, for the current generation, an accumulated loss of income close to 17 trillion dollars, equivalent to 14% of global Gross Domestic Product (GDP) (UNESCO, 2021). These factors contribute to increased school dropout, intensified by global economic crises and prolonged interruptions in access to education. This situation has been described as a generational catastrophe (UNESCO, 2020); the virtualization of teaching represented a challenge for students and teachers accustomed to a traditional in-person model, and this sudden transition generated feelings of uncertainty and anxiety, as it required new teaching-learning skills and strategies.

Beyond the educational sphere, the impact of COVID-19 was also reflected in the human development of societies. According to the 2020 Human Development Report, as can be seen in figure 1, there was an unprecedented drop in the Human Development Index (HDI), showing a setback in key indicators such as education, health, and income; this deterioration particularly affected people living in extreme poverty, including millions of students at all educational levels.

According to United Nations data, from its Development Programme (2020), the closure of educational facilities primarily affected countries with fewer economic resources, widening pre-existing gaps in access to education; this situation could lead to an irreparable learning crisis and an increase in school dropout: it is estimated that more than 24 million students worldwide could leave their studies as a direct consequence of lack of access to technology and economic resources during the pandemic. The digital divide continues to represent a significant obstacle to the integration of ICTs and artificial intelligence in education, because not all students have the same access to devices and internet connectivity; this situation reproduces existing gaps and limits learning opportunities.

Any socio-educational policy aimed at addressing inequalities within higher education must recognize these factors as obstacles to be overcome, in order to guarantee each student a formative process that takes their academic particularities into account and is not limited to promoting generalization or homogeneity within the university community (Achoy-Sánchez & Jiménez-Segura, 2023). It is therefore essential to implement policies that guarantee equitable access to educational technology tools (Ramírez-Ochoa et al., 2024). Literature review

This section presents a systematic review of the relevant literature underpinning the link between educational innovation and the development of digital knowledge, especially in the context of the crisis brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic. It highlights theoretical and empirical contributions that help us understand how technologies have been incorporated into teaching-learning processes and the challenges this represents.

Educational innovation

Educational innovation is understood as a set of concepts, procedures, and strategies, systematically organized, that seek to generate significant modifications to traditional teaching methodologies. Sebarroja (2002) conceives of it as a continuous process that critically examines classroom practice, institutional structure, interaction within the educational community, and the teacher's professional approach; innovation, therefore, should not be considered an isolated event, but rather a sustained process of transformation involving all educational actors.

According to Rivilla et al. (2015), digital resources have revolutionized social interaction within education, facilitating new forms of communication through e-books, forums, chats, email, social networks, among others; these tools not only allow information to be shared, but also promote collaborative and participatory environments.

In this context, Mejía-Trejo and Aguilar-Navarro (2022) warn that the success of innovative educational projects does not depend solely on the quality of the design, but also on uncontrollable external factors, such as the structural conditions of access to technology, which were severely affected by the pandemic. The implementation of new pedagogical strategies poses significant challenges for both teachers and educational institutions, among them the migration from the traditional in-person lecture model to virtual formats, which has generated emotions such as anxiety, uncertainty, and resistance to change (Menéndez-Vega, 2012).

Digital knowledge

Shulman (1986) proposed a model that recognizes three fundamental types of teacher knowledge: content knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, and curricular knowledge; these components are integrated into what he called Pedagogical Content Knowledge (PCK), emphasizing the importance of pedagogy in disciplinary teaching. However, this model proved insufficient in the face of emerging technological challenges.

Mishra and Koehler (2006) updated this approach by incorporating an additional component: technological knowledge, giving rise to the TPACK model (Technological Pedagogical and Content Knowledge). This model recognizes the need to integrate digital technologies into pedagogical practices, allowing for a reflective and contextualized use of technological tools according to educational content and objectives; however, teacher resistance to change, associated with the use of traditional, familiar technologies (such as the chalkboard or the physical classroom), remains a relevant obstacle.

In the wake of the health emergency, pedagogical strategies such as the flipped classroom were revived, proposing a reorganization of traditional roles in the teaching-learning process; in this model, knowledge transmission takes place outside the classroom, through digital resources, and class time is devoted to deepening activities, problem solving, and practical application.

According to Bristol (2014), this strategy allows the student to learn at their own pace, as long as the information is available and accessible; the teacher, for their part, takes on the role of guide and facilitator, designing learning pathways aimed at achieving specific competencies. The flipped classroom approach is based on three pillars: student-centered learning, the development of higher-order thinking skills, and structured pedagogical guidance between student and teacher.

Finally, the hybrid learning model combines the best of in-person and virtual environments. Christensen et al. (2013) define it as a formal learning model in which the student controls key aspects such as pace, time, and place, while the teacher partially supervises and guides the process; this model integrates digital experiences with planned in-person meetings, generating flexible learning pathways adapted to students' needs.

Methodology

This study adopts a qualitative, documentary approach, focused on a review of specialized literature published from 2015 onward. The research focuses on analyzing educational innovation in the context of higher education during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, based on the use of technological tools and their influence on the teacher-student relationship in virtual environments.

This study prioritizes understanding how higher education institutions have responded to the health crisis through innovative teaching-learning models; for this reason, emerging educational models and their impact are also analyzed, based on the principle that education is constantly evolving and must adapt to new contexts. Technological tools and learning technologies are key elements in this process, closely tied to the foundations of the constructivist model (Davies et al., 2013).

This analysis made it possible to identify and classify various pedagogical approaches implemented in response to the health emergency. Knowledge of these models provides elements for understanding their characteristics, areas of application, limitations, and relevance according to specific educational needs. Results

Distance learning models have represented a significant challenge for higher education institutions, as they require attending to the diversity of student learning styles, ensuring technological accessibility, and ensuring the quality of content; in this context, the teacher's role has been radically transformed: it is no longer limited to designing content, but also requires ensuring its effective delivery, adapting to the conditions of the environment, and fostering a Virtual Learning Environment (VLE) that responds to the individual needs of each student (Sankey et al., 2010).

One of the main findings of this study is the widespread adoption of ICTs as the primary resource for knowledge transmission; the adaptation of faculty and students has been key to the success of educational innovation during the pandemic caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, and this context gave rise to a new concept: virtual classrooms as emerging learning settings.

Zubillaga and Gortázar (2020) note that distance education requires greater course planning and design, but many higher education institutions (HEIs) were forced to accelerate these processes given the sudden closure of classrooms. This response was defined as emergency remote teaching, a provisional solution that led to the adoption, adaptation, and innovation of multiple pedagogical models.

Distance education is, by nature, a complex phenomenon; it involves unconventional forms of teaching-learning that demand intensive use of technology, allowing temporal and spatial flexibility as well as greater student autonomy (Moore and Kearsley, 2012; Vlachopoulos and Makri, 2019). Nevertheless, this flexibility also revealed a structural problem: digital inequality.

Inequalities in access to higher education are widening, as social sectors with middle and high incomes are overrepresented in university enrollment; at the same time, public universities are going through a profound crisis of both political and social legitimacy, caused by a drastic reduction in funding, which has negatively impacted the working conditions of academic staff as well as the state of the physical and virtual infrastructure from which programs and courses are delivered (Silva, 2025). According to Cabrera et al. (2020), access to distance education in disadvantaged households is conditioned by the educational level of family members and the technological resources available. Fernández-Enguita (2016) stresses that having a computer and an internet connection is not enough: it also matters how many devices exist in the household and how many people must share them, a common situation in Latin America.

Despite HEIs' efforts to equip their students with adequate technology, structural inequalities persist (Alva de la Selva, 2015); this reality forms what Sevillano-García et al. (2016) call a "plurimodaltic" ecosystem, characterized by various combinations of technological resources and levels of access, which directly affects learning opportunities and educational equity. Over the past year, technological platforms designed to support university teaching have proliferated, and models such as blended learning (Llorente and Cabero, 2009) have gained popularity for their ability to integrate in-person and virtual experiences, facilitating more adaptable and sustainable pedagogical scenarios.

Information and communication technologies (ICT) continue to transform higher education with an unprecedented impact (Falco, 2017); this evolution has driven the design of educational technology models that can be registered as patents, although significant challenges remain, especially in the Latin American context, and in particular in Mexico (Arriaga et al., 2022). In this scenario, ongoing faculty training is essential; Johnson et al. (2009), in the Horizon Report, note that knowledge construction must be supported by ICTs, since these enable organizing, creating, and working collaboratively.

Finally, studies such as those by Sigalés (2004) and Cejudo (2008) emphasize the importance of continuous teacher training to improve the quality of technology use in virtual environments. The British Educational Communications and Technology Agency (Becta, 2004), as well as Kalembera and Majawa (2015), have noted the importance of recognizing and addressing the technological barriers and shortcomings present in educational environments; these limitations directly affect both faculty and institutions, and making them visible is the first step toward a structural transformation capable of eliminating them at the root. Along the same lines, Duart and Lupiáñez (2005) argue that teacher resistance to using technology does not always stem from a lack of individual willingness; in many cases, this resistance originates in institutional limitations, such as organizational rigidity, the absence of clear educational innovation policies, or a lack of investment in technological infrastructure and teacher training; this institutional resistance, often invisible, is one of the main obstacles to consolidating sustainable digital educational models.

Distance education has represented not only a challenge but also an opportunity for the educational system, testing its responsiveness, its technological infrastructure, and its pedagogical strategies; the high demand for access to education from students, especially those who could not continue their in-person education, forced higher education institutions (HEIs) to quickly adapt their teaching-learning methods (Cantor, 2019).

In this context, many HEIs committed to strengthening their technological and pedagogical resources, dedicating significant efforts to developing virtual learning environments that would meet new demands; this accelerated transformation, resulting from the pandemic, not only involved changes in teaching modalities but also implied a profound modification of traditional pedagogical practices (Paul & Jefferson, 2019).

Faced with this landscape, HEIs face the challenge of improving their structural components in order to develop relevant skills and competencies in their students, aligned with the demands of a competitive global environment; this process requires collaboration strategies with external entities, such as Public Research Centers (PRCs), which play a crucial role in strengthening scientific knowledge without compromising university autonomy; these collaborations allow teachers, students, and researchers to participate in innovation and knowledge-production processes in a coordinated way (Arriaga et al., 2022a).

Discussion

Education worldwide was put to the test during the health emergency caused by COVID-19, especially at Higher Education Institutions (HEIs); this scenario forced a profound rethinking and modification of traditional training processes, and in this context, distance and hybrid learning models emerged as key alternatives to ensure educational continuity and answer the call for innovation.

The intensive use of technological tools had a significant impact on teaching, forcing teachers to develop new digital competencies and redesign their pedagogical strategies; as a result, technology became permanently embedded in educational processes, promoting student-centered models with more flexible, autonomous, and collaborative learning.

Virtuality not only made it possible to maintain communication between teachers and students, but also expanded opportunities for international collaboration; distance ceased to be a barrier, enabling conferences, classes, and academic gatherings through digital platforms, which enriched both teaching and research. However, unequal access to technology and connectivity revealed persistent gaps in the educational system, which continue to represent structural challenges to achieving equitable and inclusive education; despite institutional efforts, full coverage has not yet been achieved, requiring HEIs to develop sustained pedagogical and financial strategies to close these gaps.

This shift affects not only the technological sphere but also the way learning is assessed, raising questions about the validity of traditional practices in digital environments. Higher education must move toward authentic assessment models that value understanding, critical thinking, and student autonomy. In this new context, the teacher's role has changed radically, no longer as the sole transmitter of knowledge but as a facilitator, mediator, and designer of learning experiences; this shift requires ongoing training, effective instructional planning, and mastery of digital environments, and so, in short, the educational world has changed; this new paradigm demands a redefinition of the teaching-learning process, the consolidation of new pedagogical strategies, and a collective commitment to ensuring that future generations develop within a dynamic, adaptive, and sustainable educational environment.

Conclusions

The health crisis caused by COVID-19 had an unprecedented impact on every sphere of society globally, with higher education being one of the most affected sectors, particularly in Latin America. Faced with the challenges posed by the pandemic, Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) were forced to adapt quickly, adopting alternative approaches to ensure continuity of the educational process; this situation accelerated the incorporation of digital technologies and educational platforms, radically transforming the way knowledge is taught and accessed.

Based on the analysis carried out, several key aspects are identified that must be considered in order to strengthen educational innovation in contexts of uncertainty; among the areas where the most problems were found are: how to implement policies that reduce the digital divide between students and teachers, providing economic and technological support to vulnerable students, strengthening teacher training in digital competencies through ongoing training programs, fostering interaction and emotional support in virtual environments, improving online assessment strategies to ensure their relevance and fairness, and guaranteeing institutional investment in technological infrastructure and innovative educational resources.

The pandemic acted as a catalyst to accelerate educational transformation processes that, under normal circumstances, would have taken many more years; although the abrupt transition to online teaching models posed a significant challenge, it also opened the door to new opportunities to reimagine education.

The use of emerging technologies, the strengthening of hybrid learning, and the development of soft skills stand out as central pillars in the redesign of post-pandemic higher education. In this context, crises take shape as spaces of opportunity for reinvention, and the COVID-19 health emergency was no exception; the experience represents an urgent invitation to build a more resilient, inclusive, and sustainable educational system, capable of responding to the challenges of the present and the future.
