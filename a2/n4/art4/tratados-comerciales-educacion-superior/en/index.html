The impact of trade agreements on higher education

El impacto de los tratados comerciales en la educación superior

Abstract.

The objective of this article is to analyze the educational implications of the Treaty between Mexico, the United States and Canada (T-MEC) through a critical comparison with with two other strategic alliances which are the Common Market of the South (Mercosur) and the European Union (EU) in order to formulate recommendations to strengthen the educational dimension of the T-MEC. The comparison was made through an exhaustive bibliographic search and an analysis of the treaties with a focus on the internationalization of higher education and the following categories of analysis: recognition of degrees, academic mobility, educational systems and migration policy. It was concluded that Mercosur and the European Union have specific agreements for the recognition of higher education degrees, which facilitates academic and professional mobility in the respective regions as a strategy for internationalization. On the other hand, although the T-MEC has increased academic mobility, networks and academic collaborations between the three countries, the agreement does not directly include professional mobility in the treaty. Research is relevant in strategic, social and academic terms, considering the review of the conditions established in the treaty by the three countries in 2026.

Keywords:

trade agreements, educational implications, Mercosur, European Union

Introduction

The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO, 2022), in the working document of the Third World Higher Education Conference, states that cooperation among countries will be key to achieving the goals of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and to building a safer, fairer, and more sustainable environment for the future. In a socio-economic context as complex as the current one, Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) are making efforts to produce knowledge through transdisciplinary approaches, via increasingly close networks and alliances.

Trade agreements should lead to closer collaboration among the world's countries and regions in order to reduce disparities through international cooperation (UNESCO, 2022). The integration of labor markets and the recognition of degrees are key pieces of the internationalization of higher education. Deepening the processes of market globalization is essential to achieving greater points of international convergence in higher education (Van Damme, 2019).

The general objective of this paper is to analyze the educational implications of the Treaty between Mexico, the United States and Canada (T-MEC) through a critical comparison with the educational frameworks of two other strategic alliances, namely the Common Market of the South (Mercosur) and the European Union. The research is carried out with the aim of generating proposals to strengthen the educational dimension of the treaty.

As specific objectives, the paper set out to:

  • Compare the educational internationalization strategies adopted by the T-MEC, Mercosur, and the European Union.

  • Analyze and systematize the regulatory frameworks for professional practice and the free movement of people within the T-MEC, Mercosur, and European Union regions.

  • Formulate recommendations to strengthen the integration of educational aspects into the T-MEC, based on good practices and comparative models.

This study fills knowledge gaps, given that there is little work on the link between trade provisions and their connection to educational policy, and there is also a lack of analysis regarding educational policies and the strategic sectors of the T-MEC. Finally, little attention has been paid to regional educational cooperation opportunities.

In the literature review presented below, an extensive bibliographic review was carried out on internationalization, international cooperation in Latin America, and trade agreements, in order to establish a general overview and conduct a critical analysis of the current status quo.

Background

This section analyzes the key concepts of regionalism, internationalization, and international studies, followed by a contextual description of the trade agreements and their main characteristics.

Sehgal (2010) states that regionalism is a concept that has developed since the Second World War, taking on political and economic dimensions, with the creation of various strategic alliances such as the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO), the Commonwealth, and the EU. These organizations were created to maintain national and regional stability. Regionalism was a defining feature of the postwar period and led to the creation of the new global economy, characterized by ever-greater interdependence among countries and a lack of national self-sufficiency.

The aforementioned author, referring to the T-MEC, states that it is one of the few examples of a treaty between two developed countries and one developing country in the same region, designed not only to expand trade among North American neighbors, but also to strengthen the ability of North American companies to compete more effectively in regional and global markets.

UNESCO (2023), among the Ten Pillars for Rethinking Tomorrow's Education in Latin America and the Caribbean, considers the internationalization of higher education one of the key pieces for improving educational quality, and cooperation agreements are part of it. According to de Wit et al. (2017), the internationalization of Higher Education in Latin America should seek a balance between competitiveness and its commercialization, thinking of education as a public good with a greater focus on internationalization at home.

The Latin American and Caribbean Institute for Higher Education (IESALC, 2019) states that internationalization in the region should be based on interinstitutional collaboration grounded in solidarity and equality, prioritizing South-South cooperation and integration. It is crucial to foster intercultural dialogue that respects cultural particularities and identities, promoting the creation of networks among universities, and favoring the circulation and use of knowledge as a strategic social resource that drives the sustainable development of the region and its nations.

International Studies, as a multidisciplinary academic field, analyzes the relationships among states, transnational networks, and globalization processes that affect social, economic, and educational structures worldwide. Its objective is to understand global phenomena such as diplomacy, trade, security, cooperation, and international governance (Baylis, Smith & Owens, 2020).

Education has become an essential component within International Studies, insofar as it fosters the formation of global citizens and promotes the development of intercultural, linguistic, and political competencies. Building human capital with a global perspective has become a strategic priority for countries integrated into trade blocs such as the T-MEC, the European Union, or the Pacific Alliance.

Contemporary trade agreements are no longer limited to the exchange of goods and services, but now incorporate chapters on educational cooperation, academic mobility, and mutual recognition of degrees (De Lombaerde & Van Langenhove, 2007). These instruments seek to align educational policies with the demands of the global labor market, strengthening the ties among universities, productive sectors, and governments.

The contextual framework of the FTA, MERCOSUR, and the EU

First, it is essential to present the definition of free trade provided by Jaramillo and Tonon (2023), who interpret it as spaces in which goods, services, or labor can circulate freely. The authors note that half of world trade is generated within these blocs and consider it a positive tool for the economy when applied correctly, bringing about economic, cultural, and social changes, for example in the education sector.

The following presents some useful data to contextualize NAFTA in comparison with Mercosur and the European Union. Canada, the United States, and Mexico generate a combined Gross Domestic Product (GDP) similar to the GDP of 13 Asian countries and 28 European countries. During the term of the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), trilateral trade grew by 265.4% (Gobierno de México, 2024).

The Latin American country with the highest GDP is Brazil, with 2,126.81 billion US dollars, followed by Mexico with 1,811.47 and Argentina with 621.83; Uruguay ranks 13th with 76.24 and Paraguay ranks 15th (World Bank Group, 2024). Comparing the countries belonging to the EU and NAFTA, it is worth noting that the EU, with its 25 member countries, had a Gross Domestic Product (GDP) of 16-18 trillion in 2023, while the NAFTA countries together generated 24-26 trillion dollars. In 2023, the world's GDP was estimated at approximately 105 trillion dollars, and the United States was the country with the highest GDP in 2023 (World Bank Group, 2024).

The European Union

The European Union (EU) is a bloc with deep economic integration, a common currency (the euro) for 19 of its 27 members, and a single market. This facilitates trade and the movement of people among member countries. The EU economy is based on a combination of high-technology industries, advanced manufacturing, and a strong services sector. Education occupies a central place in the European Union, as it is considered the foremost tool for "building" its citizenship, since it brings cultures into contact and raises awareness of a shared European heritage, while also serving to strengthen European economic and technological competitiveness (Onursal, 2012).

Since the Bologna Process in 2000, which marked the creation of the European Higher Education Area and the unified credit system, the European Union has opened up to internationalization and the free movement of students throughout the Union, so that educational cooperation would link education to the economy and shape European citizenship (Lawn & Grek, 2012).

Mercosur

Mercosur was created on March 26, 1991, with 4 countries: Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay. In 1996, Chile, and in 1997, Bolivia, became part of a Free Trade Area with Mercosur. The treaty has not achieved a common market like the European one, but rather an imperfect customs union with the elimination of tariffs, where large socio-economic differences persist among member countries. The European Union has supranational bodies with decision-making power over nations; Mercosur does not — agreements reached must then be approved at the national level, which makes it more difficult to achieve the objectives agreed upon within Mercosur (Nuske, 2023).

From its inception, Mercosur included education as a key element. Work began on the accreditation of undergraduate programs to harmonize university diplomas and degrees through the Experimental Accreditation Mechanism (MEXA) and the creation and implementation of national program evaluation and accreditation agencies, achieving good results for the movement of professionals in the region and the recognition of degrees. From the outset, the education sector was engaged, establishing quality criteria with the goal of creating a common language among countries for the accreditation of certain degree programs, including agronomy, medicine, and engineering (González, 2015).

The Free Trade Agreement (FTA)

In January 1994, the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) was created, which was later renamed the Mexico, United States, and Canada Agreement (T-MEC) in 2018. The treaty was approved after a decade of negotiations and, although characterized by a large discrepancy in the size of the three economies involved, it laid solid foundations for future growth, providing a valuable example of the benefits of trade liberalization, and it drastically reduced barriers to trade and investment, which nearly tripled among the three countries. Today, the United States leads in technology and innovation, Canada has a solid economy based on natural resources and mining, while Mexico stands out for manufacturing and low-cost goods production (Ruiz and Gómez, 2021).

From its inception, several researchers were concerned with analyzing the educational impacts of the treaty (Altbach, 1995; Marúm, 1997) and pointed to the need to include education in the FTA in order to build educational bridges given the evident inequality among the three countries. Mexico had to make major changes to its educational system, since the program accreditation mechanisms already active in Canada and the United States were absent in the country. As a result of the treaty, accrediting agencies were created in Mexico to improve and harmonize the quality of Mexican education. Another issue addressed with the entry into force of the Free Trade Agreement was the creation of a credit system, very useful for the revalidation of studies abroad or exchange programs within national territory. Development and academic excellence programs were also created, including the Mexican Association for International Education, founded in 1992, and the Center for North American Research established by UNAM (Barrow et al., 2003).

Aboites (2007) reports that trade agreements can lead education to become a service, opening countries to the international commodification of education and facilitating the free cross-border flow of knowledge that occurs within large corporations. Although the FTA has improved quality of life in North America, more ingredients are still needed, such as improvements in educational quality, the environment, and labor standards, according to Sehgal (2010).

Method and results

The methodology used in this study was a comparative documentary analysis, a strategy that allows for organizing and evaluating information from secondary sources to identify similarities and differences among the educational policies of the regions analyzed. It was therefore necessary to identify the categories of analysis for the research through a literature review, in which relevant documents were analyzed, such as the official regulations of the three blocs (Tratado de Libre Comercio, 1994; Gobierno de México, 2020; Comisión Europea, 2024 and MercoSur, 2024), internationalization databases (Bustos Aguirre et al., 2022; Erasmus +, 2024; MercoSurEducativo, 2024; Opendoor.org, 2024 and Statistics Canada, 2024), as well as academic sources that allowed for a critical analysis of the regulations (Altbach, 1995; Marúm, 1997; Barrow et al., 2003; Aboites, 2007; Sehgal, 2010; Lawn & Grek, 2012; Onursal, 2012; González, 2015; McKenzie, 2016; Ruiz & Gómez, 2021; Nuske, 2023; Passarini, 2023).

The categories were then systematized and contrasted across the different treaties. The categories derived from an exhaustive review of the literature were: academic mobility, recognition of degrees and professions, accreditation of university programs, and migration policies. These categories show how each bloc interprets educational internationalization. Comparative documentary analysis makes it possible to compare educational policies or regulatory frameworks across different contexts, generating valuable knowledge on best practices and areas for improvement. The European Union promotes the comparability and compatibility of member states' educational systems, seeking to ensure that university programs meet quality criteria. In Mercosur, even though each country carries out an internal evaluation, the treaty's peer evaluators conduct an external evaluation to ensure compliance with regional criteria. The stated objective is to strengthen regional cooperation among HEIs and facilitate the mobility of professionals within the bloc (Passarini, 2023). In the T-MEC, each country seeks to meet national educational quality standards, and there are limitations on the mutual recognition of degrees due to the lack of a common framework. There is no integrated accreditation system as in the EU or Mercosur, since education falls under each country's own jurisdiction (the US, Canada, and Mexico). On the other hand, Mercosur and the European Union have specific academic mobility programs, while the T-MEC does not.

The migration policies of the United States, Canada, and Mexico reflect their geopolitical, economic, and social priorities, showing both similarities and differences. The United States adopts a restrictive approach, emphasizing border security and control of illegal immigration, especially along its border with Mexico. Its system favors immigration based on labor skills and family reunification, although the processes are complex and slow. In recent years, policies have become more polarized, affecting refugees and asylum seekers.

Canada, in contrast, has a more open migration policy, prioritizing education, labor skills, and adaptability. It promotes immigration as a tool for economic growth and cultural diversity. It also stands out for its humanitarian commitment, with extensive refugee programs. Mexico, due to its geographic location, acts as a country of origin, transit, and destination. Although it has historically facilitated regional migration, in recent years it has strengthened controls under pressure from the United States. Its asylum system has grown, but faces resource and management challenges.

In common, all three countries face challenges such as human trafficking and uncontrolled migratory flows. However, the differences lie in their emphasis: security (US), integration (Canada), and regional balance (Mexico). The European Union stands out for having the most structured and efficient system for the recognition of higher education degrees, which facilitates mobility within the region. Discussion of Results

From the analysis carried out, it can be inferred that Mercosur has made more systematic efforts at the regional level for the accreditation and recognition of degrees through the ARCU-SUR system (González, 2015; Souto, 2018), because the treaty considers that education and the movement of professionals within the regional space can benefit the economy. As for the T-MEC, on the other hand, education and the revalidation of studies aimed at the free movement of professionals in the region are not addressed, due to the enormous diversity and disparity of economic and social situations that exist between Mexico and the other two countries belonging to the treaty. It focuses more on bilateral agreements and the mutual recognition of certain professions; it does not include a specific formal mechanism for the recognition of higher education degrees, but it does promote educational cooperation and dialogue among the educational systems of the three countries (McKenzie, 2016; Ornelas, 2017; Smith, 2020).

Regarding academic mobility, what Barrow et al. (2003) already noted more than twenty years ago about Mexico is observed, and the same trend continues: inbound mobility was low and remains so today, according to the data presented in Table II in the previous section. What the authors observe, including Dado (2017), is that the FTA has driven the creation of research networks and academic collaborations among the three countries.

On the other hand, the European Union has implemented the Bologna Process, which establishes a common framework for higher education, including the creation of the European Higher Education Area (EHEA). This process facilitates the mutual recognition of degrees and academic credits among member countries (Nuske, 2023). The EU's progress on migration policy is key to the creation of the EHEA, and the T-MEC's lag in educational matters is related to a closed migration policy. In trade terms, the T-MEC has brought very different benefits. The United States could be considered one of the major beneficiaries in terms of market access and lower prices for consumers, although with costs in terms of employment in some sectors. Canada also benefited significantly, especially in terms of access to the US market and attracting investment. Mexico experienced notable economic growth and a major increase in exports, but with internal challenges in terms of social and economic inequality (Pastor, 2011).

The three regional spaces analyzed are very different. As Van Damme (2019) noted, the European Higher Education Area is one of the most developed examples of a regional-scale qualifications system. Trade agreements such as Mercosur provide for, albeit to a lesser extent, the mutual recognition of qualifications, thereby driving convergence processes in international education.

Conclusions

The T-MEC served at the time as a catalyst for raising awareness of the need to transform the Mexican educational system, changes that have taken place over the years. Although trilateral relations with Canada and the United States have increased, the foundations for a true trilateralization have not been built. Unlike what happened in Europe and partly in Mercosur, educational relations among Mexico, Canada, and the United States of America (USA) still need to become closer. From the analysis carried out, it is observed that the key to an international education policy is having a migration policy that sustains it.

It is true that internationalization processes in the region have improved, given that inbound mobility of students from the United States and Canada to Mexico has increased since the implementation of NAFTA thanks to the exchange agreements created among the countries.

However, to date the T-MEC remains a trade agreement that does not include education and falls far short of what has occurred in Mercosur and the European Higher Education Area, because migration issues and educational issues were excluded from the treaty at the time.

For its renewal, it would be desirable for education to be included as a priority axis, considering the following aspects. First, the treaty should facilitate academic mobility and the recognition of degrees by expanding exchange programs for students, faculty, and researchers, establishing a framework for the mutual recognition of degrees and certificates, and promoting trilateral academic exchange programs.

Additionally, it is essential to create a cooperation framework for training in strategic areas such as artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, and renewable energy through dual education, partnerships between companies and universities, and trilateral training centers in key sectors to strengthen the labor skills of the future.

It would also be desirable to harmonize educational standards and research and development (R&D) cooperation through the creation of certifications that allow for the standardization of technical and professional competencies in the three countries, through trilateral funding for joint research projects, promoting the creation of centers of excellence at universities in the three countries, and thereby facilitating the mobility of researchers to foster knowledge transfer.

Integrating education into the T-MEC would not only strengthen economic competitiveness, but would also drive innovation, inclusion, and sustainable development in North America. If these aspects are considered in a future renewal of the treaty, the region would be better prepared to face the challenges of the 21st century.

2026 is a key year because the T-MEC is under review, and it would be timely for the review to include a joint internationalization strategy comprising: academic cooperation, and the revalidation, recognition, or accreditation of degrees or certifications so that students can continue their studies in the three treaty countries without major difficulties, or professionals can move freely to work in the three countries.
