Operant conditioning techniques as facilitators in the performance of managerial activities. Case: Gardening and landscaping services company

Abstract.

Managerial performance can be influenced by the effectiveness of human talent and is reflected in business indicators across various areas such as marketing, production, or services. To improve managerial performance (Daniels, 1989), it is necessary to equip managers and administrators with practical techniques that help stabilize and regulate staff activities, promoting both organizational development and individual satisfaction. In this regard, operant conditioning, as a practical alternative, offers easily understandable and applicable tools for managers in the context of organizational performance management. The objective of this case study is to evaluate the impact of positive reinforcement on the performance and productivity of operational workers and its consequences on the company’s operations. The study focuses on a tertiary-sector company, applying positive reinforcement techniques to a manager and three subordinates from a corporate gardening and landscaping services firm in the Guadalajara Metropolitan Area. Overall, the results show an increase in employee productivity and performance as a consequence of the reinforcement program to which they were exposed.

Keywords:

operant conditioning, performance, management

Introduction

One of the main topics in the study of organizations and management in general is business performance and the way in which managers and administrators generally seek to improve and develop their organizations through, among other resources, human talent. From this perspective, the executive, manager, or business owner, even though their organizations often have process-control systems and resource-management tools in place, sometimes find it difficult to get work carried out correctly in order to reach organizational objectives; and the question is: why, despite having those systematized processes and the tools a business needs to function, does the business fail to develop or achieve its objectives?

This puzzle may originate in the performance of human talent, since it is people who carry out all of a company's systems, and both managerial performance and company performance depend on this resource. Thus, inadequate execution of people's functions within a company may stem from lack of training, the individual's own interest in the work they do, or simply because staff are not motivated — the latter potentially being a relevant factor, since it is closely related to the functioning and performance of the company. Drawing on the theoretical review, this motivation stems from both internal and external factors (García, 2012); the former relate to the individual's own interests, while the latter relate to motivational states generated within the company. Focusing on external factors, managerial performance and the way managers or administrators lead take on significant importance, since, on the one hand, they are responsible for generating motivational states in human resources and, on the other, they are responsible for their own performance and that of the company.

This paper addresses topics related to managerial performance and motivation within organizations, proposing managerial techniques for staff management, applying them within the company, and subsequently analyzing how the use of these techniques improves both managerial and organizational performance.

Managerial performance in human talent management

Performance as such is related and oriented to the outcomes of an activity carried out by an individual, a work team, or the organization as a whole (Ochoa et al., 2014); it could be defined as the effectiveness and efficiency in reaching a particular objective or goal (Lusthaus et al., 2002; Muchinsky, 2002; Ochoa et al., 2014), and it also involves innovation, change management, as well as the business's financial profitability (Lusthaus et al., 2002).

Managers, executives, or administrators in general share a common objective in their planning: the development of the organization and the improvement of performance across the company's functional areas, interpreted as an improvement in profit margin, costs, or business profitability. Since companies carry out activities to satisfy society through goods and/or services, and companies also work with individuals, organizational results depend directly on the performance of those individuals; in this sense, human resource management can play a pivotal role in how the company functions.

According to Mondy and Noe (2005), human resource management can be defined as the use of people or individuals, viewed from the standpoint of company resources or assets who help achieve the organization's objectives, and they assert that managers must be involved in human talent management, since it is assumed that managing implies carrying out an activity through other people, which means whoever manages must be efficient at managing people.

In this sense, managers play a fundamental role in how human resources are administered, since their work and their relationships within the company are rooted in interaction with staff, as people are the focus for transforming inputs into results.

Along these lines, Calderón (2004) notes that proper human talent management and its appropriate involvement in the company is reflected in capabilities that improve organizational performance: the company's capacity to execute its strategies; the capacity to improve executives' performance in the efficiency and effectiveness of their activities; and the capacity for organizational change.

An important factor in developing these capabilities is staff motivation, since this motivating drive makes it possible to achieve organizational objectives and improve employee performance.

Motivation can arise from various sources, one being each individual's personality, preferences, and tastes; likewise, motivation can arise from the environment surrounding the individual within the company, which, according to García (2012), identifies as external motivating factors: financial compensation, social recognition in the sense that the work performed by the individual serves the community or society at large, and recognition within the company.

In this sense, motivating staff is a difficult task for administrators, since aligning the company's proper functioning with staff's good performance often creates a gap that unbalances the fulfillment of organizational objectives against the development of people and their interests. Thus, motivating is not solely about financial incentives but goes further, involving delegating, training, coaching, and developing people (Soberanes, 2014).

Applying behavioral principles to increase organizational performance

According to the literature review, the application of behavioral principles within organizations has helped them in areas that become fundamental within the organization (Daniels, 1989); among the most common, one can identify those that resolve quality-management problems (e.g., Vargas, 2007), which address situations involving the implementation of systems that improve the quality of company outcomes, and where, through behavioral modification, such systems can be carried out or maintained in the pursuit of customer satisfaction through products and/or services.

Another common problem is addressing employee motivation and job satisfaction (e.g., Young, 2007), through incentive programs or schemes aimed at retaining employees longer within the institution; likewise, efforts have been made to improve task performance (e.g., Stajkovic & Luthans, 2003; Teen Wei & Yazdanifard, 2014) through training using behaviorist learning methods in order to increase staff capabilities.

In this context, behavioral modification procedures have been applied to manage workplace safety and health (e.g., Cabrera, 2013; Martínez, 2015; Torres, 2019), specifically in situations where the use of protective equipment becomes unavoidable, as may be the case in many organizations where employees, for various reasons, do not use safety equipment correctly, generating incidents that translate into financial losses and harm to employees' physical integrity — this is where behavioral procedures come into play.

Similarly, behavior modification has been used to improve the performance of administrative and managerial staff, seeking to manage personnel better (e.g., Bahadur, 2008; Lilian et al., 2016) and, of course, to become leaders.

In the study by Martínez and Rodríguez (2020), whose objective was to increase employee productivity at a customer-service company through the use of tangible (bonuses) and intangible (recognition) positive reinforcers over a six-week period, the results showed a 35% increase in case resolution by the experimental group, while the control group maintained constant levels. This study showed that immediate, specific recognition can generate sustained improvements in job performance.

In the study by González et al. (2018), operant techniques were applied to improve teacher punctuality and attendance at a private institution. The objective was to reduce absenteeism through social reinforcers (public praise) and material reinforcers (consumption vouchers). The methodology was observational with direct intervention over three months. As a result, attendance increased by 22% and perceived workplace climate improved according to internal surveys. This study highlights how positive reinforcement can influence not only observable behaviors but also subjective variables such as motivation and commitment.

Likewise, Ramírez and Torres (2019) explored the application of positive reinforcement at a logistics services company, with the aim of reducing errors in shipment documentation. They used a point system accumulated through correct performance, redeemable for internal benefits. The methodology was experimental, with weekly follow-up over two months. Errors were reduced by 40%, and workers reported greater satisfaction with the evaluation system. This case demonstrates how reinforcement can be integrated into performance-management systems without generating resistance.

Finally, López and Hernández (2021) conducted a study at a financial services company, where behavioral modification techniques were applied to improve customer service. The objective was to increase the frequency of empathetic behaviors among advisors. Immediate verbal positive reinforcement from supervisors was used, along with weekly feedback. The methodology was longitudinal, involving analysis of call recordings and satisfaction surveys. The results showed an 18% increase in customer service ratings, along with improved employee retention.

In general, these interventions have taken place within the context of the operant tradition, better known as the principles of positive reinforcement (Kurgat et al., 2015; Lattal & Porritt, 2008), without excluding or failing to recognize other approaches within psychology that can be and have been applied in businesses (e.g., cognitive, interbehavioral, psychoanalytic). However, for the purposes of this case study, the work was carried out from the operant-conditioning approach.

Principles of positive reinforcement

Operant conditioning, or positive reinforcement, is based on the three-term contingency (Skinner, 1953): that is, a stimulus evokes a response in the individual, and in the presence of that response, a reinforcer is presented contingently, which in turn serves as a stimulus for some other response to occur. Under this logic, a stimulus, in general terms, is an object, event, or behavior that evokes a response or behavior in the individual, so that a reinforcer is a stimulus that, presented contingently on the response, causes the probability of the individual emitting that behavior to increase, decrease, or remain unchanged.

Thus, reinforcement contingencies can be either positive or negative, translated into terms of positive or negative reinforcement, as well as positive or negative punishment. It is reinforcement (+, -) when the presentation or removal of a reinforcing stimulus following a response increases the probability of that response occurring, and it is punishment (+, -) when the presentation or removal of a stimulus following a response decreases the probability of that response occurring.

In this way, individuals' behavior is controlled by reinforcement schedules (Domjan, 2010), which prescribe the onset and termination of stimuli, specifying the relationship between the response and the reinforcer (Bados and García, 2011; Martin and Pear, 2008); these are rules establishing the moment at which a reinforcer is delivered for an emitted behavior, and these schedules can be classified as continuous or intermittent.

Method

For this study, the case of a company in the Guadalajara Metropolitan Area dedicated to gardening and landscaping services for businesses and individuals was taken, applying a reinforcement program to four company employees; three of them work as gardeners (operational staff) and one as manager (administrative staff). An organizational diagnosis was carried out in order to detect problematic symptoms using the "Root Cause Diagram" tool (Lara, 2011), focusing on problems affecting staff and the company's results. Training was then provided to the manager and the business owner on understanding and applying reinforcement techniques within the organization; the implementation procedure was as follows:

Following the diagnosis, a behavioral modification plan was drawn up, in which the following were identified: 1) the operational staff's problem behaviors; 2) the model or target behaviors for operational staff; 3) the measurement parameters for the behaviors; 4) the establishment of reinforcers; and 5) implementation.

In step 1, both the manager and the business owner identified problem behaviors that were causing a decline in organizational performance; records of this were kept over a four-week period in order to establish an initial baseline, which then set the standard for recording desired behaviors in step 2. For this case, a list was drawn up of the behaviors employees should exhibit while performing their work duties (see Table 1), which were later incorporated into the token economy board (step 5); these behaviors were assessed based on four productivity parameters: quality of service, adherence to processes, and handling of work equipment (step 3).

Table 1.

List of the activities to be performed by operational employees.

1Properly arrange tools in the warehouse.
2Properly arrange tools in the truck.
3Aerate soil.
4Sweeping: Garden cleaning.
5Clear brush.
6Manual weeding: Flower beds, tree wells, lawn.
7Prepare the appropriate tool according to the type of work.
8Fill out the service checklist.
9Collect trash.
10Use, care for, and take responsibility for the continued and proper use of PPE.
11Prune hedges, shrubs, and trees.

Note. Own elaboration

For each activity, a score from 0 (activity not performed) to 100 (activity that fully meets the established parameters) was given; if the employee failed to meet one of the parameters, a score within that range was assigned. At the end of the week, employees exchanged their accumulated points from the weekly rating for the reinforcer(s) of their choice (i.e., each reinforcer offered to the employees involved had a specific point value) (step 4). The maximum number of points per activity was 600 per week (100 per day), and in total each employee could earn up to a maximum of 7,200 points per week (600 x 12) across the 12 proposed activities.

All of the above, now in step 5, was implemented with the help of the "Token Economy" tool (Martin & Pear, 2008), made up of the list from step 2, the days of the week (Monday through Saturday), and the name of each employee, in order to record their daily scores there.

It should be noted that the application of both the tool and the reinforcers, as well as the performance records, depended exclusively on the administrative staff in charge (manager) with support from the business owner, since they are responsible for improving both their own performance and that of their employees. Finally, the application period for measuring results was four weeks, and results were measured according to the productivity parameters established in the plan (step 3). Likewise, the results were analyzed using charts showing the records and progress of the activities for each of the employees involved.

Discussion

This case study shows the impact that the application of operant techniques has on the performance and productivity of operational workers, as well as the possible effects on business operations. According to the results, it was observed that, for the three gardeners, their initial evaluations were identical, in line with the measurement parameters proposed by the company and the manager's rating judgment, agreeing that the tasks were performed incompletely or at an average level, which is consistent with the initial diagnosis carried out. Likewise, it can be observed for all three cases that none of the employees performed activities 1, 2, 7, and 8 (see Table 1), even though they were asked to, and this directly affected the business's productivity in terms of equipment maintenance, as it resulted in repair or replacement costs, as well as in sales, since it led to poor service. In this sense, administering reinforcers significantly increased performance, especially for these activities, as shown by the percentages, since they spiked from the first week of intervention.

It is likewise possible that employee motivation increased with the delivery of reinforcers, since the behaviors remained on the rise for the remaining three weeks of the intervention; although scores dipped in the third week for two of the three cases, they never returned to baseline levels and recovered by the final week of intervention.

According to a post-intervention interview with the manager, he reported having greater control over his subordinates' activities and that the activities that had not previously been performed would improve the quality of service provided to client businesses, as well as equipment care and the time spent organizing inventory. Regarding the above, and pending further studies, it can be said that applying the principles of positive reinforcement can improve employee performance, ease the work of those directing activities, and, above all, have a positive impact on organizational results.

Likewise, it is suggested that studies with a longer exposure period be conducted to analyze the maintenance of the behaviors, so that these gains are not merely a result of novelty within the company producing such exponential increases, as well as to define precise metrics for evaluating the company's results as a consequence of implementing reinforcement programs within the organization.

Conclusions

The benefits of positive reinforcement in service companies are numerous. First, it makes it possible to increase desirable behaviors without resorting to sanctions, which improves the workplace climate. It also strengthens intrinsic motivation when combined with verbal recognition and can be easily integrated into performance-evaluation systems. It has also been observed to reduce staff turnover by generating greater satisfaction and a sense of belonging.

However, there are difficulties in its application. One of the main ones is consistency in delivering the reinforcer, since if it is not administered immediately and contingently, it loses its effectiveness. Resistance may also arise on the part of supervisors, who are not always trained to identify reinforceable behaviors. Moreover, if reinforcement is perceived as manipulation or favoritism, it can generate internal conflicts.

For this reason, it is essential that organizations wishing to implement these techniques do so with proper training, clear evaluation criteria, and ongoing follow-up. Positive reinforcement is not a magic solution, but when properly applied, it can transform organizational culture and significantly improve performance.
