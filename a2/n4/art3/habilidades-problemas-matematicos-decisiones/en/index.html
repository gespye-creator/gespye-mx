Development of skills to solve mathematical problems applied to Administrative Sciences for decision making

Abstract.

This paper presents the results of implementing an analytical assessment rubric, designed as a methodological guide for solving and evaluating mathematical problems focused on decision-making in the business environment. The study was conducted with a second-semester group of higher education students in the business field at a public university in west-central Mexico. The research is cross-sectional, descriptive, qualitative and quantitative. To design the rubric, four elements described in the literature as part of the problem-solving process were taken and a fifth was proposed for the interpretation of results within the context. The findings indicate that all five categories improved by the end of the academic year compared to those recorded at the beginning. Finding the solution showed the lowest performance at the beginning of the course but also the greatest variation, while interpreting the mathematical results in context showed the least change.

Keywords:

decision making, mathematical skills, educational resources

Introduction

Decision-making in business is a cornerstone of organizational success, as it enables leaders to face complex challenges, develop innovative strategies, and implement effective solutions that help achieve stated objectives, especially in uncertain and rapidly changing environments (Ruisli et al., 2025).

Mathematics plays a primary role in many areas of knowledge, and the field of business administration is no exception, as it helps develop analytical and problem-solving skills, for example. Teachers with mathematical expertise can make an important contribution to training students in entrepreneurship and business management (Lawal et al., 2025).

The logical reasoning frameworks provided by mathematics positively affect capabilities in data analysis, problem analysis, risk analysis, financial management, strategic planning, problem solving, innovation, and effective decision-making (Lawal et al., 2025; Ruisli et al., 2025).

For decades, students' ability to use mathematical knowledge to solve applied problems has been a central topic in mathematics education research (Cevibkas et al., 2022; Ukobizaba et al., 2021). In addition, the teaching-learning process highlights students' difficulty in thinking critically, an indispensable ingredient for problem solving.

Cevibkas et al. (2022) argue that applying mathematical knowledge in the real world is a central feature of mathematical literacy, which is why fostering this competency is a widely pursued goal in mathematics education. In the educational setting, problem solving enables students to build their learning from didactic or real-life situations, in addition to representing an assessment element that seeks to identify and understand their reasoning process in a specific situation, as well as examine the characteristics of that process (Pascual Vigil et al., 2020).

The purpose of this proposal is for students to develop skills to solve problems in a logical and structured manner for decision-making within a business-economic context, applying higher-education differential and integral calculus concepts and tools, guided by an analytical assessment rubric.

Background

The mathematical problem-solving process

In the mid-1940s, mathematician George Pólya established a four-stage heuristic reference framework to describe the mathematical problem-solving process: understanding the problem, devising a plan, carrying it out, and examining the solution. Although his proposal originated in the field of mathematics, the model became a reference in other areas of knowledge (Schoenfeld, 1987) (11)

Four decades later, mathematician Alan Schoenfeld argued that, in addition to the heuristic aspects or cognitive strategies proposed by Pólya, other elements are required to describe the complexity of the mathematical problem-solving process, in particular: mastery of disciplinary knowledge; the use of metacognitive strategies involving self-monitoring of learning processes; and students' belief systems about the nature of mathematics and its teaching (Schoenfeld, 1992) (8)

For its part, the socio-constructivist perspective establishes that, in addition to the cognitive component of problem solving, interaction with other people represents a factor that accelerates learning, since discussion favors the creation of new patterns of analysis and solutions (Alonso-Berenguer et al., 2018) (7)

The systematic literature review by Alonso Berenguer et al. (2021) (6), conducted on various types of publications spanning two decades, identifies the resources needed to address the mathematical problem-solving process: mastery of specific knowledge, mathematical skills, strategies (heuristic and metacognitive), algorithmization, problematization, contextualization, and the didactic transposition of mathematical content.

Assessment of the mathematical problem-solving process

For decades, educators have shown concern for implementing processes they can use to plan, implement, execute, and evaluate teaching-learning processes, with the aim of ensuring the construction, appropriation, and management of students' knowledge (Bancayán Oré, 2013)

The work of Serres Segarra et al. (2024) (3) proposes an assessment rubric to detect mathematical talent in elementary school students. It measures characteristics of mathematical ability and mathematical thinking; the problem-solving process, level of reasoning, speed in solving problems, and capacity for transversality; autonomy, the invention of new problems, and mathematical inquiry.

Ordaz Villegas and Acle Tomasini (2021) (4) used a system of rubrics to measure mathematical performance in first- through third-grade elementary students. This study made it possible to identify students' strengths, as well as areas of insufficient performance that could hinder the acquisition of new knowledge.

The study by Acebo-Gutiérrez and Rodríguez-Gallegos (2021) (1) proposes a rubric to assess the level of mathematical modeling in secondary school students, including four dimensions related to the phases of mathematical modeling: problem formulation, resolution, interpretation, and validation. The authors suggest this type of instrument to promote mathematics learning through modeling strategies.

The study by Flores López and Auzmendi Escribano (2018) (2), framed around gender and ethnicity issues in the context of higher education, states that competencies for solving mathematical problems are related to the processes of thinking and reasoning; arguing and justifying; communicating; modeling; representing; and posing and solving problems. The results were obtained from a 32-item questionnaire organized into 6 competencies, which consisted of categorizing and describing the properties, characteristics, and profiles of the people, groups, communities, processes, and objects analyzed.

For their part, Aravena Díaz et al. (2022) (5) analyzed the characteristics of STEM skills (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) in first-year civil, industrial, computer, and construction engineering students, using mathematical modeling as a bridge between mathematical concepts and their respective disciplines. This study focused on the case method to measure: mathematical modeling and communication skills; scientific skills for formulating hypotheses and carrying out research processes; technological skills for the use of media and communication; and engineering skills for proposing solutions and drawing conclusions. The results were obtained through content analysis of the students' work.

The systematic literature review by Cevibkas et al. (2022) highlights that the main discussion on measuring students' competence in mathematical modeling —which involves problem solving— is centered in Europe, with a predominance in Germany, Africa, and Asia; in North and South America, a limited number of studies were found. Additionally, most of them focus on the analysis of the upper-secondary level and, to a lesser extent, the university level.

Rationale

Authors such as Flores López and Auzmendi Escribano (2018) highlight the importance of linking mathematical disciplinary content with its application in the field of professional training. In particular, there is evidence that teachers who tend to link mathematical content with applied problems tend to improve student training in the business field (Lawal et al., 2025).

In relation to this field, the mathematical concepts addressed at the higher-education level are used to model cost, revenue, profit, and production functions; to analyze changes in their behavior; to calculate total or average values; to maximize production, sales, or profit; to minimize costs and reduce inventory expenses; to measure the sensitivity of demand to price changes; to analyze the equilibrium between producers and consumers of a product or service in an economy; to determine how much market value is being captured through the calculation of consumer and producer surplus; and to calculate monetary values accumulated over time, to mention a few examples.

Despite its importance, mathematical problem solving is difficult for most students, and efforts continue to understand its complex nature in order to facilitate its teaching and learning (Alonso Berenguer et al., 2021).

The ability to solve problems is a mathematical competency that can be developed with an appropriate combination of instructional strategies, as shown by the systematic literature review by Cevibkas et al. (2022), while studies such as that of Ukobizaba et al. (2021) additionally highlight the importance of assessment. The latter also state that enriched assessment activities are linked to the improvement of students' problem-solving skills, insofar as these tasks represent challenges to the understanding of conceptual aspects.

Contributions such as those of Gómez Mena (2024) and González Garibay et al. (2020) hold that, within a study program, it is essential to carry out an adequate assessment that provides information on students' progress in order to design improvement actions toward achieving greater learning.

In the context of the evolution of educational institutions, there is a need to introduce assessment systems that improve validity and reliability over traditional forms and, moreover, that involve the student in that process (Ríos Muñoz and Herrera Anaya, 2017; Valverde Berrocoso and Ciudad Gómez, 2014). In this sense, one of the characteristics of assessment for learning is that it should provide timely and specific feedback to students while also giving the teacher information on the achievement of expected learning outcomes (Gómez Mena, 2024; Martínez González, 2020). Good assessment should foster self-learning and self-regulation in students so that they become aware of their practices and learning (Gómez Mena, 2024; Ríos Muñoz and Herrera Anaya, 2017).

For authors such as Sánchez Mendiola (2020) and Valverde Berrocoso and Ciudad Gómez (2014), the rubric is a recommended instrument for carrying out assessment of and for learning. From the perspective of formative assessment, its use guarantees an increase in students' learning and academic performance (Fraile et al., 2017).

For the design of this research, the notion of formative assessment derived from Gómez Mena's (2024) systematic literature review was considered, whose holistic vision highlights its continuous nature, centered on improving student learning, with an emphasis on providing timely and meaningful feedback that allows educational practices to be adjusted when necessary.

For their part, rubrics can help students visualize learning goals and the extent to which they have been achieved, through assessment criteria together with the level of achievement of each one (Valverde Berrocoso and Ciudad Gómez, 2014). Specifically, the analytical rubric makes it possible to assess student performance, as well as identify strengths and weaknesses in what is being assessed (González Garibay et al., 2020), so as to reinforce the former and undertake correction or improvement strategies for the latter (Gómez Mena, 2024; Rodríguez-Gallego, 2014).

Methodology

The research detailed below is descriptive, cross-sectional, quantitative, and qualitative in nature. Non-probabilistic convenience sampling was used, and questionnaires were designed and administered with questions measured on a 5-level Likert scale, as well as dichotomous and open-ended questions. An analytical assessment rubric comprising 5 categories and 4 achievement levels was designed and applied.

The study was carried out with a group of 47 students during the second academic term of 2024, in a second-semester mathematics course at a public university in west-central Mexico. It presents the results of implementing an analytical assessment rubric as a guide for solving mathematical problems in an applied context, for solving problems and making decisions in a business-economic context.

The course covers topics in differential calculus of functions of two variables and integral calculus of functions of one variable, and is part of the curricula of the bachelor's degree programs in Economics, International Business, Marketing, Administration, Financial and Systems Administration, Human Resources, and Environmental Management and Economics, which represent half of the academic programs offered on the institution's business-economics campus. The course's prerequisite is precalculus and differential calculus of functions of one variable, taken in the immediately preceding term.

Classes were held twice a week in two-hour sessions under the Flipped Learning model (Flipped Learning Network, 2014). By institutional policy, work was conducted in person for three consecutive weeks plus one week of asynchronous virtual work each month throughout the academic term.

An exploratory survey was designed and administered at the beginning of the course to inquire about students' expectations regarding the course, their study and work context, their perception of their performance in the applied problem-solving process, among other aspects. At the end of the academic term, the same exercise was carried out with a different instrument that, in addition to the previous topics, gathered their opinions on various factors, notably course design, the assessment method, and the design and usefulness of the rubric.

The course's assessment method consisted of individual questionnaires with automatic feedback, quick team assessments, and integrative team activities at the end of each learning unit.

For the design of the educational resource, an analytical assessment rubric was considered to guide and assess students' work in solving applied problems in the context of business-economic sciences. The instrument comprises 5 categories representing the parts of the problem-solving process, and 4 performance levels.

The literature offers various approaches that classify the stages for solving problems. For this study, the first four categories of the rubric were considered based on the proposal by Pascual Vigil et al. (2020), and a fifth category was incorporated into this instrument to assess the interpretation of the results obtained in a business-economic context. The categories are:

  1. Information and data describing the problem. Key information is extracted from the initial problem statement, for example: Is it a production, cost, or profit problem? What are the variables? In what units are the variables measured? What is the model's time frame?, among others.

  2. What needs to be done to solve the problem. The path to be followed to obtain the expected result from the data provided in the problem is identified.

  3. Strategies for solving the problem. Which mathematical tools can be used to reach the expected result — in other words, the "how".

  4. Solution of the problem. Was the correct result obtained? Were there any calculation errors?

  5. Interpretation of the results in the context of the problem. Is a specific meaning given to the results of the process carried out? Is this information used to guide decision-making within the context of the problem?

Regarding performance levels, four were considered: 1. Absent, 2. Basic, 3. Sufficient and 4. Exemplary. To develop the achievement descriptors, the Anderson and Krathwohl taxonomy for teaching-learning and assessment processes was used, adapted to the higher-education field (Bancayán Oré, 2013), which is built on Bloom's cognitive-domain taxonomy. This author states that this taxonomy facilitates knowledge management, as it activates sequential thought processes that ensure the acquisition and operationalization of skills and knowledge.

To grade students, a numerical rating scale out of 100 points was established, taking into account each stage of the problem-solving process along with its level of difficulty and performance. The rubric is shown in Table 1.

At the beginning of the academic term, with advance notice to students, an individual diagnostic assessment was carried out, consisting of solving an applied problem in the administrative and business field using the mathematical concepts learned in the prerequisite mathematics course. During the test, students were allowed to use formula sheets, class notes, and a scientific calculator.

By the third week of the course, the rubric was presented to students and used regularly to solve problems during in-person work sessions, so that they would learn to work with it as a guide for solving applied problems in the context of their professional training.

At the end of each of the three learning units that make up the course program, an integrative activity was carried out in teams of two or three, consisting of solving one or more applied problems in a business-economic context. Each assessment of this kind incorporated the different concepts covered since the first topic of the course, that is, it was cumulative in nature. To carry out these activities, teams were allowed to use the rubric, class materials, and specialized software. It was specified that Generative Artificial Intelligence could not be used, nor could consultations be made with people outside the team.

In the session following each integrative activity, a detailed review of the teams' work was carried out in order to analyze the strategies proposed, the processes followed, the results obtained, and their various interpretations. Even though the rubric provides immediate feedback once the grade is announced, this session allows students to reflect on their own processes and results and to complement their perspective with the work done by their classmates.

At the end of the academic term, an individual assessment was carried out that included solving a problem covering the course topics, in which students used the rubric, course materials, and a scientific calculator or specialized software.

Results and discussion

The individual diagnostic assessments, the team integrative assessments, and the individual final assessments were graded using the proposed assessment rubric. For the results analysis, only students who completed the course and carried out all the activities were considered, that is, a total of 35 people. This group of students had an attendance rate of 70% or more in the in-person work sessions. The following presents the analysis of assessment results for the five categories that make up the proposed analytical assessment rubric.

Diagnostic exam

The results of the diagnostic exam show that, on a one-to-four average scale, for identifying Information and data describing the problem, students scored 3.1; What needs to be done to solve the problem, 2.4; Strategies for solving the problem, 2.1; Solution of the problem, 1.4 and finally, Interpretation of the results in the context of the problem, 1.9. These results are shown in Figure 1.

Given that the achievement-level measurement scale ranges from one to four, students showed the best performance in Identification and data describing the problem at a Sufficient level. This may be because academic mathematical problems commonly present certain functions, variables, and a context, for example, in order to begin working. On the other hand, some aspects that tend to be less evident to students are the units of measurement of the variables (whether the mathematical model considers hundreds of units or thousands of pesos, for example) and the time frame (whether measurements are monthly, annual, etc.), among others.

In the following stages of the process, students' performance decreased, and even though they had some clarity regarding the data presented in the assessment, they were less able to determine What to do to solve the problem and even less able to define a Strategy to solve it; for this last aspect, the level found was Basic. Finding the Solution, meanwhile, showed the lowest score of all the measurements, barely above Insufficient. In this regard, it has been observed that students frequently make mistakes by not respecting the order of operations, or when handling the calculator or using specialized software, for example.

Regarding the Interpretation of mathematical results in a context, greater ability was found compared to actually finding the Solution, placing slightly below the Basic level. The results of the survey conducted at the beginning of the academic term indicate that around 85% of students considered themselves capable of finding the solution to the problem, while approximately 69% believed they were capable of interpreting the results obtained.

Integrative activities

The results obtained in the team integrative activities carried out at the end of each of the course's three learning units are presented below. On average, the category Information and data describing the problem scored 2.7, 3.0, and 3.6, respectively; What needs to be done to solve the problem, 2.8, 2.8, and 3.8; Strategies for solving the problem, 2.6, 3.1, and 3.9; Solution of the problem 1.3, 1.6, and 2.1 and, finally, Interpretation of mathematical results in an applied context 2.2, 2, and 2.4. In the first integrative activity, the analysis of group work reflects that the category Information and data describing the problem decreased slightly compared to individual performance, going from an average of 3.1 to 2.7. However, What needs to be done to solve the problem and Strategies for solving the problem, rose from 2.4 to 2.8 and from 2.1 to 2.6, respectively. Together, these three categories were close to the Sufficient level on the performance scale.

For its part, this activity showed a performance similar to the individual one, though slightly lower, in Solution of the problem and Interpretation of mathematical results in a context, going from 1.4 to 1.3 and from 1.9 to 2.2, respectively — barely above the Insufficient level in the first case and Basic in the second.

For the second integrative activity, a slight improvement could be seen in Identification and data describing the problem, Strategies for solving it, and Solution of the problem. Regarding What needs to be done to solve the problem, no change was observed, as it remained at 2.8, close to the Sufficient level, while Interpretation of mathematical results in an applied context, showed a slight decrease from 2.2 to 2, barely a Basic level.

It is worth mentioning that the course's first learning unit covers differential calculus content for functions of two variables, which follows on from the prerequisite course, taught by different teachers with diverse methodologies, teaching strategies, and assessment methods, among others, while the second unit includes integral calculus concepts for functions of one variable, specifically indefinite integration, and the third unit covers definite integration. In these last two units, the topics are introduced for the first time and are covered with all students at the same time, with the same materials and under the same working dynamic, which allows information and concepts to flow under a single design logic.

The results of the third and final integrative activity showed improvement in all rubric categories compared to the previous one. Overall, Information and data describing the problem, What needs to be done and Strategies for resolving it went from being below the Sufficient level in the first activity to close to the Exemplary level in the last one (from 2.7 to 3.6, 2.8 to 3.8 and 2.6 to 3.9, respectively).

The same comparison between activities showed a change in Solution of the problem, from 1.3 (barely above Insufficient) to 2.1 (Basic), while Interpretation of mathematical results in a context went from 2.1 to 2.4, that is, barely above Basic, making it the category with the least change in the group-reported work.

Comparison of individual assessments

Group activities do not allow for clearly identifying each individual's specific contributions, or the degree to which they were made. The results of the final assessment aim to show the individual impact on each part of the applied problem-solving process. In Information and data describing the problem, the average score went from 3.1 to 3.9, that is, from level Sufficient to nearly Exemplary; What needs to be done to solve the problem, from 2.4 to 3.5, that is, above Sufficient; Strategies for solving the problem, from 2.1 to 3.3, or from Basic to a bit more than Sufficient; Solution of the problem went from 1.4 to 2.8, that is, from slightly above Absent to nearly Sufficient; and the Interpretation of mathematical results in a context, rose from 1.9 to 2.3, just above a Basic level. As can be seen, by the end of the academic term, all rubric categories showed improvement compared to the measurements taken at the beginning of the course. Notably, students generally improved in their ability to clearly identify Information and data describing the problem, such as the scale of measurement of the variables, time frame, among others, that is, the specific context of application in contrast to the mathematical model. The improvement reported in this category represents 26% relative to the initial performance found.

For their part, What needs to be done and Strategies for solving the problem, have a direct bearing on the course's disciplinary content. Students showed good progress in this regard, with an improvement of approximately 46% in the first case and 57% in the second, relative to the beginning of the academic term.

It is worth noting that the category that showed the greatest change was Solution of the problem, with 100% improvement according to the results gathered. Although this does not represent an excellent final result, the evidence suggests it can continue to improve. This aspect is not directly related to the course topics, but rather to correctly substituting values, the precise execution of manual operations, and the proper technical handling of tools such as a scientific calculator or specialized software.

On the other hand, as confirmed in the analysis of the team assessments, Interpretation of results in a context is the part of the process that requires the most attention, as it showed the most moderate variation according to the individual results, with barely a 21% improvement relative to the beginning of the course.

At the end of the academic term, students were asked whether they felt their ability to solve applied problems had improved. Opinions included Fernando's: "Yes, it improved a lot compared to my knowledge before starting this course, and I think I learned to properly interpret mathematical problems from an economic standpoint," and Judith's: "Yes, because besides being useful for my future career, I now notice things that are related." Most comments centered on a lack of full mastery, such as Jaqueline's: "Yes it improved, but not completely," Celia's: "It helped me understand them a bit more, although I feel I still need more practice and guidance," or Ian's: "That's what has been hardest for me — the applications to economics. Even when it's something very simple that I already master, once it's put in economic terms it confuses me a lot." Given the context of the students' professional field, the authors of this study consider it desirable for them to achieve better performance in this particular area; in line with the view of Cevibkas et al. (2022) and based on the study's results, a single academic term appears to be insufficient to achieve this.

The assessment rubric

As a formative assessment mechanism, the proposed instrument made it possible to explore students' problem-solving process and understand their reasoning process (Pascual Vigil et al., 2020; Valverde Berrocoso and Ciudad Gómez, 2014). Despite the advantages of this assessment method, the systematic literature review by Cevibkas et al. (2022), conducted on 75 academic articles, showed that analytical rubrics were used in only 3% of the reviewed materials to verify students' competence related to modeling and solving contextualized problems.

In this study, 93% of the group felt that having the rubric was useful for carrying out the team integrative activities, as can be seen in comments such as Alessandra's: "(It was) very useful for doing everything possible to (achieve) what was required," who regarded the instrument as a guide; Andrés's: "I like that even without the exact result, the process you used to get there and the understanding we had of the topic count for a lot," or David's: "It's very good because it takes into account that in mathematics it's not just about the result, but also about identifying the information, knowing what to do with it, performing the necessary calculations, arriving at the result, and knowing what that result means," which reflects a broader consideration of what it meant to have the instrument during the problem-solving process.

For its part, as the literature suggests, it is essential that grading and feedback be provided in a timely manner (Gómez Mena, 2024; Martínez González, 2020), since postponing this part of the process dilutes its potential benefits. Some comments about the feedback sessions reinforce this idea, such as Eunice's: "I liked them and they helped me because that way I could clear up doubts that came up during the exam," or Diana's: "Very good because they help us notice our mistakes so we can improve in the next activity." Some students took their reflection beyond a personal implication, as exemplified by José's comment, who mentioned that these sessions "are very important so that the professor stays up to date on the processes and dynamics that are actually working," as noted by Bancayán Oré (2013).

On the other hand, incorporating a variety of teaching strategies laid the groundwork for developing a favorable working environment for building problem-solving skills, as suggested by the literature review of Cevibkas et al. (2022), since around 93% of students felt they had received high or very high support from the professor in following up on course activities, while approximately 83% considered teamwork useful or very useful for carrying out the integrative activities.

Finally, grading with an analytical rubric can offer advantages in terms of the depth and quality of the assessment, but it is neither automatic nor quick to carry out compared to other forms or instruments. In general, it requires carefully reviewing students' exercises or work to verify the level of achievement in each category. Likewise, the professor must thoroughly understand the rubric and keep in mind the range of possibilities that may arise in order to determine that a criterion has been met. This aspect is also important for maintaining consistency and objectivity in the assessment.

Conclusions

The application of mathematical concepts to solving contextualized problems at the higher-education level remains a subject of interest in mathematics education. There is also considerable evidence confirming the difficulty students face in carrying out this process. It is imperative to use mechanisms that help improve it, since these are competencies expected of graduates from different fields of knowledge.

An instrument that can help achieve this is the analytical assessment rubric, as it not only allows teachers to study how students carry out this process, but can also serve as a guide so that students first have clarity about what is expected of them in assessments, know how to achieve it, and ultimately make decisions about the level of achievement they wish to attain.

The rubric is not easy to use if one is not familiar with the instrument. It is advisable to work with it throughout the sessions so that students come to understand in practice what each rubric category represents, and reflect on how each problem should be approached in order to achieve the desired performance level.

Overall, despite differences among assessments — whether due to problem type, topic, or difficulty level — the results presented showed improvement among students in each of the different stages of the applied problem-solving process.

Even when it is the same course, it must be noted that group behavior tends to vary depending on various circumstances: the class schedule (it is not the same at 7 a.m., 1 p.m., or 8 p.m.); whether students are taking the course for the first time or retaking it; whether or not they work; the total number of courses taken during the term; among others. These particularities can affect the time and attention devoted to the course and, ultimately, students' performance, which is why applying the rubric to assess applied problem solving may yield different results depending on the context of application.

In addition, the results of assessment with the rubric may vary when dealing with more complex academic problems that could arise at more advanced stages of their professional training, or with those present in the workplace.

As future work, it is proposed to investigate whether there is a relationship between attendance at in-person sessions and students' improvement in the problem-solving process; whether similar results could be expected if the integrative activities were carried out individually rather than in teams; to conduct a comparative analysis of solving academic problems versus real problems from the administrative context; how course design affects the development of problem-solving skills; to follow up with students who had worked with the rubric in the prerequisite course; and how Generative Artificial Intelligence could be used to improve the problem-solving process.

Finally, it is emphasized that the proposed instrument can be used to assess individual or group work in mathematics courses across different professional fields or academic levels, as it focuses on the different stages of the problem-solving process.
